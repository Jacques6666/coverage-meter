# Chapter Thirteen

## Anni

Six hundred and twelve steps.

I don't remember any of them. I remember the bell, so loud in the stairwell that the stone shook with it, and the lamp swinging somewhere above me, and Severin's voice behind me saying my name once, sharp, and then not saying it again. I remember that at Rook's landing he caught my arm and pulled me to a stop, and I nearly hit him.

"Go to the Hall," he said. "Stay there. Whatever happens, you were there."

"I wasn't there. I was with you."

"Tamsin—"

"*Where are you going?*"

But I knew where he was going. I could see it in his face. He let go of my arm and went past me down the stairs, three at a time, the lamp jerking and flaring, and at the next landing he didn't go down toward the court. He went through a narrow door I'd never noticed in the wall of the stairwell, and it closed behind him, and the light went with him, and I was standing alone in the dark with the bell tolling over my head.

He was going to the sluice.

I went down the rest of the way by feel, one hand on the curved wall, counting, because counting is what I do.

---

The iron door of the Tithe Hall was standing open.

Not unlocked. Open. Swung all the way back against the stone, with Rook's great ring of keys still hanging from the lock. Inside, the lamps were lit, every one, and the Hall was full of people who'd been asleep a moment ago: tithes sitting up in bed with their blankets clutched to their chests, eyes huge, and Steward Rook in the middle of the aisle in her grey wrapper with her hair down her back, turning in a slow circle as if she couldn't remember where she'd put something.

"Count off," she was saying. "Count off. Count *off*."

Nobody was counting off. Everybody was looking at the bed by the far wall.

Pell was on the floor beside it. He was sitting with his back against the iron frame and his huge legs out in front of him like a child's, and he was holding Anni Rusk's blanket in both hands. Just the blanket. Her bed was empty.

"I was *awake*," he was saying, to nobody. His big gentle face was wet. "I was. I was sitting right here. I said I'd sit up with her, after the library, I said I'd sit up every night, and I was awake, I *was*, I was counting the drips from the ceiling, I'd got to two hundred and something—" His voice cracked. "And then I wasn't."

Bry was standing over him in her shift with her fists clenched. When she saw me in the doorway, something went across her face so fast I couldn't name it, and then she crossed the Hall in four strides and grabbed me by both arms.

"Where were you?"

"The tower. I was—the bell went, I came—"

"You weren't *here*." She shook me, once, hard. "You weren't here. Good. *Good.*" And then, so quietly only I could hear: "Tam. Everybody went to sleep."

"What?"

"*Everybody.* All at once. I was awake, I was lying there thinking about Crane's face, and then I wasn't, and it was like being hit on the head. Like a blanket coming down." She swallowed. "I woke up with my mouth tasting of pennies. And the door was open. And she was gone."

I looked around the Hall. At the tithes rubbing their faces, dazed. At Rook, still turning. At the iron door, standing wide, with the keys in the lock.

"Nobody touched my keys," Rook said suddenly, loud, to the whole room, as if someone had accused her. "Nobody touched them. They were on my belt. They were *on my belt*."

A stilling. A big one. Big enough to drop a whole Hall full of people where they lay, Steward and all, for as long as it took to walk in and take a girl out of her bed and walk out again. I did the sum in my head without wanting to, the way I'd done a hundred sums for Severin that week at the velvet table. A room this size. Twelve people. Long enough to unlock a door.

A year. Maybe two.

Nobody spends two years on a stilling. Nobody has two years to spend on a stilling. Unless they've been saving.

I turned and looked at Bry. She was looking at her own arm. At her count: fifty-one years, where there'd been fifty-eight on Tithe Day.

"He's been taking years off me," she said, very slowly, as if each word weighed a stone. "Every night. *Practicing*, he said. And keeping them."

Her face went grey.

"For *this*," said Bry.

---

They found her at first light, on the sluice grate, where they'd found Joss.

I didn't see it. They didn't bring us out to the court this time to look. I think someone had decided that once was a lesson and twice was a riot. But it went round the Hall in whispers before breakfast, the way everything did, passed from bed to bed by tithes who'd heard it from servants who'd heard it from wardens.

Caught on the bars like a leaf. Her arm gone blank to the wrist. A purse of forty crowns in her pocket, soaked black. And the Ledger, when Clerk Penn opened it at dawn, already had a sale written down: *Annika Rusk. Sixty-three years, five months. Sold in full. Broker Seven.*

And one more thing. The thing that made the whispers stop and start again in a different voice.

The wardens who found her weren't the first ones there.

"The Thane heir," said a Vey tithe named Corrie, who'd heard it from a kitchen girl who'd heard it from the warden himself. "In the water. Up to his waist, they said, holding on to her. Trying to get her off the grate. *Holding* her." She looked at me sideways, and so did everyone else. "Covered in it, they said. River muck. He wouldn't let go of her till they made him."

Nobody said what they were all thinking. They didn't have to.

*His last tithe went home in a box.*

I sat on the end of my bed with my hands in my lap and my sleeve rolled down over my ink, and I thought of the narrow door in the stairwell, and the lamp going through it, and his face when the river groaned under the island. *I always get there too late.*

He'd got there too late. And they'd found him holding her.

---

Captain Marr questioned us one at a time, in Rook's office.

It was a small square room at the top of the Hall stairs with one window onto the court and a desk too big for it, and it smelled of ink and lamp oil and Rook's tea. Marr sat behind the desk with his long grey coat still on and a notebook open in front of him. Clerk Penn stood at his shoulder with the Ledger book clutched to his chest like a shield.

And in a chair by the window, with his legs crossed and his golden head tipped back against the wall, sat Lysander Crane.

"Representative of the Houses," said Clerk Penn, when he saw me look. "The Chancellor's wish. A wright must be present when tithes are questioned."

"Of course," said Lysander, and smiled at me. "Someone has to look after their interests."

I sat down in the chair across from Marr. My knees wouldn't have held me much longer anyway.

Marr looked at me for a while. He looked worse than he had the morning they'd found Joss: grey-skinned, red-eyed, the creases in his face cut deeper. There was a fresh ink stain on his cuff.

"Fenn," he said.

"Captain."

"The counter."

"Yes."

"You told me last time that Mallory didn't sell." He turned a page in his notebook. "Do you want to tell me anything about Rusk?"

I looked at Clerk Penn, with his book. I looked at Lysander, lounging by the window in the grey light like a cat on a sill, watching me with polite interest.

"Same as Joss," I said. "I'd guess. If I saw her."

"You won't." Marr made a note. "Where were you last night, Fenn? From the ninth bell."

"In the tower. My wright sent for me."

"Until?"

"Until the alarm."

"The Steward's book says you left the Hall at the ninth bell and did not return until after the alarm had sounded. Near two in the morning." He didn't look up. "That's five hours."

"Yes."

"Doing what?"

"Lessons," said Lysander Crane, from the window.

He said it softly, almost kindly, and he let it hang in the air for a moment before he went on. "That's what Rook wrote down, isn't it? *Lessons*. Every night this week." He tilted his head against the wall. "My cousin's very devoted to his tithe's education."

Clerk Penn went faintly pink.

"I asked the girl," said Marr, without turning around.

"Lessons," I said. "The Table of Hours. He's teaching me the costs."

"Why would a wright teach his tithe the Table of Hours?"

Because he needs me to fake my count for his grandmother. Because I sweated through my ink on the Dial. Because the thing he spends his life on is not me.

"I asked him to," I said. "I wanted to know what it was costing me."

Marr looked up at me, finally. His washed-out blue eyes stayed on my face for a long moment, and I made it into a wall the way I'd learned to, and he looked at the wall, and I couldn't tell whether he saw through it or not.

"Were you with him," he said slowly, "at the moment the sluice opened?"

My mouth was dry. "Yes."

"You're sure."

"We both heard it. In the tower. The river makes a sound." I swallowed. "He was standing in the doorway with a lamp. He'd just said it was late. And it groaned, under the island, and the clocks shook, and he said *the sluice*. And then the bell went."

"And then?"

"And then he went down. To see."

"To see," said Lysander, very gently.

Marr didn't look at him. "You were with him all evening. From the ninth bell to the sluice opening. He never left the room."

"No."

"All night."

"Until the bell. Yes."

There was a short silence. Then Lysander laughed.

It was a soft laugh, warm, delighted, the laugh of someone who's just been told the best piece of gossip of the season. He uncrossed his legs and leaned forward with his elbows on his knees and looked at me with his beautiful golden face full of sympathy.

"All night," he said. "In his rooms. Learning the Table of Hours. Five hours of *arithmetic*." He shook his head slowly. "Captain, I do think you should write that down exactly as she said it. The Gilt is going to adore it."

Clerk Penn made a sound like a kettle beginning to boil.

The door opened.

I didn't need to turn around. The thread at my wrist had gone hot before the handle moved, and his heartbeat came into the room ahead of him, fast and uneven, like someone who'd run a long way and not stopped since.

Severin stood in the doorway.

He'd changed his clothes, but he hadn't done it well. His shirt was buttoned wrong at the throat. His hair was still damp and there was a long streak of grey river silt drying on the side of his neck that he'd missed, and his gloves were on, but the right one was torn across the palm where the old scar was. He looked like somebody who'd been in the cold water for a long time.

He looked at Marr. Then at Penn. Then at Lysander, by the window, still smiling. Then, last, at me.

"She was with me," said Severin.

His voice was hoarse. He didn't raise it.

"From the ninth bell until the alarm. In my rooms. All night." He turned his eyes back to Marr. "If you need to write that down, Captain, write it down. If you need me to sign it, I'll sign it."

Nobody said anything.

"Well," said Lysander, into the silence, softly, "there it is."

---

Marr let me go after that. He let all of us go, eventually, one by one, with his long jaw set and his notebook full and nothing to show for any of it. By noon, Clerk Penn had read the Ledger's finding to the whole college in the Refectory: Annika Rusk, tithe to House Lisle, had sold her count in full to a licensed broker and subsequently drowned. The House would send the body home with a coat and a blessing. The Ledger considered the matter closed.

Nobody mentioned a ham this time.

Nobody mentioned the Thane heir, either, up to his waist in the river with his arms around a dead girl. Not out loud. Not in the Refectory.

Everywhere else, they mentioned nothing else.

I heard it in the corridors. I heard it on the stairs. I heard it from two Marchbank wrights at the next table at supper, who didn't bother to lower their voices: *all night, she said, all night in his rooms, and he came in and said it himself, in front of Marr, in front of Crane, can you imagine*. I heard Lysander's word, *arithmetic*, passed from mouth to mouth with a snigger behind it, until by evening it didn't mean arithmetic anymore.

By the next morning, it was in the Gilt.

Hollis found me in the library at study hour and sat down across from me at our dusty table at the back and didn't say anything for a while. He looked like he'd been crying again. He'd liked Anni. Everyone had liked Anni.

"They're saying—" he started.

"I know what they're saying."

"Lysander's telling everyone. He's telling it very well. He's had *practice*." Hollis turned a book over in his hands without looking at it. "It's mostly about Severin. That he's—that the Thane heir has taken his Wick tithe to bed. That he *paid thirty* for it. That's the part they love." He winced. "And the other thing. That he was found in the sluice with Anni. That he couldn't account for the hour before. Except that his tithe says he was with her. And his tithe would say anything, wouldn't she? If he were—" He stopped.

"If he were what?"

"If he were her lover," said Hollis miserably. "That's how they put it. I'm sorry. I'm only telling you because you'd rather hear it from me than from a Crane."

I looked down at the table.

"Is it true?" said Hollis quietly.

"No."

He breathed out, slowly.

"Then why?" he said. "Why'd he say *all night*? He could have said till the sluice. That's all Marr asked. He made it worse. He made it so much worse, on purpose, in front of Lysander—"

"Because Marr asked *me* first," I said. "And I'd already said it."

Hollis was quiet.

"He walked in," I said, "and he heard what I'd said, and he could have said I was lying. He could have said I came at the ninth bell and left at the eleventh and he didn't know where I went after. It would have been easy. It would've been *believed*. I'm a Wick girl. He's the Thane heir." I swallowed. "And then it'd have been me with no account for the hour before the sluice. Me with the longest count in the Hall that anybody knew of after Anni's. Me who was out of bounds. Me who stood up in the Tithe Court and told Captain Marr exactly how a tithe gets drained."

Hollis stared at me.

"He didn't say it to save himself," I said. "He said it to save me. And everybody in the Gilt is going to think the worst thing they can think about both of us, for the rest of the term, and he knew that, and he said it anyway."

---

That night, in the Hall, Pell sat on the floor beside Anni's empty bed, as he'd sat the night before, and wouldn't be moved. Steward Rook let him. She brought him a blanket herself and put it over his shoulders without a word, and locked the iron door, and this time she pulled a chair up against the inside of it and sat in it, with her keys in her lap, and her lamp lit, and her good eye open.

Joss's boots were still under his bed. Anni's blanket was in Pell's arms. I lay in the dark and looked at both.

From the next bed, Bry said quietly: "Tam."

"Mm."

"Is it true?"

I knew what she meant. Everybody meant the same thing.

"You're in his bed?" said Bry.

I lay there and looked at the vaulted ceiling with the green dark seeping through the slots of the windows, and the faint gold glow of eleven counts under eleven blankets, and the place where twelve should have been. And I thought about a bare arm on black velvet, nine lines of gold and all that grey. Two hands on two wrists, each taking the other's pulse. *I'm not going to guess at you, Tamsin.*

"No," I said. "I wish the reason were that simple."
