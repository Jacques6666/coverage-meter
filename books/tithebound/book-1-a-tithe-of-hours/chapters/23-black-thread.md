# Chapter Twenty-Three

## Black Thread

The door was heavier than it looked. I had to put my shoulder to it, with the black water sucking at my waist and my numb feet slipping on the stone, and push, and push, until the crack was wide enough to get through sideways. The iron scraped. The clock faces worked into its surface slid past my cheek, cold, every hand on eleven.

On the other side, there were steps going up out of the water.

I climbed them on my hands and knees. I couldn't feel my legs. I couldn't feel anything below the waist except a deep, grinding ache in my bones, and when I reached the top step and got to my feet, they went out from under me, and I sat down hard on wet stone and stayed there for a moment, shaking, with water streaming off me in sheets.

I was in a corridor. Dry, or nearly. Rough black rock, like the wall around the door, with the marks of old tools in it. A torch burned in an iron bracket a little way along, and that was the yellow light I'd seen through the crack: smoky, guttering, throwing my shadow up the wall huge and wavering. The corridor went on past it, deeper, into the dark.

And the beat came up through the floor. So close now that I didn't feel it in my feet anymore. I felt it in my chest. In the hollow of my throat. In the thread at my wrist, which had gone warm as a hand and was pulling me on along the corridor, past the torch, toward the dark.

I got up.

The corridor turned once, and then again, and opened into a room.

---

It was small and square and very old, the walls the same tool-marked black rock, the ceiling low enough that I could have touched it with my hand. Iron rings were set into the walls at the height of a person's wrists, a dozen of them, rusted. There was a second torch in a bracket by a gap in the far wall, where another passage went on, deeper still. There was a stone bench along one side.

And on the floor, under one of the rings, there was Bry.

She was sitting with her back against the wall and her legs stretched out in front of her and her head fallen forward onto her chest, the way Pell had sat beside Anni's empty bed. Her wrists were above her head, held there by iron cuffs on a short chain run through the ring. Her plain grey dress was soaked to the waist. Her hair was down. In the torchlight, her face was the color of candle wax.

Her left arm, held up in the cuff, was dark.

Almost all of it. Thirty-one years, she'd had, when she climbed the tower behind Rook's lamp at the tenth bell. Now the ladder of lines up her forearm was grey from the elbow down, every rung of it, gone out. At the wrist, just above the cuff, there was one notch still glowing. One. And the faint shimmer at the tip of it, the days, so dim I had to lean close to see it at all.

Two weeks. Maybe less.

"Bry."

I was on my knees beside her. I don't remember crossing the room. I had my hands on her face. It was cold, cold as the water, and for a terrible moment I thought, *too late, I always get there too late*, in a voice that wasn't mine. Then her eyelids moved.

"Burn me," whispered Bry, without opening them. "Took you long enough."

I made a sound I'd never made before in my life.

"Don't," she said. "Don't cry on me. I'm wet enough." Her eyes opened, a slit. They found my face, and then my arm, held up and blazing gold in the torchlight, and something moved in them. "Tam. Your *arm*."

"I know."

"It's all lit up. All of it. Even the top, under the—" Her voice was a thread. "Tam, you've got *seventy*—"

"I know, Bry. Hold still."

The cuffs weren't locked. That was the first piece of luck, and I've wondered since whether it was luck at all. There was just a pin through each hasp, an iron pin on a little chain, the kind you'd use to close a gate. I pulled them both out and caught her as her arms came down, and she fell against me like a sack of wet sand, and I held on.

Then I saw the thread.

It was tied around her left wrist, below the cuff. Black. Not silver like a Ledger binder's thread, not the line of light that ran between Severin's wrist and mine. Black, dull, fibrous, like waxed cord, the kind that came on bales in the back of Gannet's shop. Sewn in under the skin, with a raised red welt all around it. And tied off at the inside of the wrist with a knot I knew.

Small. Neat. Flat. A double loop with the tail tucked under.

It had been cut. A few inches from her wrist, the other end trailed loose on the stone floor beside her.

And beside the loose end, lying on the stone where somebody had dropped it, or left it, was a broker's needle. Long and black with use. As long as my hand.

---

"Bry," I said. "How long have you got?"

She knew what I meant. Everyone in the Wick knows how to read their own count, even in the dark. She looked down at her arm, slowly, as if it were very far away.

"Two weeks," she said. "Bit less." Her mouth twisted. "He said he'd leave me a little. So I'd last till somebody came." She shivered, hard, against me. "He said somebody would come."

I didn't think about what that meant. I couldn't afford to. I picked up the needle.

"Tam." Her eyes had gone to it. "What are you doing?"

"I sat behind Gannet's counter seven years," I said. "I watched the brokers on the Row thread people a thousand times. Black thread, broker's twist, a quarter of an hour if you don't mind the scars." I picked up the loose end of the cut thread from the floor, and held it up in the torchlight. "I know how."

"It kills one in three."

"Then we'll be the other two."

"*Tam.* You can't—they'll *see*. Whatever you are, they'll see it. My count goes up, Rook writes it down, the whole island knows. That's what he—" She stopped. She swallowed. "He *wants* you to."

"I know."

"Then *don't*."

"Bry." I put my free hand on her face again, on her cold cheek, and made her look at me. "You've got two weeks. And I've got seventy-one years and four months, and every one of them comes home by breakfast. Every single one. It always has." My voice was steady. I was proud of it. "Let me."

She looked at me for a long moment in the smoky light. Then her face crumpled, all of it at once, the way I'd never seen it crumple, not when she broke her own hand in the ring at fifteen, not when her father went grey, not the morning after the Greycoats came to our door.

"You *idiot*," she said. "You absolute idiot. Since you were *nine*?"

"Since I was nine."

"And you never *told* me."

"I'm telling you now."

---

It hurt more than the threading in the Tithe Court. Much more. A Ledger binder's needle is fine as a hair and silver and goes in clean; a broker's needle is thick and black and has been in a thousand arms before yours, and it doesn't go in so much as push. I did it myself, with my left wrist held out in the torchlight and my right hand shaking so badly I had to stop twice. Under the skin. Around. Out. The black thread following, cold and then burning, cold and then burning. I bit through my lip. I didn't stop.

Then the knot.

The broker's twist is ugly and quick: three wraps and a pinch, so the knot sits under the skin like a grain of rice. I'd seen it done ten thousand times. I'd never done it, never once, and my fingers were numb with the cistern water and slick with my own blood. The first time, it slipped. The second time, it held.

The thread sank into my wrist and vanished, leaving a raw red line beside the silver one.

And I felt her.

Not like Severin. Severin was a drum in another room, slow and even, a heartbeat trained into steadiness. Bry was a bird. A small, fast, fluttering thing, beating against the inside of my wrist like something trapped in a box, too fast and too light, and fading.

I took hold of it the way I'd taken hold of the silver thread in the ballroom. With the part of me that knows my own count. And I pushed.

It went.

A year. I felt it leave me, that long hot pour and then the drop under the breastbone like missing a stair, and across the thread I felt it arrive in her. She gasped. Her whole body jerked against me. I looked down at her arm, and above the one glowing notch at her wrist, a line had lit. Gold. Faint, and then brighter, the way an ember brightens when you breathe on it.

Another. Another. Five. Ten.

Her arm was lighting up rung by rung, from the wrist toward the elbow, the gold climbing it like water filling a glass. And mine was going dark. I could see it. Not under the ink at the top; lower, where the gold was bare. The sixtieth line went out. The fifty-ninth. The fifty-eighth. Brown at the ends, then grey. The way a count goes out when it's sold. The way I'd watched it go out on Pruett's counter.

It hurt. It always hurts. Every year was a door closing on a room I'd never get to walk into. I didn't care.

Fifteen.

Bry was crying now, without any noise, her face pressed into my shoulder.

Twenty.

I stopped.

I didn't want to. I could have kept going. I could have given her forty, fifty, all of it, and it would all have come home by breakfast. But the counter in my head, the one who'd stood behind Gannet's desk for seven years, was saying very quietly: *Twenty is a lot to explain. Fifty is a miracle. Don't give them a miracle.*

I let go of the thread.

Bry lifted her head off my shoulder and held up her arm in the torchlight and looked at it. Twenty years. Twenty bright gold lines, from the wrist halfway to the elbow, where an hour ago there'd been one notch and a shimmer.

"Tam," she whispered.

I looked down at my own arm. Below the ink, a long grey stretch where ten bright lines had been. Under the ink, ten more gone dark that only I knew about.

And as I watched, the bottom one began to warm.

---

"*Tam!*"

The shout came from the corridor behind me, and bounced off the stone, and came back three times. Then footsteps, running, splashing, slipping on wet rock. And Hollis came around the corner into the torchlight.

He was soaked to the chest. His plum dressing gown was black with cistern water and hanging off him like weed, and his bare feet were bleeding, and his long freckled face was grey with cold and white around the mouth, and he was carrying a lamp that had gone out. He stopped in the entrance to the little square room and stood there, heaving for breath, and stared at us.

At Bry, on the floor, with twenty years blazing up her arm.

At me, kneeling beside her, with a black thread on my wrist and blood on my mouth and my left arm held out in front of me.

"Severin's not in the tower," he said, between breaths. "The door wouldn't open for me. I knocked till my hand hurt. He's *gone*, Tam, I don't know where, so I came after you, I followed the—" He stopped. He was looking at my arm.

The torch guttered and flared. In its light, the long grey stretch in my count, the dead lines between the bright ones near the wrist and the ink near the elbow, was changing.

The lowest one had lit. Faint. Then brighter.

Then the next.

Slowly. Not like a cup. Like a bucket. A warmth coming up from below, a heaviness returning, rung after rung of gold filling back in from the bottom, the way it always did, the way it had every morning of my life since I was nine years old, as though somebody somewhere were patiently pouring them back in.

Hollis set down the dead lamp on the floor very carefully, the way Severin set things down. He came across the room and went down on his knees beside me on the wet stone in his ruined dressing gown, and he didn't touch me. He just looked. At my arm, filling up in front of him, line by line, in the torchlight.

His face did something I'd never seen a face do. All of it at once. Wonder and terror and something like grief.

"Tam," whispered Hollis Penhallow. "Your count."

He looked up at me.

"It's going back *up*."
