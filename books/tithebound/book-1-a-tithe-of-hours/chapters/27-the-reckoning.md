# Chapter Twenty-Seven

## The Reckoning

The message came at dawn, folded small, in Vadra's slanting hand. Pell brought it down from the kitchens with the breakfast trays and slipped it under my pillow without a word.

*The bird has flown. Safe in the nest at the end of the Row, with the man from over the pie shop. He asked me to tell you he's wound the watch.*

I read it four times. Then I put it inside my shirt, against my ribs, next to Kit's first map, and sat on the end of my bed in the grey river light and let myself breathe for the first time in three days.

All down the Hall, the others were getting ready. Nobody talked. They washed their faces at the basin by the brazier and combed their hair and put on the clean grey that Rook had laid out on every bed the night before, and they rolled their left sleeves up to the elbow, because that's how it's done at the Reckoning. Every tithe on the Dial with their count bare to the sky.

I rolled mine up too.

No ink. Not a coat. Seventy-one years and four months, gold from the wrist almost to the crease of the elbow, so bright in the dim Hall that the tithes in the beds on either side of me turned to look, and kept looking. Corrie from Vey. The thin boy from the Sump who'd asked Rook what was below the Hall. A Marchbank girl whose name I'd never learned. They stared at my arm, and then at my face, and then at each other, and didn't say anything.

Bry came and sat down beside me. She had her own sleeve rolled. Twenty years, one month.

"Well," she said. "That's one way to get noticed."

"I'm done hiding, Bry."

"I know. I was there. You shouted it at a magister." She bumped her shoulder against mine, hard, the way she'd said hello on the Hours Bridge on Tithe Day. "For what it's worth, I'm done too. If Crane draws on me today, I'm going to stand there and take it and smile at him." A pause. "And then I'm going to break his nose. In front of the Long-Lived. I've been saving it."

Steward Rook came down the aisle.

She stopped in front of my bed. She looked at my arm for a long time with her good eye, while the milky one roved over my face. Down the row, everybody had gone quiet again.

"Your count's honest today, Fenn," said Steward Rook.

In the Wick, that means *you can trust her*. It means *she's not inked*. I don't know which one she meant. I think she meant both.

"Yes, Steward," I said.

She nodded once. Then she reached into her pocket and took out a ribbon, a plain grey one, and held it out to me.

"For your hair," she said gruffly. "You look like a hedge."

---

The Dial was full.

It had been full for the duel with Lysander, but this was different. Every seat on every tier of the great stone bowl was taken, and people were standing along the top, packed shoulder to shoulder against the sky: servants, binders, magisters, wardens off duty. Snow had fallen in the night and lay in the corners of the steps and along the rim of the bowl in a thin white line, and the breath of all those people went up in a single cloud into the cold blue morning.

And the gallery was full of the Long-Lived.

They'd come over from the Gilt at dawn, in their carriages, in their furs. The striped awning had been taken down and replaced by one of black silk, and under it, on cushioned benches, they sat in rows like a choir, still and silent and watching, with their jeweled cuffs on their arms and their faces smooth as eggs. I recognized some of them. The man with the ear trumpet. The woman who'd said *Wick, eighteen years old, very light, very fresh* by the fountain at Thane House.

At the north side of the Dial, at the twelve, a dais had been built out over the rim of the marble. On it stood a tall chair of black wood carved with clock faces, and in the chair sat Lady Aurelia Thane.

She was in black lace. She was always in black lace. Through her sleeves, her count blazed from wrist to shoulder. Beside her stood Clerk Penn with the great Ledger book open in his arms, and a binder in grey with a silver needle on a velvet cushion, and a small table with a silver box on it.

Around the rim of the Dial, every twenty paces, stood a Greycoat. Captain Marr had put them there, after the Long Night, after the masked men with their needles. He stood himself at the six, opposite the dais, in his worn grey coat with his hands behind his back and his long jaw set. When I walked out onto the marble with the other tithes, his washed-out eyes found me, and went to my arm, and stayed there.

At the edge of the magisters' tier, near the bottom, Magister Vadra sat very straight in her green gown with her hands folded in her lap. She didn't look at me. She didn't have to.

Hollis stood with the first-years at the rim, in his mended plum. Pell loomed behind him in grey, enormous. Under Pell's jacket, against his chest, was a shape the size of a dinner plate, wrapped in a blanket.

And Severin stood at the rim on the north side, near the dais, alone, in black, with his gloved hands behind his back and his silver cuff gleaming.

I walked across the marble to him. Every step of the way I could feel the whole Dial watching my arm.

He held out his hand as I reached him. Not the formal way, palm up, for a wrist. Just his hand. Gloved. Open.

I put mine in it.

Up on the dais, I saw Lady Aurelia Thane lift a violet out of the silver box, and pause with it halfway to her lips.

---

The Reckoning goes by House, and by year. First-years first, so the Long-Lived can laugh at the small workings and feel generous. Final-years after. Thane last, because Thane is always last.

Each wright walked out onto the Dial with their tithe. Each tithe stood in front of the dais while the binder in grey touched their count with the silver needle, a minute's draw, a true reading, and Lady Aurelia read the number aloud in her warm, carrying voice, and Clerk Penn wrote it in the Ledger. Then the wright cast, drawing from their tithe. Then the binder read the count again, and the Keeper read it out again, and the Long-Lived murmured, and applauded, or didn't.

A Vey first-year quickened a pear seed into a sapling as tall as a man, a pear tree in flower in the snow, and drew three weeks from her tithe to do it, and the Long-Lived clapped politely.

A Marchbank first-year unwound a shattered vase back into one piece and drew a month and a half, and the tithe swayed, and the Long-Lived clapped harder.

A Lisle final-year stilled a fountain someone had built at the six specially for the purpose, the water frozen mid-leap in a crystal tree, and held it for a count of sixty. Eight months, out of a girl from Saltmarket I'd never spoken to, who sat down on the marble afterward and didn't get up until two binders lifted her. The Long-Lived rose to their feet for that one.

I watched every one of them. I watched every tithe's count go down. I held Severin's hand, and he held mine, and neither of us said anything.

"Crane," said Clerk Penn. "First-year. Lysander Crane, with his tithe, Bryony Kettle."

Lysander walked out onto the Dial like a man walking onto a stage, golden, in a new coat the color of honey, with his ruby cuff on his arm and his sleeve pushed up an inch to show it. He bowed to the gallery. The Long-Lived applauded before he'd done anything at all. Behind him came Bry, with her chin up and her shoulders back and twenty years bright on her bare arm.

The binder touched her count with the needle.

"Bryony Kettle," read Lady Aurelia. "Twenty years, one month." She smiled at Bry, warmly. "My, my."

Along the rim, a murmur went through the tithes. Everyone on the island had heard what Bry's count had been in Frostmonth. Thirty-one. Everyone had heard about the night she didn't come back to the Hall.

Lysander turned and looked straight across the Dial at me. And smiled.

Then he took Bry's wrist and cast.

He'd chosen something showy, of course. He threw his free hand up and the air above the center of the Dial *tore*, a ragged ringing sound like a sheet being ripped in half, and gold light poured down out of the tear like a waterfall, splashing on the marble and running across it in streams and rising again as mist, glittering. It didn't do anything. It was only light. It was beautiful and useless and it went on and on, and the whole time the gold rope came out of Bry's arm into his hand.

She didn't fall. She didn't sway. She stood there beside him with her jaw locked and her eyes on his face and took it.

When he let go, the binder read her again.

"Eighteen years, two months," said Lady Aurelia. "Two years, near enough." She laughed, delighted, and clapped her small hands. "Oh, *well* done, Lysander. So *lavish*."

The Long-Lived applauded. Lysander bowed again, deeply.

And Bry turned her head and spat on the marble at his feet.

It was perfectly aimed. It landed right on the toe of his polished boot. And from the rim of the Dial where the tithes stood in their grey, somebody whistled, long and low, and somebody else laughed, and then they were all cheering. Every tithe on the rim. Stamping and whistling and cheering, the way you'd cheer in the Sump when a fighter you'd bet on gets up off the floor.

Lysander's smile didn't move. But I saw his hand close.

Up on the dais, Lady Aurelia ate a violet and watched, and didn't stop smiling.

---

"Penhallow," said Clerk Penn. "First-year. Hollis Penhallow, with his tithe, Pell Darrow."

Hollis walked out onto the Dial with his hands in his pockets.

Pell came behind him, slowly, holding his jacket closed over his chest with one huge hand. The binder touched his count. "Pell Darrow," read Lady Aurelia. "Forty-nine years. And a little." She tilted her head. "Unused, I see. All term."

"Yes, my lady," said Hollis cheerfully. "I've been saving him for something special."

"And what will you cast for us today, Mr. Penhallow?"

"Ah," said Hollis. "Yes. About that."

He took one hand out of his pocket. There was something in it, small and brown. He held it up so the gallery could see. A seed. Just a seed.

"I'm a Penhallow," said Hollis. "We're not very good at big things. We don't live long enough to practice." He looked at the seed in his palm. "And I find I'd rather not draw on Pell. Today or any day. So I'll be casting this one myself. If that's quite all right."

The Long-Lived murmured. Not approvingly.

Hollis didn't wait for anyone to tell him whether it was all right. He pushed back his tin cuff with his thumb, and closed his hand around the seed, and cast. Small and slow and careful, a thin line of gold drawn up out of his own short count and into his fist.

When he opened his hand, there was a flower in it.

A primrose. Pale yellow. Small and perfect and alive, in the middle of the snow, on a bed of green leaves in the palm of his hand.

He turned around and gave it to Pell.

Pell took it. He held it in his enormous cupped hands as if it might break, and looked down at it, and his gentle face crumpled.

"A week," said Hollis to the dais, brightly, pulling his cuff back down. "In case anybody's counting. From me. Not him."

There was a silence on the Dial.

Then, along the rim, a tithe stood up.

It was Corrie, from Vey. She stood up in her grey and started to clap. Then the thin boy from the Sump beside her. Then the Marchbank girl. Then all of them, every tithe on the rim of the Dial, on their feet in the snow, clapping. Not stamping and whistling, the way they'd cheered for Bry. Just clapping, slowly, steadily, for a boy with six years who'd spent a week of them on a flower.

And at the end of the tithes' row, in her grey dress, with her keys at her belt, Steward Agathe Rook stood up and clapped too.

Up under the black silk awning, the Long-Lived sat in perfect silence, and didn't clap at all.

Hollis bowed to the tithes. Not to the gallery. To the tithes. Then he walked back to the rim with his hands in his pockets, and passed me on the way, and didn't look at me, but I saw that his eyes were wet, and that he was grinning.

---

"Thane," said Clerk Penn.

His voice had gone very quiet. The whole Dial had gone very quiet.

"Final year. Severin Thane, with his tithe, Tamsin Fenn."

Severin's hand tightened on mine. Then he let it go, and we walked out together onto the white marble, across the brass numerals, toward the twelve and the dais and the tall black chair.

I could feel every eye in the Dial on my arm.

Six hundred people. More. The tithes on the rim, and the wrights in the tiers, and the servants packed along the top, and the Long-Lived in their furs under the black silk. Captain Marr at the six. Magister Vadra at the edge of her tier. Hollis and Bry and Pell, at the rim, very close together. And in the tall carved chair at the twelve, small and sweet in black lace with her count blazing through her sleeves and a silver clock-hand diadem in her honey-gold hair, the Keeper of the Ledger, watching me come.

We stopped in front of the dais.

The binder in grey came forward with his needle on its cushion. He reached for my wrist.

"That won't be necessary," said Lady Aurelia.

The binder stopped.

She rose from her chair. She came down the two steps of the dais onto the marble, slowly, with her black lace whispering over the stone, and stopped in front of me. So close I could smell the violets on her breath. She looked up at me, with her warm brown eyes and her dimples. And then she looked down at my arm.

She didn't take my wrist. She didn't need to. There was no ink to see through. Nothing to brush with her thumb. Just gold, bare and bright and blazing, from my wrist almost to my elbow, in the cold morning light, in front of the whole Gilt.

She looked at it for a long, long time.

Then she lifted her face to the gallery, and to the tiers, and to the whole of the Dial, and spoke in her warm, carrying voice, so that every soul in that stone bowl could hear.

"Tamsin Fenn," said Lady Aurelia Thane. "Tithe to House Thane. Seventy-one years, four months."

A pause.

"Exactly as on Tithe Day," she said. "Not a single day less."

The silence was so complete that I could hear the river.

Then the Dial broke. A sound came up out of it like the sound of the gallery on Tithe Day when Severin said *thirty*, but bigger, a rustle that became a murmur that became a roar. *Seventy-one?* The Steward's book said sixty. Everyone on the island had heard Rook read it out every Seventhday, a little less each week. *Seventy-one? Since Tithe Day? But he's been drawing on her all term, everyone's seen it, every class.* Clerk Penn was flipping frantically back through the Ledger. In the gallery, the man with the ear trumpet was on his feet, shouting something nobody could hear.

Lady Aurelia raised one small hand. The roar died.

"Curious," she said, softly, smiling at me. "Isn't it?"

Then she turned to her great-great-great-grandson, standing beside me in black on the white marble, and her smile grew warm, and fond, and terrible.

"Darling," she said. "Your great working. We're all waiting."

She looked at me.

"Draw on her," said Lady Aurelia Thane. "*All* of it."
