# Chapter One

## The Tithe Market

In the Wick, nobody asks how old you are. They ask how long you've got.

I painted my answer onto my arm by the light of a tallow stub, the morning they sold me.

The truth ran from my wrist almost to the crook of my elbow: seventy-one gold lines laid close as the rungs of a ladder, and across the wrist bone a little row of four notches for the months. Count-light, the old women on Tallow Lane called it, and they liked to say you could warm your hands at a long count on a winter morning. They said it about every long count. They had never seen one like mine.

Nobody in the Wick had a count like mine. That was the problem.

I dipped the brush. Tallow ink is the grey of old dishwater and stinks like a church that's caught fire, and a thimble of it costs more than a week of bread. I had scraped the pot down to the glaze. Thin coats. Feathered ends. Never in a tidy row, because real counts don't go out in tidy rows. They gutter. They brown at the ends first, then grey, then nothing. Mother Gannet had taught me that the year I turned twelve and started working her counter, and she'd made me practice on a pig's leg until I could fool her from across the shop.

One year, gone under the brush. Two. Three.

Downstairs, the chandler's dog was whining at the door. River fog pressed its wet face against our one window, and somewhere toward Broker's Row a bell was ringing the half hour for people who could afford to care what time it was.

Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten.

I sat back and looked at what I'd made. Sixty-one years and four months, honest as a magistrate. A Wick girl with sixty-one years is lucky, and lucky is fine. Lucky gets you bought.

Seventy-one gets you asked questions.

Here is the thing I had never told anyone, not Bry Kettle, not Mother Gannet, not even my brother: my count has read seventy-one years and four months since I was nine years old.

It doesn't tick down on my birthday. It doesn't go dark when I'm sick. Two winters ago, when Kit's lungs filled up and the physic in Saltmarket wanted silver we didn't have, I walked into a broker's shop under someone else's name and sold three years. I watched them drain out of my arm like water from a cracked cup. I went home and was sick in the yard. In the morning the three years were back. Every line.

I have sold fourteen years since then, a few at a time, in four districts, under six names. I've had every one of them back by breakfast.

I don't know what I am. I know exactly what they would do to me if they found out.

"You're doing it wrong," said a voice from the cot.

Kit was sitting up in his nest of blankets with his hair flattened on one side, watching me with the frank interest of a ten-year-old who was supposed to be asleep.

"I'm doing it perfectly."

"The fourth one's too dark." He pointed. "Real ones go brown at the ends first. Yours look like you dipped them."

I looked. He was right. I hated that he was right, and I hated more how he knew, which was that every morning of his life he had watched his own count go out.

He kept his sleeve pulled down over it, the way all the short kids in the Wick did, but I knew it the way I knew the crack across our ceiling. One gold line, and a few notches at the wrist like crumbs. One year. He'd been born with eleven. The midwife had looked at his arm, then at our mother, and said, *Well. Love him quickly.*

I feathered the end of the fourth mark with the dry tip of the brush. "Better?"

"Better." He hauled the blanket around his shoulders and shuffled across to sit on the floor beside me, which he knew I hated, because the floor was freezing and his lungs were made of wet paper. "Tam."

"No."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to say *don't go*. I'm going. So, no."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "Joss Mallory's sister came home with four years."

"Joss Mallory's sister was bought by the Marchbanks, and everybody knows the Marchbanks burn through tithes like lamp oil."

"Hen Pallister didn't come home at all."

"Hen Pallister fell off the Hours Bridge drunk."

"That's what they *said*."

"That's what *happened*."

Kit picked at a loose thread in the blanket. "You can't know that."

I set the brush down across the mouth of the pot. "Kit. Look at me."

He did. He had our mother's eyes, dark and too big for his face, and when he was frightened they got bigger, as though they were trying to see the whole bad thing coming at once.

"The Grace is ten years, minimum. That's the law. It's written in the Ledger and the Houses can't pay a day less. Ten years, to you, put in by a proper Ledger binder with a proper silver needle, the safe way." I made myself grin. "You'll be twenty-one. You'll be old. You'll be disgusting. You'll have a beard."

"I don't want a beard."

"Too bad. I'm buying you one."

That almost got him. The corner of his mouth went, and then it came back. "And if they draw all of yours?"

The ink was drying tight across my forearm. Under it, if I held my breath and looked at it sideways, I could still see the faint gold shine of the ten years I'd buried, like coins at the bottom of a well.

That was the other thing I had never told anyone. I was the one person in Lanthorn who could afford to be a tithe. A House could draw on me until my arm went dark to the wrist, and I'd be whole again by breakfast. The draining wasn't the danger. The danger was somebody watching while I came back.

"They won't," I said. "I'm going to be the most boring tithe they've ever bought. I'll be so healthy and dull they'll forget I'm there. And at the end of the term I'll come home and you can carry the water for once in your life, you little lord."

"You always say that."

"I always mean it."

He considered this, then reached under his pillow and pulled out a square of paper folded small and soft from handling. "I made you something. So you don't get lost."

I unfolded it on my knee. It was a map. Kit drew maps of places he'd never been and never would: the Gilt with its terraces stacked like wedding cake, the Candleworks with every chimney counted, the sea, which he had decided was green. This one was the Meridian. He'd drawn it the way I'd described it to him a hundred times, the way our mother had described it to me: the black island in the river, the long bridge with its twenty-four lamps, each one a tiny careful circle, and in the middle the great tower with its clock face. He'd put the single hand at eleven. He'd even drawn the little gulls.

Below the tower, down in the rock where nobody could see, he'd drawn a small circle, and in the circle a heart.

"What's that?"

"Where the clock keeps its heart." He said it as if it were obvious. "Mama said."

And I remembered. I hadn't thought of it in years. Our mother sitting on the end of my bed with the lamp turned low, tracing the shape of a clock in the air with one finger, the little finger on that hand bent crooked from some old break she never explained. *The clock has a heart, Tamsin, way down in the dark. And it's always hungry.*

I had been six or seven. It had frightened me so badly I'd slept with my head under the blanket for a week. I didn't know I'd ever told Kit.

"It's a good map," I said, and my voice came out wrong, so I folded it carefully and tucked it inside my shirt, against my ribs, where the paper was warm from his pillow. "Best one yet."

"You have to give it back. After."

"After," I agreed.

---

Our mother went into the Sallow the winter Kit was born. That's what the Greycoats wrote on the paper they brought to the door: *went into the river*, as though it were a place she'd decided to visit. Three weeks after the birth, in the middle of a February night, she kissed me on the forehead and told me to mind the baby and walked out into the fog, and she did not come back, and they never found her.

I was nine. I'd sat up the whole night with Kit squalling in my arms, waiting for the door. Sometime before dawn my wrist had begun to burn, hot and then cold, like a hand closing around it and letting go. I'd thought it was fear. By morning my count read seventy-one years and four months, and it has read that ever since.

I've never let myself think too hard about those two things being on the same night. In the Wick you learn which thoughts you can afford.

---

I didn't let Kit come to the bridge. He'd have argued, except he started coughing halfway through the argument, and that settled it. I tucked him back into the cot with the last of the coal in the grate and Mrs. Achterberg from across the landing promising to look in at noon. At the door I didn't turn around, because if I'd turned around I wouldn't have gone.

"Don't watch from the window," I said. "You'll get cold."

"I won't," he lied.

Out in Tallow Lane the fog was thick enough to chew. The Candleworks hadn't started its morning shift yet, so the only light came from the windows where other families were doing what we'd done: lighting a stub, rolling up a sleeve, saying the things you say. Most houses on the lane had sent somebody to a Market at some point. Most of the somebodies had come home. Some had come home with a Grace big enough to buy a mother another decade, or a father enough years to see a grandchild. Some had come home short, quiet and grey around the mouth, their counts gone dark to a handful of lines. You could tell them on the street. They walked like people carrying something heavy that nobody else could see.

I walked down to the river with my sleeve rolled to the elbow, because that's how it's done, and my sixty-one years and four months glowing in the fog.

The Hours Bridge starts at the bottom of Broker's Row and runs out across the Sallow for a quarter of a mile, and on Tithe Day it fills with candidates. There must have been forty of us that year, walking out in loose ones and twos between the lamps: Wick boys and girls between eighteen and twenty-one, the ones who were long enough to be worth buying and desperate enough to try. Nobody talked much. You could hear the river sucking at the piers, and the gulls, and somebody's teeth chattering.

Ahead of us the Meridian rose out of the fog in pieces. First the black stone of its walls, wet and shining. Then the roofs. Then the tower.

Every child in the Wick can draw the Horologe from memory, and every child gets it wrong, because nothing prepares you for how big the face is. It hangs over the island like a second moon, pale and pitted, wide as the duck pond in Saltmarket, with Gilt numerals of beaten gold. There is only one hand, black iron, longer than a house.

It points at eleven. It has pointed at eleven for three hundred years.

In the Wick we say the rich stopped the clock so they'd never have to go home.

"Fenn! Burn me, it *is* you."

A shoulder like a door slammed into mine, and I staggered and grinned before I'd even turned, because there was only one person in the world who said hello like that.

Bryony Kettle had broken my nose when we were seven and set it herself when we were eight, and we'd been something like friends ever since. She was a head taller than me and twice as wide in the shoulders, with a boxer's flattened knuckles and a laugh you could hear from the next street. Her sleeve was rolled to show fifty-eight bright years, and she'd scrubbed her arm so hard for the occasion that the skin around them was pink.

"Didn't think you'd sink this low," she said.

"Somebody has to keep you out of trouble."

"Trouble." She snorted. "I'm going to get bought by someone very rich and very stupid, and I'm going to rob him blind, and in three years I'll come back over this bridge in a carriage with my own Grace in my pocket."

"You can't buy your own Grace."

"Watch me." She looked down at my arm, and her eyebrows went up. "Sixty-one. Saints' teeth, Tam. Where've you been hiding that?"

My heart did a hard, small thing against my ribs. "Same place you've been hiding your manners."

She barked a laugh and threw an arm over my shoulders, and we walked the last of the bridge like that, under the clock that had stopped, into the Meridian.

---

The Tithe Court is a long yard of black flagstones at the foot of the Horologe, enclosed on three sides by colonnades and on the fourth by the tower itself. They lined us up along the colonnade in a single row, sleeves rolled, arms held out, palms up. A clerk in Ledger grey with ink-stained cuffs walked down the line with a register and a pencil, writing each name and count in a fine copperplate. His name, he told us without looking at any of us, was Clerk Penn, and we were to speak only when spoken to and keep our arms out until we were told otherwise, and we were not, under any circumstances, to touch a wright.

Above us, around three sides of the court, ran a gallery behind a balustrade of pale stone, and in the gallery sat the Long-Lived.

I'd seen them before, from a distance: carriages on the Gilt road, a figure on a terrace. I had never seen them up close, and I wished I still hadn't. They looked like anyone else at a glance, men and women in silk and fur, some of them looking forty, some thirty, one or two no older than me. But there was something wrong with the way they sat. They were too still. They didn't fidget. They didn't shift their weight or scratch their noses or look at the sky. They watched us the way you watch a market stall before you decide which apple to pick up, and they'd been doing that, some of them, for two hundred years.

One of them, a woman in black lace near the center of the gallery, was eating something from a small silver box. She lifted each piece to her mouth between two fingers, slowly, as if she had all the time in the world. I suppose she did.

Then the novices came down the steps from the tower, and the court went quiet.

There were twelve of them that year, the new wrights of the Seven Houses, in black coats with sashes of their House colors across the chest: Crane gold, Vey green, Ostrander blue, Lisle white. They were our age. That was the strangest thing. I'd imagined them older, but they were just young people, nervous some of them, bored some of them, one girl in Marchbank rust chewing her thumbnail. Each one walked with a binder at their elbow, and they started down the line.

They looked at our arms. That was all, really. They looked at our counts, and they asked the binders questions in low voices, and once in a while a binder would take somebody's wrist and tap a silver needle against the top line of their count. *Proving*, it was called. The usual Market fraud is gilt paint, Wick kids drawing on extra lines to look longer and fetch a fatter Grace. A real count shimmers when the needle draws its minute. Paint doesn't.

Nobody in the history of Lanthorn has ever hidden years at a Tithe Market. It's the one place in the city where long is worth money.

I kept my arm out, palm up, and my face empty, and told my heart to be quiet.

Down the line, a novice in Crane gold stopped in front of Bry.

He was the kind of beautiful that makes you suspicious. Golden hair, golden skin, a face built for statues and coins. He looked at her fifty-eight years for a long moment and then he smiled.

"Fifty-eight," he said, to nobody in particular. "Sturdy." He reached out with one gloved finger and tilted her chin up, the way you'd turn a vase to the light.

"Touch my face again," said Bry, "and you'll be counting your fingers."

Clerk Penn made a sound like a kettle. The binder at the golden boy's elbow went grey. And the golden boy threw back his head and laughed, delighted, as if she'd given him a present.

"Oh," he said. "I'll have *this* one."

He moved on. Bry's jaw was tight enough to crack walnuts. I wanted to say something to her and couldn't, because a novice had stopped in front of me.

He wore Penhallow plum, but his sash was faded, mended in one place with thread that didn't quite match. He was tall and narrow, with red-brown hair that wouldn't lie flat and a long, freckled, cheerful face that looked as if it had been designed for telling jokes at funerals.

"Hello," he said, and then, to my astonishment, he bowed a little. "Hollis Penhallow. I'm terribly sorry about all this."

I stared at him.

"I'm meant to be inspecting you," he went on, lowering his voice, "but honestly it feels a bit like being asked to choose which kitten to drown."

"Then don't choose," I said, before I could stop myself. Speak only when spoken to.

"Ah. Well. If I don't, Mother chooses for me, and she has appalling taste in kittens." He glanced at my arm and his eyebrows climbed. "Sixty-one. Saints. I'd sell my soul for sixty-one. Well. I'd sell my mother's. Mine's not worth much on the open market."

"What's yours?" It came out of me before I knew it was a question you didn't ask a wright.

He didn't seem to mind. He hooked a finger under his own cuff, which I noticed was not jeweled like the others but plain, dented tin, and pulled it back an inch. Six gold lines. Two notches.

"Six years and two months," said Hollis Penhallow cheerfully. "So you see, we're all in this together. More or less. Mostly less." He let the cuff fall. "Good luck, Miss—"

"Fenn."

"Good luck, Miss Fenn." And he moved on, and I found I was almost smiling, which was the last thing I'd expected to do in the Tithe Court.

The binder at the next novice's elbow took my wrist without asking.

His hand was dry and cool. He set the point of his silver needle against the tip of my highest lit line, the sixty-first, right at the edge of the ink. I didn't breathe. If the needle slid a hair higher it would touch tallow ink instead of gold, and tallow ink doesn't shimmer, and a binder's eye is trained to notice what doesn't shimmer.

He drew.

You can feel a minute go. It's like missing a stair in the dark: a small, sick drop behind the breastbone and then nothing. The faint shine at the tip of my count, the days, dimmed like breath fogging a mirror.

The binder was already looking down, writing in his book. *Sixty-one years, four months. Proven.*

And while he wrote, the shine at the tip of my count brightened again. Slowly. The way an ember brightens when someone breathes on it. In the span of three heartbeats my minute came home.

The binder didn't see. The novice he was with, a Lisle girl in white, was looking at the boy beside me. Nobody saw.

I let my breath out through my nose, very carefully, and lifted my eyes.

On the far side of the court, in the shadow of the tower arch where the colonnade began, someone was standing very still.

I hadn't seen him arrive. I don't think anyone had. He was tall and dressed entirely in black, buttoned to the throat, no sash, as though he didn't need one to tell anyone what House he belonged to. Black gloves. A wide silver cuff on his left forearm that caught the grey light. Dark hair, a little too long, as if nobody had dared tell him to cut it. His face was the kind you see in portraits in rich people's hallways, the kind whose eyes follow you down the corridor and disapprove of everything you do.

He was looking at me. At my arm. At the tip of my count, where the minute had just come home.

Around me, the line of candidates had gone tense in the way animals go tense when something with teeth walks into the clearing. I heard a girl two places down whisper it, and the boy beside her whisper it back.

*Thane.*

*That's the Thane heir.*

*His last tithe went home in a box.*

*Four months, she lasted. Four months.*

*It was a boy. Orrin something.*

*There was another one before that.*

The heir of House Thane looked away from me, unhurried, as if I had been a mildly interesting crack in the paving, and walked to the end of the colonnade, and stood there with his gloved hands clasped behind his back.

He didn't inspect anyone else. He didn't speak to anyone. He waited.

---

The bidding is done in years.

That's what the Grace is: years of life, paid out of a House's own reserves into the count of whoever the tithe names. Five is ordinary. Seven is generous. Ten is the floor for a good long count, by law, and plenty of Houses pay exactly the floor and not a minute more.

Clerk Penn read our names from the register one by one, and the novices bid, and the binders recorded. It went fast. Nobody made speeches. A boy from the Sump went to Vey for six. Joss Mallory, whom I knew a little from the Candleworks, all grin and big ears and sixty-six bright years, went to Ostrander for nine, and punched the air when they called it, because nine meant his girl's mother would see another winter. Bry went to the golden boy, whose name turned out to be Lysander Crane, for eight. She didn't punch the air. She looked at me across the court, and I looked back, and neither of us had anything to say.

"Tamsin Fenn," read Clerk Penn. "Of Tallow Lane. Nineteen years of age. Sixty-one years, four months. Proven."

"Ten," said Lysander Crane immediately, as though he'd been waiting.

A murmur in the gallery. Two tithes from the same novice was allowed, apparently. Nobody did it.

"Eleven," said the Marchbank girl with the bitten thumbnail.

"Twelve." Ostrander.

"Fifteen," said Lysander Crane, and smiled at me, and I felt the smile land on my skin like a fly.

Fifteen. Kit would be twenty-six. Kit would see twenty-six. I didn't care who said it. I didn't care if the golden boy wanted to do to me whatever he wanted to do to Bry. Fifteen years for my brother. I could survive anything they did to me. That was the whole point of me.

"Fifteen," said Clerk Penn. "Fifteen, from Crane. Any further—"

"Thirty."

It wasn't loud. It didn't need to be. The whole court turned.

The Thane heir hadn't moved from the end of the colonnade. He stood with his hands clasped behind him and said it again, in the same flat, clear voice, as though he were reading a number off a bill. "Thirty years."

The silence was so complete I could hear the river.

Then the gallery broke into a rustle like dry leaves, the Long-Lived turning to one another for the first time all morning. Clerk Penn's pencil had stopped moving. Lysander Crane laughed, but it came out a little high.

"Cousin," he said. "She's a Wick girl, not a racehorse."

The Thane heir did not look at him. He looked at Clerk Penn. "Thirty years of Grace," he said, "to be paid in full to Christopher Fenn, of Tallow Lane, aged ten."

All the air went out of me.

Nobody had asked me yet. That comes after the bidding: the clerk asks the tithe who's to receive the Grace, and you say the name, and they write it down. Nobody had asked me. I hadn't said Kit's name aloud since I left the house. And this stranger, this boy in black whose tithes went home in boxes, had just said it across the Tithe Court as if he'd known it all his life.

"My lord." Clerk Penn wet his lips. "Thirty. The Ledger has no precedent for—"

"Then set one."

Clerk Penn looked helplessly up at the gallery. I followed his eyes without meaning to. The woman in black lace near the center put another small, dark purple something into her mouth, chewed, swallowed, and nodded once.

"Thirty," said Clerk Penn faintly. "From Thane. Sold."

---

The threading happens right there, in the court, under the clock.

A binder brought a little lacquered box and opened it, and inside on black velvet lay a curved silver needle and a spool of thread so fine it looked like a line of light. The heir of House Thane walked over to me at last, and stopped an arm's length away, and drew off his left glove.

His hand was pale and long-fingered and scattered with small healed nicks across the knuckles, the kind you get working with tools. The silver cuff covered his forearm from wrist to elbow, so I couldn't see his count. But below the cuff, around the narrow bones of his wrist, I could see two thin white scars, fine as cobweb, one above the other.

Old threads.

The binder took my wrist and then his. "It will hurt," he said, to me, not to him. That was all the warning I got.

It feels like being sewn shut. That's the only way I can describe it. The needle goes in under the skin and around and the thread follows it, cold and then burning, and for a moment the whole world narrows to the circle of your own wrist. Then the binder pulled the thread across the space between us and did the same to the Thane heir, and he didn't even flinch, and then the binder said a word I didn't know and tied a knot in the air between our hands.

The thread sank into my skin and vanished, leaving a line of silver like a bracelet.

And I felt him.

A second heartbeat, under my own. Slow and very even, as if it had been trained to be. I could feel his pulse in my wrist the way you feel music through a floor.

He was looking down at me. Up close his eyes were a pale, clear grey, the exact grey of tallow ink. He pulled his glove back on, finger by finger. Then he leaned down, close enough that nobody else in the Tithe Court could hear, close enough that I could smell clock oil and cold air on his coat.

"Your count moved, Miss Fenn," said Severin Thane. "I watched it go up."
