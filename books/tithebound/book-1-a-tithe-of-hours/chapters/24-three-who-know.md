# Chapter Twenty-Four

## Three Who Know

Hollis carried her.

I wouldn't have believed it if I hadn't seen it. He was long and narrow and freckled and looked as though a stiff wind off the Sallow would fold him in half, and Bry was a head taller than me and built like a coal barge. But he bent and got one arm under her knees and one around her back, and lifted her off the stone floor of that little square room, and staggered once, and then didn't stagger again.

"Penhallows," he said, through his teeth, "are much stronger than we look. It's all the coffins."

"Put me *down*," said Bry, faintly, into his neck. "I can walk."

"You can't. You've got twenty years and no feet. Hush."

She hushed. I think it was the shock of being told to.

I went ahead with my arm held up to light the way. Back down the corridor, past the guttering torch, down the steps into the black water, through the iron door with the clock faces all pointing at eleven. Before we went through it, I looked back once, at the gap in the far wall of the little room where the passage went on, deeper, into the dark. The beat was still coming up through the stone. Slow. Patient. My thread pulled toward it, warm, like a hand on my wrist.

*Not yet,* I told it. I don't know why. *Not tonight.*

The cistern was worse going back. Hollis had to hold Bry up out of the water, and he couldn't see the floor, and twice he went down on one knee in the silt and came up gasping with water streaming off his chin and Bry still held clear. I walked beside him and held my light high and counted pillars out loud for him, so he'd know how far, and lied about the number every time.

Pell was where I'd left him, at the top of the seventy-three stairs, planted in front of the iron door with his huge fists clenched. When he saw the light coming up the steps, he made a sound like a dog that's been left alone all day. And when he saw what Hollis was carrying, he came down the stairs three at a time and took her, the whole of her, as easily as if she were a child, and held her against his chest, and buried his face in her wet hair.

"You're cold," he kept saying. "You're so cold."

"Burn me, Pell," said Bry, with her eyes shut. "Don't squeeze. I've only just got my insides back."

---

We put her in her bed with every blanket in the Hall on top of her, and dragged the brazier as close as it would go, and Pell sat on the floor beside her and held her hand. Hollis sat on the end of my bed in his ruined dressing gown, shaking so hard his teeth rattled, and I wrapped my own blanket around him and he didn't argue.

Nobody else woke. The tithes slept on under their blankets in their faint gold glow, every one of them, the way they'd slept the night Anni went. I didn't let myself think about that.

Steward Rook was still asleep in her chair.

I went back to her with the great iron ring of keys wrapped in the corner of my blanket, and knelt, and lifted her folded hands off her lap a quarter inch at a time, and slid the ring underneath, and laid her hands back on top of it.

Her good eye opened.

Just a crack. Just enough for me to see the lamplight in it. It looked at me, kneeling there on the cold stone, soaked to the chest, with blood dried on my lip and a raw red welt on my wrist beside the silver thread. It looked at the iron door to the stairs, closed and locked.

Then it closed again.

Her breathing went long and slow. She didn't say a word.

I got up and went back to my bed and sat down beside Hollis, and I didn't know what had just happened, and I decided I didn't need to.

---

Severin came at dawn.

I felt him before anyone saw him: the thread at my wrist going hot, and then his heartbeat coming in, fast and ragged, the way it had come in Rook's office the morning after Anni. Then the iron door opened, and Rook stood up out of her chair, and there he was on the threshold of the Tithe Hall.

No wright had ever come into the Tithe Hall. Not in living memory. Rook had told us so the first night, with some satisfaction. Down the row, tithes who'd been asleep a moment before sat up in their blankets and stared.

He was soaked to the thighs. Mud and silt to the knee, a long grey streak of it down one side of his black coat, his gloves black with river muck, his hair plastered flat. His face was the color of the stone. He looked as though he'd been standing in cold water all night.

He looked down the Hall until he found me.

I watched him find me. I watched his whole body change, the way a stilled man's body changes when the working breaks: something going out of his shoulders, something coming back into his face. He put one hand on the doorframe. He didn't come in.

"I felt you go into the cold," he said. Not loud. It carried anyway, down the long stone room. "Last night. Through the thread. I went down the Thane passage to the gate in the tunnel. The one with the chain." He swallowed. "I couldn't get through it. I tried. All night."

The tithes were staring. Rook was staring. Pell, on the floor by Bry's bed, had turned his head and was staring with his mouth open.

I looked at Severin, standing in the doorway in his ruined coat. I thought about the door at the top of the tower that wouldn't open for five days. About the folder. *Falls? No.* About six hours in his grandmother's study and *I was going to fix it first*.

About him standing in black water all night at an iron gate, trying to get to me.

I didn't know what to do with any of it. So I did nothing. I turned my head away, toward the vaulted wall, and looked at the crack in the stone shaped like a lightning bolt.

After a long moment, I heard the iron door close.

---

"Well," said Hollis.

It was midmorning. The others had gone up to breakfast and lessons. Rook had looked at Bry's bed, and at Pell beside it, and at Hollis still sitting on mine in somebody else's spare grey jacket and his own ruined dressing gown, and had said, "The infirmary's full. Kettle stays here. Penhallow, you're excused from Theory of Hours, I've no doubt you'll bear the loss." And then she'd gone out and locked the door behind her, and it was only the four of us in the long stone room, with the green river light coming in cloudy through the frosted windows.

Bry was sitting up against the iron headrail with a cup of Pell's tea between her hands. She had color in her face again. Not much. Enough.

I'd rolled my sleeve up to the shoulder.

There was nothing left on it but the last thin smear of ink I'd stretched out of the thimble pot at Thane House, and it was failing, cracked and lifted in a dozen places by the cistern water, so that gold showed through everywhere like sun through a torn blind. Seventy-one years. Four months. The twenty I'd given Bry had all come home in the night. Every one.

They looked at it. All three of them. For a long time.

"Since you were *nine*," said Bry again, as if she still couldn't make it fit in her head.

"Since I was nine."

"And you've been selling—"

"Fifteen years. In Saltmarket, mostly. Under other names."

"And they come *back*."

"They always come back."

"*Burn* me." Bry put her cup down on the blanket. "All those years. When we were kids. When you were working Gannet's counter, and I was boxing for coppers in the Sump, and Kit's lungs were bad, and you never had anything, *never*. You were sitting on a—" She stopped. She stared at me. "You could have been rich."

"I could have been dead," I said. "A long count gets a knock on the door. A count that comes back gets a sack and a black thread and a cellar in the Gilt for the rest of your life." I pulled my sleeve down. "Ask the woman in Vadra's engraving."

Nobody said anything.

Then Hollis laughed.

It burst out of him, high and astonished, and he clapped both hands over his mouth as if he could stuff it back in, and couldn't, and it kept coming. He laughed until there were tears on his face, and Pell looked at him with alarm, and Bry looked at him as though he'd lost his mind.

"Sorry," Hollis gasped. "Sorry. I'm sorry. It's just—" He wiped his eyes with the sleeve of the borrowed jacket. "Tam. Do you know what you *are*? Do you have any idea? Every House in the Gilt would go to war for you. *War.* The Cranes would sell their own grandmothers. The Lisles would sell their grandmothers' *bones*. You could buy the Ledger. You could buy the *city*. And you've been sitting in the Tithe Hall in scratchy grey wool, faking your count with *spit*." He started laughing again, helplessly. "It's the most Wick thing I've ever heard in my life."

"It's not funny, Hollis."

"It's a bit funny."

"It's a bit funny," I agreed. And then, somehow, I was laughing too, and so was Bry, weakly, into her blanket, and even Pell, looking from one of us to the other with his gentle face confused, started to smile.

It didn't last long. It couldn't. But for a minute, in that cold stone room under the river, the four of us laughed until we couldn't breathe.

---

When it was over, Hollis sat looking at his own wrist. At his tin cuff, plain and dented, over his six years and two months.

I knew what he was thinking. I'd known since the cistern, since he knelt beside me on the wet stone in the torchlight and watched my count fill back up. I'd been thinking it too.

"Hollis," I said.

He didn't look up.

"Six years," I said. "That's nothing to me. You saw. Twenty went into Bry last night and came back before dawn. I could give you ten. Twenty. Forty. You could see fifty. You could—"

"*Don't.*"

It came out of him like a slap. Hollis, who never raised his voice, who apologized to pigeons. He'd gone white to the lips.

"Don't you dare," he said. "Don't you *dare* offer me a single hour."

I stared at him.

"Do you understand what you just did?" He was looking at me now, and his long freckled face was furious. "Last night. In that room. You poured twenty years into Bry, and this morning she's got twenty years on her arm where last night she had two weeks. And on Seventhday, Rook's going to hold her arm up to the lamp, and write *twenty* in her book. And Lysander Crane, who *left* her with two weeks, who left the *needle*, is going to look at Bry's arm across the Refectory and know exactly what happened." He swallowed. "Every hour you give away is an hour somebody can see you give. Every single one. And I'm not going to be the reason they find you."

"They've already found me, Hollis. She knows. Severin told me."

"Then I'm not going to be the reason they're *sure*." His voice cracked. "And I'm not going to be one more thing you carry. Do you hear me? You've been carrying Kit since you were nine. You've been carrying Bry since you were five. You've been carrying Severin's secrets and your own and the whole stupid Tithe Hall on your back since Tithe Day, and you think nobody's noticed, and we have. *I* have." He stopped. He took a breath. "I'm a Penhallow. We die young. We're very good at it. Let me be good at something."

The Hall was very quiet. Up on the frosted windows, the green light shifted as something moved on the river.

"I'm sorry," I said.

"Don't be." He wiped his face with the sleeve of the borrowed jacket again, roughly. "Just don't ever offer again. Promise me."

I promised.

---

"She was there," said Bry.

We all turned to her. She was holding her cup in both hands, but she wasn't drinking from it. She was looking at the far end of the Hall, at the small iron door with its cut chain, which Rook had found that morning and stared at for a very long time and then barred again without a word.

"Last night," said Bry. "In that room. Before he started. I didn't tell you. I didn't—I wasn't sure, at first, I thought I'd dreamed it." She swallowed. "Lysander took me down through the water. In the dark. And there was someone already in that room when we got there. Waiting."

My skin went cold all over.

"I couldn't see her," said Bry. "The torch was behind her. Just the shape. Small. Smaller than you, Tam. In black. And she was eating something." Her mouth twisted. "Something that crunched. And it smelled sweet. Sweet like flowers. Like—"

"Violets," said Hollis, very quietly.

Bry looked at him. "Yes."

Nobody said anything. Pell had gone very still.

"She talked to him," said Bry. "While he was doing it. While he was taking it out of me. Like they were having tea. And when he'd got me down to the last notch, she said, *Leave her two weeks, darling. Leave the needle. If the girl's what we think, she'll come, and she won't be able to help herself.*" Bry's hands were shaking around the cup. "And Lysander said, *And then we take her?* And she laughed. And she said, *Not yet. Oh, not yet.*"

She looked at me.

"*At the Reckoning,*" said Bry. "That's what she said. *In front of everyone. I want them to see.*"

---

Two weeks went by.

I don't know how. They went the way time always goes at the Meridian, ticking along like one of Severin's clocks, bells and lessons and meals. Bry got better. She got up on the third day, and walked to the Refectory on the fifth, and on the eighth she put her fist through a door in the tithes' washroom because Corrie from Vey said something about Crane, and Rook made her pay for the door out of her Grace and didn't seem very angry about it.

On Seventhday, Rook held Bry's arm up to the lamp, and looked at it for a long time, and said, "Kettle. Twenty years, one month." And wrote it in her book. And then looked at me, down the line, with her good eye, and didn't say anything at all.

Lysander didn't send for Bry again. Not once. He didn't need to. Across the Refectory at supper, the first night after the weighing, he looked down the long room at the tithes' wall, at Bry with twenty years bright on her bare arm, and then at me. And he smiled, and raised his glass an inch, and drank.

I went to Magister Vadra. I told her I'd run out of ink. She looked at me for a long time across her green study, and then opened a drawer in her desk and took out a small clay pot sealed with wax and put it in my hand, and said nothing, and I said nothing, and I went.

I didn't go up the tower.

The door would open for me. I knew that now. I could feel him up there every night, through the thread. Not shut away anymore. Open. Right there. His heartbeat sitting under mine all evening, waiting, the way you'd wait by a door for a knock. Sometimes, late, it would go fast, as if he'd stood up, as if he were coming down. It never came down.

I lay in the dark with my hand over my wrist and didn't go up.

---

On the fourteenth day, at the morning weighing, Steward Rook closed her book and stood at the end of the aisle with her hands behind her back and her milky eye moving over all of us.

"The Reckoning," she said, "is in three days."

Down the line, somebody's breath caught.

"For those of you who've not had the pleasure, I'll tell you how it goes. You'll stand on the Dial with your wrights. The whole Meridian will watch. The Long-Lived will come over from the Gilt and sit in their gallery. And every wright on this island will cast. The first-years, something to show they've learned to measure. The final-years, their great working: the biggest thing they can cast, to show the Houses what they're worth. All of it drawn from their tithes." Her eye moved down the line. "From *you*."

Nobody said anything. The river moved past the frosted windows.

"And because it's tradition," said Rook, "before each working, and after, your count will be read aloud. To the whole Dial. By the Keeper of the Ledger herself."

Her milky eye came to rest on me.

"She'll be looking at every arm on that floor," said Steward Rook. "Every one. Before and after."
