# Chapter Eighteen

## Stilling

His hand closed on my wrist in the dark.

"Stay with me," said Severin.

He didn't let go. He pulled me back through the glass doors into the ballroom, and the screaming came up around us like water rising in a flooded cellar.

It was dark, but not entirely. The fountain was still glowing. That was the only light in the whole enormous room: the great black basin in the middle, and the silver woman rising from it with her arms raised, and the streams of gold pouring from her hands, lighting everything nearby from below, so that every face near the fountain looked like a face in a fire. Everything else was shadow. The mirrored walls had gone black. The silver stars on the ceiling had gone out. And in the dark, people were moving. Running, stumbling, colliding. Glass smashed somewhere, and smashed again. A woman was shrieking one word over and over, a name. A man was shouting for lights, for footmen, for *anyone*.

And there were others.

I saw them in the gold glow from the fountain, coming in through the servants' doors along the far wall. Men in black, with black cloth over their faces, and on every mask a single slash of white across the forehead, like a thread cut through. They moved fast and low, in twos and threes, and they had things in their hands, and when the fountain light caught what they were holding, it gleamed silver.

Needles. Broker's needles, black with use, as long as my hand.

"*Free the tithes!*" one of them roared, in a Wick voice, from the dark. "*Unwound! Free the tithes!*"

Along the walls, the tithes in their grey began to scatter. A masked man had one of the Marchbank girls by the arm and was dragging her toward a servants' door while she screamed and kicked. Another had a Vey boy around the waist. I saw a Lisle wright, old enough to be a final-year, try to cast something, a flare of gold in his hand, and a masked man came out of the dark behind him and put a needle into his wrist, and the gold went out.

"That's not them," said Severin, against my ear. His voice was very flat.

"What?"

"The Unwound don't do this. They cut threads. They don't take tithes in the dark at Thane House." His hand tightened on my wrist. "Somebody wants it to look like them."

I didn't understand. I didn't have time to understand. Something hit us.

Not something. Someone. A shoulder in the dark, hard, slamming into Severin from the side, and his hand was torn off my wrist, and I heard him make a sound, a grunt of pain, and then I was spinning, alone, in the black, in the crowd, with people crashing into me from every side, a sleeve, an elbow, a woman's hair, somebody's heel on my foot.

"*Severin—*"

A hand closed around my arm above the elbow.

I knew it before I knew it. I knew it the way you know a smell from your childhood. Wine and expensive soap. A grip like a vice.

"Come along, little tithe," said Lysander Crane in my ear. "Let's get you somewhere safe."

---

I fought.

I fought the way Bry had taught me when we were eleven, the way I'd fought on the Hours Bridge in the fog with my teeth in a gloved hand. I twisted and kicked and went for his eyes. I got my heel into his shin. I got my nails into the back of his hand, hard enough to feel the skin go. He laughed. Right in my ear, warm and delighted, the way he'd laughed in the Tithe Court when Bry told him she'd break his fingers.

And then he moved, and I moved with him, and the world went to streaks.

A hastening. I'd never been inside one. You don't feel fast. You feel as though everything else has gone slow and thick and stupid, like honey, like being dragged through a dream: the faces around us frozen with their mouths half open, the glass hanging in the air where somebody had dropped it, the screams stretched out into long low moans. And his hand on my arm, dragging me through it all, toward the far wall, toward a servants' door standing open on darkness.

I could feel what it was costing. Not me. Him. And not his own time, either. Through his hand, through my skin, I could feel the years burning out of him, hot and fast and careless, as though he had more than he could ever use.

*Saving up,* Bry had said. *Like he's filling a jar.*

He was spending her. He was dragging me through Thane House on Bry's years.

The servants' door was getting closer. Twenty paces. Fifteen. Beyond it, in the dark, I could see stairs going down.

*Below*, I thought. Everyone in this city who'd ever vanished had gone *below*.

Ten paces.

And then, at my wrist, the thread went hot.

---

I heard him before I saw him. Not with my ears. In my wrist, in my blood: the second heartbeat, slamming hard and fast and close, so close, coming toward me through the dark like someone running down a corridor.

*I'm coming.*

I don't know if he said it. I don't know if I imagined it. But I felt it, through the thread, as clearly as if he'd put his mouth to my ear.

And then I felt him cast.

I'd felt him cast before. In the Lantern Theatre, the warm trickle welling up out of his wrist into a ribbon of water. On the Dial, the hot rush of it, the hastenings, the gold streaks. In the tower, seven minutes for a match.

This was not like any of that.

This was a river.

It came up out of him so fast and so huge that I felt the thread *buck* at my wrist like a rope with a horse on the end of it. It poured out of him into the air of the ballroom, into the dark, into everything, and the air rang. Not like a fingertip on a glass. Like every glass in Lanthorn struck at once. A high, enormous, unbearable note that went on and on and on, so loud I felt it in my teeth, in my spine, in the backs of my eyes, and the gold light came with it, not a ribbon, not a streak: a wave, rolling out from somewhere in the middle of the ballroom in a great ring, washing over the black marble and the crowds and the masked men and the fountain and the mirrored walls, gold, gold, everywhere.

And everything stopped.

Everything.

The screaming stopped mid-scream. The running stopped mid-stride. The glass that somebody had dropped hung in the air, a crystal flute turning slowly in the gold light, three feet above the floor, and didn't turn anymore. The streams of gold pouring from the silver woman's hands in the fountain stopped mid-fall, arcs of light frozen in the air like bent glass.

Lysander stopped.

His hand was still clamped on my arm. His face, inches from mine, was caught in the middle of a laugh, his golden head thrown back a little, his mouth open, his eyes bright. A man made of glass. A statue of a beautiful boy laughing. And the servants' door, five paces away, and the stairs going down into the dark.

I could move.

I didn't understand that at first. I stood there in the ringing gold with Lysander's frozen hand on my arm, and I turned my head, and it turned. I lifted my free hand and it lifted. Everyone in the ballroom had been stilled, every guest, every footman, every masked man with his needle, every tithe at the wall. Everyone except me.

Because the thread runs through the caster. Vadra had said so, the first week. A working doesn't touch the one who casts it, and it doesn't touch the one he's tethered to, because they're part of the same line.

I looked across the ballroom.

He was in the middle of it. Right beside the fountain, in the gold light, with his left arm raised high above his head, the silver cuff blazing, his fingers spread wide as if he were holding up the ceiling. His face was white. His teeth were bared. I'd never seen him look like that, never, not once, not even on the Dial.

He was holding the whole room still. Everyone in Thane House. Hundreds of people.

I did the sum.

I didn't want to. It did itself, the way sums do, the way he'd taught me at the velvet table every night for a week. A stilling. A room this size. This many people. Held this long. Every second past the first costs more than the last, because the working has to keep reaching.

*A year,* said the counter in my head. *Two. Three. Four.*

He had nine.

---

I felt it go.

I mean I felt it go out of him. Through the thread. The way I'd felt Joss's hours go into Fitch Ostrander's candle at the First Draw, except that this wasn't hours, it was years, it was his *life*, pouring out of his wrist into the ringing gold air like blood out of an opened vein. I felt each one go. I swear I did. A long hot surge, and then a drop, like missing a stair in the dark, and then another surge. One. Another. Another.

Nine. Eight.

His arm was shaking.

Seven.

I'd stopped thinking. I'd stopped doing sums. I was looking at him across the room, at his white face and his bared teeth and his arm held up against the dark, and something in me just *moved*.

I didn't know I could do it. Nobody had ever told me it could be done. A tithe is a well; the wright draws. That's the whole of it. That's all Magister Vadra had ever taught us, all the Table of Hours said, all the Ledger knew. The time goes one way.

I took hold of the thread.

Not with my hand. With whatever it is in you that knows your own count, that feels a minute go out under a binder's needle like missing a stair. I reached down into my wrist, into the silver line there, the line I'd felt for nine weeks like a drum in another room, and I took hold of it, and I *pushed*.

And it went.

Warmth. Out of me. Up out of my arm, out of the long gold ladder under the ink, through my wrist, into the thread, along it, across the whole dark frozen ballroom, toward him. I felt it go. I felt a year leave me, and another, and a third, not slowly like at Pruett's counter but in a rush, a long hot pour, and it hurt, it hurt the way it always hurt, a terrible lonely falling feeling under the breastbone. And I didn't stop.

*Take it,* I thought. *Take it, take it, take it, you stupid boy.*

Across the room, his head turned.

Slowly. Like a man moving underwater. His arm still high, still shaking, still holding up the whole of Thane House. His face turned toward me through the gold light, and his eyes found mine across all that frozen distance, and I saw them change.

He'd felt it.

He'd felt it arrive. Into his wrist. Into his count. Warm and fast, coming the wrong way down the line. Coming *from* me. And I watched him understand it, the way I'd watched him understand in the tower when the cuff came off: slowly, all at once, completely.

I didn't stop. I gave him another. And another.

And then, under my sleeve, under the silver lace and Celandine Penhallow's silk and Gannet's four careful coats of ink, I felt the other thing happen. The thing that had always happened. The thing I'd never once in my life been able to stop.

My count began to fill.

Not fast. Not like a cup. Like a bucket, slow and steady, the way it always was when I'd lost more than a few days: a warmth coming up from the bottom, a heaviness returning, the years I'd just given flowing back into my arm from wherever it was they always came from, as though somebody somewhere were patiently pouring them in.

And he could feel that too. I knew he could. I could see it in his face. He could feel my years coming into him along the thread, and he could feel, behind them, under them, my count not dropping. Not going down at all. Filling up again as fast as I poured it out.

His lips moved. I couldn't hear him over the ringing. I didn't have to.

*You refill.*

---

He held it for one breath longer.

Then, with his arm still high, he started to walk toward me.

Across the black marble, through the frozen crowd, between people standing like statues with their mouths open and their hands raised and their glasses hanging in the air. Past a masked man frozen in mid-lunge with his needle out. Past the Lisle wright on his knees with his wrist held up. Past Pell, enormous, stopped in the act of lifting a gilt chair over his head, his gentle face screwed up in a snarl I'd never have believed he could make. Past Hollis, caught with his arms spread wide in front of the Vey girl in green, shielding her, his long freckled face fierce and frightened at once. Past Bry, stopped dead with her fist buried to the wrist in a masked man's stomach and her teeth bared in a grin.

He reached me.

He didn't say anything. He took Lysander's frozen fingers, one by one, with his bare right hand, and peeled them off my arm. They didn't want to come; a stilled hand is like a stilled anything, stiff as cold iron. He bent them back anyway. Then he put his arm around me, his raised left arm still holding up the room, his right arm pulling me hard against his chest, and I put my face in the black velvet of his coat, and I felt his heart going under my cheek and in my wrist at the same time, two drums, both of them racing.

"You stupid girl," he said, into my hair. His voice was shaking. "You stupid, stupid girl."

"You first," I said into his coat.

His arm tightened.

And somewhere behind us, in the middle of the frozen ballroom, in the gold-lit silence, something moved.

I felt him feel it. His whole body went rigid against mine. I lifted my head from his chest and turned, and looked.

At the far end of the ballroom, on the low dais under the black silk canopy, there was a small figure in black lace and a crown of silver clock hands.

She was moving.

Everyone else in Thane House was standing as still as the silver woman in the fountain. Every guest, every footman, every masked man, every tithe. Lysander, at my shoulder, laughing at nothing with his golden head thrown back. Hollis with his arms spread. The crystal glass hanging in the air.

Lady Aurelia Thane reached out one small hand to the silver box on the table at her elbow. She opened the lid. She took out a candied violet and put it between her lips, and chewed it, slowly, thoughtfully, looking down the whole long length of the frozen ballroom at her great-great-great-grandson standing in the gold light with his arm raised and a Wick girl held against his chest.

She swallowed.

And then she lifted her small hands, and smiled with both dimples, and began, slowly, to clap.
