# Chapter Fifteen

## Tallow Lane

Wat Sorrel's mother lived two streets from Tallow Lane, in a court off Candle Row where the drains backed up every spring, and on Fourthday evening she sent Wat a message by the boy who brought the annex its ink, and Wat brought it to Bry, and Bry brought it to me in the Refectory at supper with her face gone hard.

"Greycoat on Tallow Lane," she said, low, sliding onto the end of the tithes' bench beside me. "Yesterday afternoon. Knocking on doors."

I put my spoon down.

"Asking about your mam."

The Refectory went on around us, loud and warm, the clatter of plates and the wrights laughing at the long tables and the smell of roast meat we'd never get to eat. I sat very still in the middle of it.

"What about her?"

"What her count was. The year she went in the river." Bry kept her eyes on her bowl. "Whether she ever sold. Whether she had any other children." A pause. "Wat's mam says he had a list. He was asking about other women too, women who went in the river years back, all up and down Candle Row. But he knocked on your door, Tam. He talked to your Kit. Mrs. Achterberg came out on the landing with a poker and chased him down the stairs." She finally looked at me. "Red-faced, Wat's mam said. Big. Grey coat, silver buttons."

The red-faced Greycoat. The one who'd stood behind Marr in the Tithe Court that first morning, with his grim satisfaction, saying *sold out* and *they do it, I've seen it a dozen times in the Sump*.

*Whether she had any other children.*

I stood up.

"Tam," said Bry. "It's past the eighth bell. There's no laundry till Seventhday. You can't—"

"I know," I said, and went.

---

It was the first time I'd climbed the tower without being sent for.

Six hundred and twelve steps. I ran most of them. By Rook's landing my lungs were on fire, and I kept going, and at the top I didn't knock. I didn't have to. The black door opened by itself before I reached it, as if it had been listening for me.

He was at the velvet table. Not working. Sitting with his hands flat on the cloth in front of him, the way he'd sat the night after Joss, looking at nothing. When I came in he looked up, and something went across his face, too fast for me to read, and then it was gone.

"Fenn."

"A Greycoat went to Tallow Lane," I said. I was still breathing hard. "Yesterday. The red-faced one from the court. He talked to Kit. He asked about my mother. What her count was. Whether she ever sold." I swallowed. "Whether she had any other children."

He was on his feet before I'd finished.

He didn't ask anything. He didn't say *are you sure* or *what does it mean* or *it's past the eighth bell*. He went to a hook on the wall by the window and took down the plain dark Wick coat he'd worn on the bridge, and put it on over his black shirt, and pulled the sleeves down over his cuff, and took the lamp off the table.

"Get your jacket," he said. "We're going."

"There's no laundry till—"

"We're not going by the laundry."

---

He took me down the tower stairs, past Rook's landing, past two more landings, to the narrow door in the wall of the stairwell, the one he'd gone through the night the sluice groaned. Up close it was very plain, black wood without a handle, set so flush into the curved stone that you'd never see it unless you knew. He laid his bare left hand flat against it. Something clicked inside the wall.

The door swung inward onto darkness and cold, wet air.

"Thane passage," he said. "Every House has one. Ours is older." He held the lamp up. "Mind the steps. They're slick."

They were. They went down in a tight spiral, much narrower than the tower stair, so narrow my shoulders brushed both walls, and they were wet, and they went down a long way. Past where the court must be. Past where the Tithe Hall must be, under the river. I could hear water somewhere, dripping and running, and the air got colder and colder until my breath smoked in the lamplight.

Then the stairs ended, and we were in a tunnel.

It was brick, old brick, red gone black with damp, arched overhead just high enough for Severin to walk upright if he ducked his head a little. It ran away ahead of us in a straight line into the dark. The floor was stone, worn smooth down the middle. Water stood in puddles along the sides, and there was a smell I knew from the Sump in spring, river and rot and stone.

"We're under the Sallow," I said.

"Under the bed of it. The Founders built it. Before the bridge." His voice echoed strangely off the brick. "It comes up on the Wick bank, not far from the Row. A quarter of an hour, if you walk fast."

We walked fast.

For a while neither of us said anything. The lamp swung in his hand and threw our shadows ahead of us down the tunnel, long and then short and then long again. I could hear the river above us. Not hear it, exactly. Feel it. A kind of weight, pressing down on the brick, on the stone, on the back of my neck.

"You stopped sending for me," I said.

He didn't answer at once. When he did, he didn't look at me.

"Every night you climbed those stairs, Lysander added another line to his story. I thought if you stopped climbing, the story would get bored."

"It didn't."

"No."

We walked. Our footsteps echoed.

"I missed the lessons," I said. I don't know why I said it. It was the kind of thing you'd only say in a dark tunnel under a river, where nobody could see your face.

He was quiet for a few steps.

"So did I," said Severin.

---

About halfway along, the tunnel forked.

The main way went on ahead, straight, toward the Wick. But on the left, set into the brick wall, there was a second arch, lower and older-looking, the bricks around it a different color, almost black. Steps led down from it, steep, into a dark the lamp couldn't reach. And across the top of the steps, from wall to wall, there was a gate of black iron bars, rusted, with a great padlock on it and a chain through the padlock wrapped three times around the bars.

I stopped.

I didn't mean to. My feet just stopped, the way they'd stop at the edge of a roof. Because the thread on my wrist had gone warm.

Not hot, the way it went when Severin's heart jumped. Warm. A slow, spreading warmth, like a hand closing gently around it.

And under my feet, through the stone, I felt something.

A beat. Slow. Very slow, slower than any heart I'd ever heard. A long, deep throb that came up through the soles of my boots and into my bones, and then nothing for a long time, and then again. Like a drum being struck very softly a long way off. Like something enormous, asleep, breathing.

"Fenn." Severin had stopped a few steps ahead. He turned back with the lamp. "Not that way."

"What's down there?"

"The Undercroft." He came back and stood beside me and looked at the gate, and the lamplight lay along the side of his face. "It's sealed. It's been sealed since before my grandmother was born. Nobody goes down there."

"Something's breathing."

"It's the river." He said it very evenly. "All that water pressing on the stone. It makes the tunnel settle. You get used to it."

I looked at him. He was looking at the gate. Not at me.

The beat came again, up through my feet. Slow. Deep. And the thread at my wrist pulsed warm with it, once, as if answering.

"Come on," said Severin quietly. "Your brother's waiting."

I went. But I looked back at the gate twice before the lamplight lost it, and both times I could feel it, that slow beat in the stone, following me down the tunnel like a voice calling a name I couldn't quite hear.

---

The tunnel came up in a cellar full of empty crates that smelled of old tallow. There was a ladder, and a trapdoor, and above the trapdoor the dark ground floor of what must once have been a chandlery, with broken windows and moths. Severin put out the lamp. Then he opened a door onto an alley I'd walked through a hundred times without ever looking at this door, and the fog of the Wick came in around us, and the smell of it, and the sound of a dog barking two streets over.

Tallow Lane was ten minutes away. He walked half a step behind me the whole way, with his collar up and his hands in his pockets, and nobody looked at him twice.

Kit was awake.

Of course he was. He was sitting up in the cot with the blanket around his shoulders and a candle lit beside him, and he wasn't drawing. He was just sitting there, very straight, the way he sat when he was frightened and trying not to be. Mrs. Achterberg's poker was leaning against the wall by the door. When I came in he made a sound and threw the blanket off and was halfway across the room before he saw who was behind me.

He stopped dead in the middle of the floor.

Severin had to duck to come through our door. He stood just inside it, in his plain Wick coat, with his shoulders nearly touching both sides of the frame, and looked down at my little brother standing there in his nightshirt with his thin legs and his huge dark eyes, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all.

"Kit," I said. "This is—"

"I know who it is," said Kit, in a whisper.

Severin inclined his head, very gravely, the way I'd seen him bow to magisters in the corridor.

"Good evening, Christopher," he said.

Kit stared at him. Then, after a moment, he lifted his chin.

"Nobody calls me Christopher," he said.

"What do they call you?"

"Kit."

"Good evening, Kit."

"Good evening," said Kit. And then, because he was ten and couldn't help it: "Did you really pay *thirty*?"

"Kit—"

"I did," said Severin.

"Why?"

Severin looked at him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he folded himself down, all that height, until he was sitting on his heels on our bare boards with his coat pooling around him, so that his eyes were level with Kit's.

"Because your sister was worth it," he said. "And because I didn't want anyone else to have her."

Kit considered this with great seriousness.

"That's two reasons," he said.

"Most things have two reasons. The second one's usually the true one."

Kit thought about that too. Then he nodded, as if it were a perfectly reasonable answer, and sat down cross-legged on the floor facing him. "There was a man," he said. "Yesterday. In a grey coat."

I knelt down beside him. My hands were shaking. I put them in my lap.

He told it well. Kit always told things well, carefully, in order, the way he drew maps. The knock on the door in the afternoon. The big man with the red face and the silver buttons, who smelled of beer and talked too loud. Who asked his name, and how old he was, and who his mother was, and what her name had been, and when she'd gone in the river.

"I said I was a baby," said Kit. "I said I didn't remember. He asked what her count was. I said I didn't know. He asked if she ever went to the brokers. I said I didn't know." He frowned. "He asked if she had a girl. As well as me. Older."

My stomach turned over.

"What did you say?"

"I said no." Kit looked at me with his enormous eyes. "I said there was only me. Was that wrong?"

"No," I said. "No, that was right. That was exactly right."

"And then Mrs. Achterberg came out with the poker and said she'd brain him, and he went." Kit looked at Severin. "He had a list. On paper. He looked at it before he knocked. There were lots of names on it. Ours was near the bottom."

Severin said nothing. But I saw his hand, resting on his knee, close slowly into a fist.

---

Kit showed him the maps.

I don't know how it happened. One moment we were talking about the Greycoat, and the next Kit had scrambled up and fetched his whole sheaf of papers from under the cot and was spreading them out on the floor between them by candlelight, and Severin was bending over them with the same absorbed attention he gave to the inside of a watch. The Gilt with its terraces. The Candleworks with every chimney. The sea, which Kit had decided was green. Severin looked at every one. He asked questions. *Why is this street wider than that one? How did you know about the gate at the end of Salt Road?* And Kit answered every question with the shining seriousness of a scholar being examined by a magister.

Then Kit laid down the Meridian.

The new one, from above, as a gull would see it. The island and the bridge and the court and the tower. The Tithe Hall under the river. The cisterns, black and wavy. And below the tower, deep in the rock, the little circle with the heart in it.

Severin went still.

I'd seen him go still before. In the Refectory, with his grandmother. On the Dial, frozen in the middle of the clock. This was different. This was like watching a clock stop.

He put out one finger, bare, and touched the circle with the heart in it. Not quite touching. Just above the paper.

"Who told you about this?" he said quietly.

"Mama," said Kit. "Well. Tam told me Mama said. Where the clock keeps its heart."

Severin looked up at me over Kit's head.

I couldn't read his face. I couldn't read anything in it at all. But I could feel his heartbeat through the thread, and it had gone fast and hard, the way it had in the Lantern Theatre when Bry hit the floor.

I thought of the iron gate in the tunnel. The steps going down into the dark. The slow beat coming up through the stone.

*It's the river.*

He looked back down at the map. He didn't say anything else about it. After a moment, he reached into the inside pocket of his Wick coat and took something out and held it out to Kit, on his open palm.

It was the tin watch. The cheap one, with the dull case and the star-cracked crystal, that he'd been rebuilding the first night I came to the tower. The crystal had been replaced. The case had been polished. It was ticking.

"It keeps good time," said Severin. "Wind it every morning. Not too tight."

Kit took it in both hands as if it might break. He held it up to his ear. His whole face changed.

"Is it for me?"

"I've been fixing it for a while," said Severin. "I didn't know whom it was for. I think it was for you."

I had to look away. I looked at the window, at the fog pressing its wet face against the glass, and I bit the inside of my cheek until it hurt, because something was happening in my chest that I didn't want, that I couldn't afford, that I'd spent six weeks telling myself wasn't happening at all.

I'd thought he was a monster with a cellar. I'd thought he was a cold boy in a room full of clocks. And he was sitting on our bare floor in a Wick coat with his knees folded up like a heron, giving my brother a watch.

I hated it. I hated it so much.

---

We had to go. It was past the eleventh bell. I tucked Kit back into the cot with the watch under his pillow, ticking, and he let me, and he held on to my sleeve a moment longer than he usually did.

Severin was waiting in the doorway, ducking under the frame.

"Kit," he said. "If the man in the grey coat comes back, don't open the door. Go across to Mrs. Achterberg. Tell her to send for your sister through the Ledger annex. Wat Sorrel's mother knows how." He glanced at me. "I'll make sure it gets through."

Kit nodded, solemn, from the cot.

Then, as I was turning down the candle, he said, "Lord Thane?"

Severin paused.

"Can I ask you something?"

"You can ask."

Kit sat up a little in the blanket, with the watch's faint tick coming from under his pillow, and his face in the last of the candlelight was very small and very serious.

"Mama said the clock is hungry," said Kit. "Is that true?"

Severin stood in our doorway with the fog behind him and the dark stairwell below.

He didn't answer.

He looked at my brother for a long time, and then at me, and I felt his heartbeat through the thread go slow, and slower, and slow, like something in deep water sinking out of reach. And still he didn't answer.

That was how I knew.
