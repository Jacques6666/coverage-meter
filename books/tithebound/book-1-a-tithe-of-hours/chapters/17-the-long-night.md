# Chapter Seventeen

## The Long Night

The carriages go through the Wick.

I hadn't thought about that. There's only one road from the Hours Bridge to the Gilt, and it runs straight up through the middle of the river flats, wide and cobbled and lit with tall iron lamps every twenty paces: the Gilt Road. In the Wick, we call it the Long Way, because nobody's allowed to walk on it after dark unless they're carrying something for somebody who lives at the top. Every Long Night I'd ever known, I'd stood at the end of Tallow Lane with Kit on my hip, and then Kit at my side, and watched the carriages go up it in a line, black and gold and silver, the horses' breath smoking, the lamps inside showing the faces of the people going to Thane House to celebrate living forever.

Now I was inside one.

It was warm. That was the first thing. There was a little brazier set into the floor under a brass grille, and fur rugs on the seats, and the windows were glass, real glass, and the whole inside smelled of leather and cedar and something faintly sweet. I sat on one side with my hands in my lap and Celandine Penhallow's silk pooled around my feet. Severin sat on the other, with his back very straight and his gloved hands on his knees, looking out of the window.

Neither of us said anything for the length of the bridge.

When we came off it onto the cobbles of the Wick, I looked out of my window too. And there it was, all of it, sliding past in the dark beyond the glass. The end of Broker's Row, with Gannet's shop shuttered. The Candleworks chimneys, black against the clouds. The mouth of Candle Row, and the mouth of Tallow Lane after it, and people standing at the corners in the cold to watch the carriages go up, the way I always had, wrapped in shawls and old coats, their breath smoking. A woman with a baby on her hip. Two boys sharing one blanket. An old man holding up a lantern so the people in the carriages could see his face, and smiling, as if he thought someone might wave.

I looked for a small boy with a tin watch. I didn't see him. I hoped he was in bed with the blanket up to his chin and a man in the room over the pie shop watching his window.

"My grandmother will speak to you tonight," said Severin, without turning from the glass.

"I know."

"She'll be kind. She'll be charming. She'll ask about your brother and your dress and whether you're warm enough." His reflection in the dark window didn't move. "Don't believe any of it."

"I wasn't planning to."

"Don't eat anything she offers you. Don't drink anything she offers you. If she touches your arm—"

"I know, Severin."

He turned his head and looked at me then. In the light of the carriage lamp, his face was pale against the black velvet.

"I know you know," he said. "I'm saying it anyway."

The carriage began to climb. The cobbles changed under the wheels, the rough stones of the Wick giving way to something smooth and pale, and outside the window the dark tenements gave way to walls of white stone, and the walls gave way to gardens behind iron railings, glass houses glowing gold from inside, fountains frozen mid-leap and lit from beneath so that the ice shone like crystal. Up and up. Terrace after terrace.

"What happens," I said, "at the Long Night? Hollis said someone always falls in the fountain."

That almost got a smile. "Someone always does." He looked back at the window. "There's a toast, at the ninth bell. My grandmother gives it. Then there are gifts. The Long-Lived give each other years, in glass. It's considered elegant. Then there's dancing until dawn. The tithes stand at the wall. The wrights dance with each other."

"And you?"

"I don't dance."

"Never?"

"Never," said Severin.

---

Thane House stands at the very top of the Gilt, on the highest terrace, and it is black.

Every other house in the Gilt is white stone or pale gold. Thane House is black basalt, like the Meridian, like the stone of the island, and it rises out of the white terraces around it like a burnt tooth. It was lit from end to end. Every window blazed. Silver lamps on silver chains hung along the front of it, and silver lanterns lined the long white drive, and the snow on the lawns on either side had been raked into patterns, spirals and circles, like the faces of clocks.

Inside, it was warm enough to make my eyes sting.

I don't remember the hall, or the stairs, or the footmen who took our cloaks. I remember the ballroom. You walk into it through a pair of doors twice the height of a man, and the floor is black marble, and the walls are mirrors, so that the room goes on forever in every direction, and the ceiling is black glass set with hundreds of little silver lights in the shapes of the stars, so that you seem to be standing out of doors on the longest night of the year, under the whole sky.

And in the middle of it there was a fountain.

It was huge, a great round basin of black stone with a silver figure rising out of the center, a woman with her arms raised, and from her hands water poured down into the basin in long bright streams. But the water was gold. Not painted, not lit from below. *Gold.* It glowed the way a count glows. The way the vial on the end of Pruett's needle had glowed when it filled with my year.

All around the fountain, the Long-Lived were dipping crystal glasses into the basin, and lifting them up, and drinking.

"Years," said Severin quietly, at my shoulder. "From the Ledger's cellars. Bought on the Row, all year, and saved for tonight. They pour them into the water, a few hundred at a time. It's diluted. A glass is a week or two." He paused. "My grandmother calls it the Wellspring."

I couldn't look away.

A man near the basin who looked about thirty but moved like a very old man was holding his glass up to the light, admiring it. A woman beside him laughed and clinked her glass against his. "*Wick, eighteen years old,*" I heard her say. "*Very light. Very fresh.*"

Somewhere in that fountain, there were years that had come out of my arm onto Pruett's counter. Out of my arm in Saltmarket. Out of Bry's arm, maybe, into Lysander's jar, and from there to here. Out of Joss, out of Anni, out of every arm on Tallow Lane that had ever gone to the Row because the rent was due.

And they were drinking it. In crystal. To celebrate.

"Don't," said Severin, very low. I didn't know what he meant until I realized I'd taken a step toward the basin, and my hands were fists. His gloved fingers touched my elbow, just for a moment, just above the silver lace. "Not here. Not tonight. Stay where I can see you."

I unclenched my hands.

---

Lady Aurelia Thane received her guests at the far end of the ballroom, on a low dais under a canopy of black silk, with a silver box of candied violets on a little table at her elbow.

She was in black lace again, but tonight the lace was sewn with tiny silver beads that caught the starlight from the ceiling and threw it back, so that she seemed to be standing inside a small night of her own. On her honey-gold hair she wore a diadem made of silver clock hands, dozens of them, fanned out like the rays of a sun. Through the lace of her sleeves, her count blazed from wrist to shoulder.

She saw us coming the length of the room. Her face lit up. Both dimples.

"Darling," she said to Severin, holding out her hands, and he bent and kissed her cheek, and she patted his face. "You've had your hair cut. How handsome. You look exactly like your great-grandfather, and he was a terrible man." She turned the dimples on me. "And you've brought your little Wick girl. Oh, but look at her. Look at the dress."

I curtsied. Hollis had made me practice it a dozen times in front of Lord Featherstonehaugh.

"Celandine Penhallow's," said Lady Aurelia, softly. She reached out and touched the silver lace at my elbow with one fingertip, not quite touching my skin. "I remember it. The Ostranders' ball. Year 274. The swans got loose." Her brown eyes came up to mine, warm and gold-flecked and delighted. "She was such a pretty girl. She died that spring. Penhallows always do."

I didn't say anything. I couldn't think of anything to say that wouldn't get me killed.

"Are you warm enough, dear?" said Lady Aurelia. "And your brother? Is he well? I heard he had the most *generous* Grace."

"He's well, my lady."

"I'm so glad." She smiled. She took a violet from the silver box and put it between her lips. "Do enjoy yourself tonight. It's the longest night of the year. Anything can happen."

---

At the ninth bell, she gave the toast.

The whole ballroom turned toward the dais. Footmen moved through the crowd with trays of crystal glasses already filled from the fountain, glowing gold, and the wrights took them, and the Long-Lived took them, and the tithes standing along the mirrored walls in our grey took nothing, because nobody offered.

Lady Aurelia raised her glass.

"My dears," she said, and her warm voice carried to every corner of that enormous room without seeming to rise at all. "Another year. Another Long Night. And here we all are, still." Laughter. "I won't keep you. I'm far too old to make long speeches, and you're far too young to sit through them." More laughter. She lifted the glass higher, so that the gold in it caught the silver stars overhead. "To the longest night," said Lady Aurelia Thane. "And to the longest life."

"*The longest life*," said the whole ballroom, and drank.

Severin, beside me, lifted his glass with everyone else. He held it at his lips. And then he lowered it again, and set it down on a passing footman's tray, full, without a drop gone.

Across the room, I saw Lysander drain his in one swallow and hold it out for more.

---

The tithes stood at the wall. The wrights danced.

It was a strange dance, the one they did. Slow, with a lot of turning: the couples moved in two rings, one inside the other, going in opposite directions, and every so often the music paused and everyone stopped, and turned to face a new partner, and went on. Like the hands of a clock, Hollis had said. *It's called the Turning. It's terribly old and terribly dull, and if you put a foot wrong, you're talked about for a decade.* I watched him in the inner ring with a Vey girl in green, putting a foot wrong roughly every fourth beat and apologizing to her each time with such evident misery that she was laughing too hard to care.

Pell stood beside me at the wall, beaming at him.

Bry was further along, behind the Crane place. Lysander had danced twice already, golden and laughing, and both times he'd come back to stand in front of her and drink another glass from the fountain without looking at her once. She stood behind him with her thirty-one years bare on her arm and her chin up and her face like the side of a cliff, and every so often her hand went to the wall behind her as if to make sure it was still there.

Severin didn't dance. He stood at the edge of the floor near me, alone, with his hands behind his back, and watched the rings go round.

Then the music paused. The dancers stopped and turned. In the little gap of silence before it began again, Severin walked away from the edge of the floor.

Not toward the dancers. Toward the wall.

Toward me.

He stopped in front of me. All along the mirrored wall, the tithes in their grey went still, one after another, the way the Tithe Court had gone still. I saw Pell's mouth fall open. I saw, out of the corner of my eye, heads begin to turn on the dance floor, the Vey girl and Hollis and the couples beyond them, and on the dais at the end of the room, a small figure in black lace and a crown of silver clock hands pause with a violet halfway to her lips.

Severin held out his gloved hand.

"Dance with me," he said.

"You don't dance," I said. I could barely hear my own voice.

"I've changed my mind."

The music began again. Nobody on the floor moved. The musicians, up in their gallery, played on for a few uncertain bars and then faltered, and the silence came down over the ballroom like snow.

He was still holding out his hand. He didn't look around at any of them. He was looking at me.

*Stay where I can see you.*

I put my hand in his.

---

I didn't know the steps. It didn't matter. He did, and he led, and after the first turn the musicians remembered themselves and the music came back, and after the second turn the couples around us began to move again, slowly, staring, and after the third I stopped noticing any of them at all.

The thread was singing.

I don't know how else to say it. It hummed, at my wrist and at his, the way the air hums when a working is cast: a high sweet ringing like a fingertip on the rim of a glass. Nobody was casting. Nobody was drawing. It was just us, his hand at my waist and mine on his shoulder and our two wrists close together where our other hands were joined, and the thread between them ringing like a bell.

"Everyone's looking," I said.

"Yes."

"Lysander's looking."

"Lysander's always looking. Turn."

I turned. He turned me back. His hand at my waist was very steady.

"They're going to say—"

"They've already said it." His voice was low and close to my ear. "They've said everything they can think of. They've written songs. There's one that rhymes *Severin* with *never win*."

"That doesn't rhyme."

"I know. It's been keeping me awake." The corner of his mouth moved, and this time it stayed. "Turn."

I turned. Over his shoulder, in the endless mirrors, I saw us: a tall dark figure in black velvet and a small one in silver-grey silk, going round and round with the other couples on a black marble floor under a sky of silver stars, our reflections going on forever and forever into the glass.

I saw Lady Aurelia Thane on her dais, watching. Not smiling. Just watching, with a violet held between two fingers, quite still.

I looked away from her. I looked up at him.

"Why?" I said.

He knew what I meant. He was quiet for a whole turn.

"Because you were standing at the wall," said Severin, "in Celandine Penhallow's dress. And I didn't want anyone else to have you."

*Most things have two reasons,* he'd told Kit. *The second one's usually the true one.*

---

At the end of the dance he didn't let go of my hand. He drew me away from the floor, through the crowd that parted for us the way it parted for his grandmother, past the fountain with its golden water, to a pair of tall glass doors at the side of the ballroom. A footman opened them without being asked. And then we were outside, on a balcony of black stone hung out over the whole of Lanthorn, in the cold.

It was so cold it hurt. The air was perfectly still. Frost had grown in feathers along the stone balustrade, and our breath came out of us in white clouds and hung there in front of our faces.

And below us was the city.

The Gilt fell away beneath the balcony in terraces of white stone, lit window after lit window, gardens glowing gold. Below that, the dark of the Wick, a wide black spread of rooftops with only a few lights showing here and there, small and yellow and far apart, like the last coals in a grate. Below that, the Sallow, black and still, rimmed with ice. And in the middle of the river, the island, and on the island, the tower, and on the tower the great pale face of the Horologe, lit from beneath by lamps, its single iron hand pointing at eleven.

"I used to stand here when I was a boy," said Severin. He'd let go of my hand. He was standing at the balustrade with his gloved hands on the frost. "On the Long Night. When I was supposed to be asleep. I'd come out here and count the lights in the Wick."

I came and stood beside him.

"Every year," he said, "there were fewer."

I looked down at the dark. At the few small yellow lights. Somewhere down there was Tallow Lane, and a window that stuck, and a boy with a tin watch under his pillow.

"Severin," I said. "There's something I have to tell you."

He turned his head.

"Not here," I said. "Not tonight. After. When we're back. In the tower. I'll—" My voice wasn't working right. "I'll show you. Everything. I should have before. You showed me yours, and I lied to you, and I've hated myself for it every night since. And I'm going to stop."

He looked at me for a long moment in the starlight and the cold. His breath and mine hung white between us.

"Tell me after," he said.

He took off his right glove.

He did it slowly, finger by finger, the way he'd done it at the threading. Then he lifted his bare hand and laid it along the side of my face, his palm against my jaw, his fingers in my hair where Pell had pinned it, the healed scar across his palm warm against my cold skin. The thread at my wrist was ringing like a struck bell. I could feel his heartbeat in my wrist and in my face and in my teeth, so loud I couldn't hear the music anymore, couldn't hear anything.

He bent his head. I lifted mine. His forehead touched mine. Our breath was one cloud.

"Tamsin," he said, against my mouth.

And every candle in Thane House went out.

All of them. At once. The ballroom behind us, the windows below us, the silver lanterns down the long white drive. Every light on the highest terrace of the Gilt, gone, as if a giant hand had closed over them, and the only light left anywhere was the cold, far-off face of the clock across the river.

For a heartbeat, there was no sound at all.

Then, from the ballroom, the screaming began.
