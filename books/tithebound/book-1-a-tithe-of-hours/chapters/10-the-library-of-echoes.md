# Chapter Ten

## The Library of Echoes

By supper, the whole island knew.

By the next morning, so did the Gilt. Hollis said the story had crossed the Hours Bridge before the duel was even over, carried by a Lisle footman who'd run the whole quarter mile in his livery to be the first to tell it in the kitchens of the Long-Lived, and that by breakfast there wasn't a drawing room on the white terraces where somebody wasn't saying *the Thane heir* and *a first-year Crane* and *frozen like a fly in honey* in the same delighted breath.

"They're calling it the Ten Count," Hollis said. "Capital T, capital C. There's already a song. It's terrible. It rhymes *Severin* with *never win*."

"That doesn't rhyme."

"I *told* you it was terrible."

At the top table in the Refectory, Severin ate his meals alone, the empty places on either side of him wider than ever, and didn't seem to notice anybody whispering. He came to class. He cast when he was asked to, and I faked it, and nobody looked at my arm anymore because they were all too busy looking at him. He didn't send for me. He didn't speak to me. When I tried, once, in the corridor outside the Lantern Theatre, to say something, I didn't even know what, he walked past as if I were a draft from an open window.

Lysander was everywhere. Lysander was at every table, laughing. Lysander had been invited to dine at three different Houses in the Gilt in a week. Lysander had a new cuff, gold, with a crane picked out in rubies, and he wore his sleeve pushed up a fraction to show it.

And Lysander had started drawing on Bry.

Not in class. Never in class, not since Magister Vadra's quiet voice. He did it at night. He'd send for her at the tenth bell and she'd go up the tower and come back down after midnight grey to the lips, and in the morning at the weighing Steward Rook would hold her arm up to the lamp and say "Kettle" and then not say anything for a while before she read out the number.

Fifty-seven years, six months, after the Dial.

Fifty-five, the week after.

Fifty-one, the week after that.

"What's he *doing* with it?" I said to her, in the dark, after the third time. "There's no working that costs four years. Not in a night. Not unless he's stilling the whole Refectory."

"There's no working," said Bry. Her voice came out flat and tired from the next bed. "He doesn't cast. He just pulls. Takes my wrist and pulls, and holds it, and keeps it. Puts it in his own count." She was quiet for a moment. "He says he's *practicing*. Says he wants to get better at long draws. Smooth ones. All in one go." A pause. "He watches my face while he does it, Tam. He likes to see how long I can stay standing."

I lay there in the dark with my heart going cold.

*All in one go.*

---

"Absolutely not," I said.

"I haven't even said what it is yet," said Hollis.

"You're going to say you'll cast an Echo."

He opened his mouth. He closed it again. "Well," he said, "now that you mention it."

We were in the Library of Echoes, in the back bay where the old books lived, the one Hollis had shown me the first time. It was the study hour after the midday meal, when tithes were allowed to sit in the library if their wrights sent them for books, and Hollis had sent Pell for a dozen books at once and then told him to take a very long time about it. There were four of us around a reading table in the dusty half-light: me, Hollis, and Bry, who'd come with a slip from Lysander for some book on Gilt genealogy and simply not gone back. Pell was keeping watch at the corner, pretending to read a treatise on horology upside down.

"An Echo at the sluice grate," said Hollis, in a rush, as though if he said it fast enough nobody could stop him. "Or the cistern, wherever they put him in. We'd see it, Tam. We'd see exactly what happened that night. Who brought him. Who held the thread. Faces and everything."

"How much does it cost?"

He hesitated.

"*Hollis.*"

"A night's worth of Echo, at that distance in time, maybe a month," he admitted. "Two, if the water muddies it. Water's terrible for Echoes. Everything goes wobbly."

"You've got six years."

"Six years and two months. I can spare two months. I wasn't doing anything with them." He spread his hands. "What was I going to do, learn the harp?"

"No," said Bry.

Hollis turned to her, surprised.

"No," said Bry again. She had her arms folded on the table and her chin on her arms, and the bruise on her jaw had gone yellow-green at the edges. Her count lay bare on the table in front of her: fifty-one years. "You heard her, Penhallow. You've got six. I'd sooner you didn't spend a minute of it on Joss Mallory. Joss wouldn't want you to either. Joss'd say, *burn me, mate, buy yourself a pie*."

Hollis looked at her for a long moment. Something moved in his face that I didn't expect, something soft and startled, as though nobody had ever said *no* to him for his own sake before.

"Then what do we do?" he said.

"We use what's written down," I said. "If somebody's killing tithes, somebody's writing it down. That's what the Gilt does. They write everything down. The Ledger had Joss's sale in it before he was cold." I looked at Bry. "You said once your cousin works on the island."

Bry lifted her chin off her arms. A slow smile spread across her face, the one she used to get before a fight she knew she'd win.

"Wat," she said.

"Who's Wat?" said Hollis.

"Wat Sorrel. My mam's sister's boy. Skinny little weasel, nose like a hook, can write faster than you can talk." Bry sat up. "He's a copy-boy in the Ledger annex. Down by the gatehouse, the long building with the green door. That's where all the weighing books go every Seventhday, every Steward's book on the island, and Wat and five other weasels copy them out fair to send up the hill to the big Ledger." Her smile widened. "He'd sell his own grandmother for a lemon cake."

At the corner of the bay, Pell turned around, holding the horology treatise the right way up for the first time.

"I could make a lemon cake," he said.

---

Wat Sorrel came to the library the next day at the end of study hour, and he looked exactly like Bry had said he would, which was like a weasel that had been taught to wear a waistcoat. He was perhaps sixteen. He had inky fingers and a long nose and nervous eyes that went everywhere at once, and he wouldn't sit down. He stood at the end of our table with his hands in his pockets and his shoulders up around his ears.

"Cake first," he said.

Pell put it on the table, wrapped in a cloth. It smelled so good that even Hollis, who'd grown up in the Gilt on sugar and cream, closed his eyes.

Wat unwrapped one corner, looked at it, sniffed it, and wrapped it back up and tucked it under his arm. Then he took a fold of papers out of the inside of his waistcoat and put it on the table.

"If anyone asks," he said, "I was never here, and that cake was for my mam."

"Wat," said Bry.

"And I didn't copy anything. I don't know how to copy. I can't even read."

"*Wat.*"

"What?"

"Sit down and show us."

He sat down.

He'd done what I'd asked, through Bry, the day before: gone back through the annex's fair copies of the Tithe Hall's weighing books, as far as they went, and written out every tithe who hadn't finished their term. Not the ones who'd gone home short with nine years and a coat; that was most of them, every year. The other ones. The ones who'd gone home in boxes.

It took him three pages.

He'd made a little table, in tiny neat copperplate that must have taken him half the night: name, year, House, count at the Market, date, and the cause the Steward had written in the book.

I read it once straight through. Then again, slowly, with my finger on each line, the way I'd check a broker's sums.

*Hen Pallister. Three years past. House Vey. Sixty-four years. Twelfth of Mistmonth. Fell from the Hours Bridge, intoxicated.*

Kit had been right.

*Mira Holt. Four years past. House Marchbank. Sixty-eight years. Second of Frostmonth. Sold in full. Drowned.*

*Teodor Brack. Six years past. House Lisle. Sixty-two years. Ninth of Leaffall. Sold in full. Drowned.*

*Saskia Venn. Seven years past. House Crane. Seventy years. Fourth of Frostmonth. Fever.*

Seventy years. A Wick girl with seventy years. I'd never heard of her. I'd have been twelve, working Gannet's counter, and I'd never heard her name.

There were eleven of them. Joss, at the bottom, in fresh ink. Eleven tithes in ten years who'd come over the Hours Bridge with their sleeves rolled and their counts glowing and never gone home.

"There's a twelfth," said Wat, and pointed. "Last year. But he's different."

*Orrin Fisk. One year past. House Thane. Fifty-three years. Fourteenth of Deepwinter. Fever.*

Hollis leaned over my shoulder. Bry leaned in from the other side.

"Fifty-three," I said slowly.

"So?" said Wat.

"So he doesn't fit." I ran my finger back up the list. "Look at the counts. Sixty-four. Sixty-eight. Sixty-two. Seventy. Sixty-one. Sixty-six. Every single one of them came to the Market with more than sixty years. Every one except Orrin Fisk." I looked at Hollis. "Severin's tithe isn't like the others."

Hollis let out a long breath, as if he'd been holding it for weeks.

"And look at the dates," I said.

They looked.

"They're all in the first term," said Bry. "Every one. Before the Long Night. Nobody dies after midwinter."

"Orrin did," said Hollis quietly. "Fourteenth of Deepwinter. That's after."

Another thing that didn't fit.

"And there's nothing before ten years ago." I turned back to Wat. "Did you go further back? Before this?"

"Course I did. Twenty years. I'm thorough." Wat looked offended. "Before ten years ago, nothing. Tithes went home short, tithes went home with a coat, one went home with a broken leg after she fell down the tower stairs. But nobody went home in a box. Not for sale, not for drowning, not for *fever*." He sniffed. "Then, ten years ago, the first one. And one a year since, near enough. Sometimes two."

I looked at the top line of his table. The first name.

*Corin Ashe. Ten years past. House Ostrander. Sixty-five years. Nineteenth of Leaffall. Sold in full. Drowned.*

Leaffall, ten years ago.

The year Kit was born. The year our mother kissed me on the forehead in the middle of a February night and told me to mind the baby and walked out into the fog. Eight months later, the first autumn after, a tithe named Corin Ashe with sixty-five years on his arm had gone into the Sallow too. And before that, in twenty years of weighing books, nobody.

The night she left was the night my wrist burned.

The night my count stopped.

I didn't say anything. I sat there with my finger on the first name and the library going on around me, quiet and dusty, the echoes in their brass frames turning their little ghost-plays over and over on the walls, and I thought: *it's a coincidence*. I thought: *a lot of things happen in a winter*. I thought: *you can't afford this one, Tam, you've never been able to afford this one, put it away.*

I couldn't put it away.

"One more thing," said Wat, and lowered his voice, and everybody leaned in. "The sales. The ones that say *sold in full*. They all have a broker's mark in the copy book, see, where it says who bought it. Name's withheld, under the Privilege. They give 'em numbers instead." He tapped the paper. "Every sale on this list. Every single one. Same number."

"What number?" said Hollis.

"Seven," said Wat Sorrel. "Broker Seven. And there isn't a Broker Seven. Not on the Row. Not in Saltmarket. I've copied every broker's book in this city for two years and I've never once seen that mark anywhere else." He pushed back his chair. "That's all. That's the lot. I'm going now and I was never here and if any of you ever says my name to anyone I'll tell my mam it was Bry who stole her best brooch when we were nine."

"It *was* me who stole it," said Bry.

"I know. That's why it'll work." He put the cake more firmly under his arm, and looked at Pell. "It's very good cake," he said, a little grudgingly, and went.

---

For a while, none of us said anything.

Pell came back from his post at the corner and sat down very carefully on the end of the bench, which creaked. Hollis had picked up the second page of Wat's list and was reading it again with his lips moving. Bry was staring at the wall.

"Long counts," she said at last.

"Long counts," I said.

"Somebody's picking them out." Bry's voice was low and flat. "Every year. At the Market. Standing up there in the gallery with the Long-Lived, eating their bloody sweets, looking down the line. Sixty, seventy. *That one.* And then sometime before midwinter, they come for them in the night." She swallowed. "Why? What do they want? You can't do anything with sixty-five years you can't do with fifty. Not if you're just going to drink it."

"Maybe they're not drinking it," said Hollis slowly. "Maybe they're looking for something."

We all looked at him.

He went a little pink. "Well. I mean. If I wanted years, I'd just take years. I'd buy them. I'm a Penhallow, I can't, but if I were a Crane I'd buy them, everybody does, there's a whole Row for it. You don't need to murder anyone for years in Lanthorn. It's the one thing you never need to murder anyone for." He put the page down. "But if I wanted *one particular thing*, and I didn't know who had it, and it only showed up in long counts—"

"Then you'd test them," I said.

My voice sounded very far away.

"You'd test them," said Hollis. "One by one. All the long ones. And you'd pull out everything they had, all at once, to see what happened."

Nobody said anything.

"What happens?" said Pell, in his slow gentle voice. "When you pull out everything?"

"You die," said Bry. "Usually."

*Usually.*

I sat there in the dusty light with my sleeve pulled down to the wrist, and my hands flat on Wat's three pages, and under the wool, under the ink, ten bright years lay hidden in the dark like coins at the bottom of a well.

"Anni," said Pell suddenly.

We all turned.

"Anni's sixty-three," he said. "Now that Joss is gone. She's the longest in the Hall." His big face had gone pale. "Isn't she?"

Hollis looked at the list. Bry looked at the list. Then they both looked at me.

"Yes," said Bry. "Anni's the longest now."

*No,* I thought. *She isn't.*

Somebody was hunting long counts. Somebody had been hunting them for ten years, ever since the winter my mother went into the river. And the longest count in the Tithe Hall wasn't Anni Rusk's sixty-three.

It was mine.
