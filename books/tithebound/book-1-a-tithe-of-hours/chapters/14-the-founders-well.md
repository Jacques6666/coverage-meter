# Chapter Fourteen

## The Founders' Well

On the third night after Anni, we held a vigil.

It was Bry's idea. In the Wick, when somebody goes grey, the whole street lights a candle in the window and leaves it burning till it gutters, so the dead can find their way down to the river in the dark. We didn't have windows, not ones that looked onto anything but water. We didn't have candles either, not to spare. But Pell had a way with the kitchens, and he came back from his errands that afternoon with his pockets full of tallow stubs, the ends of the Refectory candles that the servants scraped off the holders every morning and threw in a bucket for the chandlers.

We set them on the stone floor of the Hall after the eleventh bell, in a line down the aisle between the beds. Two rows. One for Joss and one for Anni. Eleven stubs in each, one from each of us, and Steward Rook in her chair by the iron door, watching with her good eye, and not saying a word against it.

When they were all lit, Pell sang.

I hadn't known he could. His voice was like the rest of him, enormous and gentle, and it filled the whole vaulted room the way the alarm bell had filled the tower. He sang the hymn Anni had always hummed, the one about the lamb, and he knew all the words, and he sang every verse. Halfway through, Rook got up out of her chair and came down the aisle and stood at the end of the row of candles with her hands folded in front of her, and when he finished, she said, "Amen," in a voice like gravel, and went back to her chair.

Nobody said anything after that. We sat on our beds and watched the stubs burn down, one by one, until the Hall was dark again except for the gold of eleven counts under eleven blankets.

Joss's boots were still under his bed. Somebody had put Anni's blanket on hers, folded square.

---

Severin didn't send for me.

Not that night, not the next. On the second evening, when Rook came down at the ninth bell, I was already sitting on the end of my bed with my boots on. She looked at me and shook her head.

"He says no lessons this week."

"Why?"

"He didn't say why, and I didn't ask him." Her milky eye roved over my face. "Take your boots off, Fenn. You're making the others nervous."

I took my boots off.

In class, he cast when he was asked to, and I faked it, and he didn't look at me. In the corridors, the Gilt watched us both, and whispered, and Lysander laughed. Through the thread, his heartbeat stayed slow and even and far away.

I told myself it was the gossip. That he was keeping his distance so they'd have nothing new to say. That it was sensible. That he was protecting me.

I lay awake every night anyway with my hand over my wrist, listening for something that didn't come.

---

The note came on the fifth day, folded on my pillow after breakfast. Not his handwriting. A woman's, slanting and quick.

*Magister Vadra requires Miss Fenn in her study at the fourth bell. East wing, the green door.*

"What does *she* want?" said Bry, reading it over my shoulder.

"I don't know."

"She's a Vey."

"She's a Wick girl who married a Vey."

"Same thing, once they've got their feet under the table." Bry handed it back to me. "Watch your arm, Tam."

---

The green door was at the end of a long corridor on the second floor of the east wing, past the Lantern Theatre, where the windows looked out over the river toward the Wick. I knocked, and a voice said *come*, and I went in.

It wasn't what I expected. I'd expected something like Severin's room, cold and strange, or like the magisters' rooms I'd glimpsed through open doors on the way past, all dark wood and leather chairs and portraits of the Long-Lived glaring down from the walls. Magister Vadra's study was small and bright and green. There were plants on every windowsill, pots of thyme and rosemary and mint, and a little lemon tree in a tub in the corner with three hard yellow lemons on it. There were books everywhere, stacked on the floor and the chairs. On the wall over the desk hung a big map of Lanthorn, the whole city, with pins stuck in it here and there, mostly in the Wick, with threads of colored wool stretched between some of them.

And on a spirit lamp on the end of the desk, there was a kettle. A battered tin kettle with a dented spout and a wooden handle wrapped in string, exactly like the one Mrs. Achterberg had across our landing. A Wick kettle. It was just starting to sing.

"Sit," said Magister Vadra.

She was standing at the window with her back to the room, the way Severin had stood the first night of lessons. She didn't turn around. I moved a stack of books off the only chair and sat on it.

She made tea. She did it slowly, with her back still mostly to me, warming the pot first, the way my mother used to. When it was done she poured two cups and brought one to me, and I took it because I didn't know how not to.

Then she sat down behind the desk, and folded her hands on the blotter, and looked at me.

"I know about the ink," she said.

I put the cup down on my knee very carefully. "What ink, Magister?"

"Tallow ink. Gannet's, I'd guess, from the way you feather the ends. Thin coats, three or four. Laid over the top of your count, from about the sixty-second line to somewhere near the elbow." Her long severe face didn't change at all. "And ash and spit at the tip, with a thumb, to fake the days, when your wright casts."

The tea in my cup had started to shake. I put both hands around it.

"You did it at the First Draw," said Magister Vadra. "And at every class since. You're very good. You're better than I was."

I looked up.

She didn't say anything. She just reached for the cuff of her long green sleeve, the one that always came down over her knuckles, and she pushed it back, slowly, up past the wrist and the forearm to the elbow, and laid her bare arm on the desk between us.

Her count was ordinary. That was the first thing I saw. Thirty-one lines, a little row of notches at the wrist, nothing to make anybody look twice. A good Gilt count, the kind you'd have if you'd been born short and married well.

The second thing I saw was the scars.

They ran up the inside of her arm under the gold lines, crossing them, so many I couldn't count them. Fine white rings around the wrist, one above another, a dozen of them at least. And above those, all the way up the forearm, a lattice of thin pale lines, like the marks a cobweb would leave if it could cut.

"Black thread," said Magister Vadra. "The rings are tethers. Six of them before I was ten. My mother sold my years to the brokers on the Row by the season, a few at a time, whenever the rent was due. It's not legal to thread a child. That's what black thread's for." She touched one of the white rings with a fingertip. "And these, above, are from the ink. If you lay it every day for two years, the skin remembers. Even after it's gone."

I couldn't say anything.

"I was a tithe in this Meridian thirty-one years ago," she said. "The same term as Agathe Rook. She was Lisle's. I was Vey's. Bought for eight." She pulled her sleeve back down, slowly, over the scars and the count, until it covered her knuckles again. "My wright was a boy called Aurel Vey. He was kind, and frightened, and he didn't want to draw. Not on me, not on anyone. His father would have thrashed him for it. So I inked, every day, for two years, so that every Seventhday his father would read the Steward's book and see his son's tithe going down." She paused. "And then I married him."

I stared at her.

"The Gilt doesn't marry its batteries," said Magister Vadra. "So they tell you. They're mostly right." Something moved at the corner of her mouth. "Aurel died eleven years ago. The Vey family let me keep the name, and the years he left me, and this post, because the alternative was a scandal. I've been teaching Tethering ever since to the children of the people who'd have hanged me." She picked up her own cup. "So you see, Miss Fenn, when I watch a Wick girl stand at the First Draw with her hand clamped to her wrist and her thumb going, I know exactly what I'm looking at."

---

"Are you going to report me?" I said.

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I don't care why the Thane heir doesn't draw on you." She drank her tea. "I care that someone else will notice he doesn't. Sooner or later. And then you'll both be in a great deal more trouble than either of you understand."

"Who?"

She looked at me over the rim of her cup. "Who reads every weighing book on this island, Miss Fenn? Every Seventhday, every Hall, every tithe, every count?"

I knew. I'd known since the Refectory. Since a small cold hand on my wrist and a thumb brushing once across my ink.

"Lady Thane," I said.

"Lady Thane has read every weighing book in the Ledger annex every week for ten years. Before that, she never bothered. She left it to the clerks." Vadra set her cup down. "Ten years ago, something happened. I don't know what. Nobody I know knows what. But something went wrong for her, ten years ago, and since then, she's been looking for something. Reading the books. Watching the Market from the gallery. Looking at the counts."

"And the tithes who die."

Vadra didn't answer that. She didn't have to.

"What's she looking for?" I said.

Magister Vadra was quiet for a long moment. Then she stood up, and went to a tall cabinet against the wall behind the desk, and took a small key out of the bodice of her gown, on a chain, and unlocked it.

Inside, on a shelf among a lot of other papers, there was a thin folio bound in faded red cloth. She took it out and brought it back to the desk and opened it flat in front of me.

It was an engraving. Black and white, very fine, the lines so small and close that it looked almost like a drawing in smoke. And I knew the style before I'd even looked properly, because I'd seen another one like it a few weeks ago in the back bay of the Library of Echoes, with Hollis leaning over my shoulder.

*The Drowning of the Wells. After the fresco in the Ledger House.*

"The Drowning fresco in the Ledger House has two panels," said Magister Vadra. "Everyone in Lanthorn knows the first. The Founders on the bridge. The wells in the river. *Never again the well,* carved over the door in gold." She laid one finger on the edge of the page. "The second panel was plastered over in the Year 80 After the Drowning, on the orders of the Keeper of the Ledger at the time. Before it was covered, a copyist named Ilse Marrow was sent to make a record of it for the Ledger's archive. She made two. She gave one to the archive and kept one." A pause. "She was hanged a month later for an unrelated matter. This is hers."

I looked down.

It wasn't the river. It wasn't the bridge.

It was a room. A vaulted chamber, deep, dark, the stone walls dripping. In the middle of it, hanging down from somewhere far above the top of the picture, there was an enormous pendulum, a disc of iron as big as a cartwheel on a shaft as thick as a tree, motionless. I knew that pendulum. I'd seen it every night for six weeks through the grate in Severin's ceiling, hanging still in the dark above the dead gears.

And under the pendulum there was a woman.

She was chained. Not with iron. With thread. Dozens of fine silver threads, drawn in the tiniest lines, running from her wrists and her ankles and her throat up into the dark, up past the pendulum, up to wherever the top of the picture ended. She knelt on the stone floor with her arms held out and up by the threads, like a puppet whose strings had been pulled too tight. Her head was bowed. Her hair hung over her face.

And her arms were covered in light.

The engraver had done it by leaving the paper white. Her arms, from wrist to shoulder, were a blaze of white lines, so many and so close that there was no ink between them at all, and the whiteness ran up over her shoulders and down her back and spilled off her onto the floor around her like water.

Around her, in a ring, knelt men and women in long robes. Each of them had a silver thread running from their own wrist to hers. Each of them had their face turned up to her, mouth open.

Drinking.

Underneath, in tiny old-fashioned copperplate: *The Founders' Well.*

---

I don't know how long I sat there.

"That isn't a drowning," I said at last. My voice sounded very far away.

"No," said Magister Vadra.

"It's a well."

"Yes."

"They didn't drown them." I couldn't take my eyes off the woman under the pendulum. "Or they didn't drown *her*. They kept her. Under the clock."

"There are people in the Wick," said Magister Vadra carefully, "who believe that not every wellspring went into the river on the night of the Drowning. That the Founders kept one, or several, for themselves. That the long lives of the Gilt don't come only from the years they buy on the Row." She closed the folio, gently, and put her hand flat on top of it. "There are people in the Wick who believe a great many things. Most of them are wrong. Some of them get hanged for believing them, like Ilse Marrow." She looked at me. "I'm not telling you this so you'll believe anything, Miss Fenn. I'm telling you so you'll understand what Lady Thane might be looking for, when she looks at the counts of Wick girls who come to the Market with seventy years on their arms."

My whole body had gone cold.

"Sixty-one," I said. "I came with sixty-one."

"Of course you did." She didn't blink. "And if you ever need to disappear, there's a woman at the end of Broker's Row who sells ink. She knows people who know how to get a girl out of Lanthorn without a thread on her wrist." She stood, and took the folio, and turned toward the cabinet. "But I'd pray, if I were you, that you're not what Lady Thane has been looking for. Because for ten years, she hasn't found it. And she isn't the kind of woman who gets less hungry with waiting."

"Wait."

She stopped with her hand on the cabinet door.

"Let me see it," I said. "Again. Please. Just for a moment."

She looked at me. Then, without a word, she came back and laid the folio on the desk and opened it again.

I bent over the woman under the pendulum.

I hadn't been looking at her face; you couldn't see her face, it was hidden behind her hair. I'd been looking at the threads, and the light, and the ring of robed figures drinking. But there was something that had caught at me the first time, a snag at the corner of my mind, and I hadn't known what it was.

Her hands.

They were held up and out by the silver threads, open, palms upward, as if she were offering something. The engraver had drawn them very carefully. Long fingers. Fine bones. And on the left hand, the little finger was bent. Crooked at the middle joint, sideways, at an angle, the way a finger sets when it's been broken and nobody's splinted it right.

I'd seen that finger trace circles in the air at the end of my bed, in the dark. *The clock has a heart, Tamsin, way down in the dark. And it's always hungry.*

The engraving was two hundred and twenty years old. The woman in it had been chained under the Horologe three hundred years ago. She couldn't be anybody I'd ever known.

But she had my mother's hands.
