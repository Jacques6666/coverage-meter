# Chapter Twenty-Six

## Echo

We went down the next night, at the twelfth bell.

Steward Rook was waiting for us at the far end of the Tithe Hall, by the small iron door. She was standing, not sitting, with her lamp in one hand and her great ring of keys in the other, and the bar was already out of its brackets and leaning against the wall. A new chain hung loose through the handle, unlocked.

She looked at the five of us coming down the aisle in the dark between the sleeping beds: me, and Bry, and Hollis in a borrowed grey jacket with the echo-lens wrapped in a blanket under his arm, and Severin in his plain Wick coat with a lamp. And Pell at the back, who was coming as far as the top of the stairs and no further, and had sworn it on Anni's hymn.

Rook's good eye went from face to face. It stopped on Severin.

For a long moment, neither of them said anything.

"Orrin Fisk," said Rook.

Severin went still.

"Calder Bay," said Rook. "A baby called Wren." Her voice was like gravel. "Penhallow told me. This afternoon. In my office. He wouldn't leave till I'd listened." Her milky eye moved over Severin's face. "I packed that boy into a box with my own hands, Thane. I washed him. I combed his hair. I've hated you for a year."

"I know," said Severin quietly.

"You could have told me."

"If I'd told you, you'd have known. And if you'd known, she could have found out from you."

Rook looked at him for a long time. Then she turned and opened the iron door herself, wide, onto the dark stairs and the cold and the smell of deep water.

"I never saw any of this," said Steward Rook. "Go on. Mind the fifty-first step; it's cracked." As Severin passed her, she said, without looking at him: "A baby called Wren. Burn me."

---

Severin had the lamp, but I didn't need it. I went first, with my sleeve pushed up to the shoulder.

There was no ink on my arm. Not a coat, not a smear. I'd scrubbed off the last of it in the washroom that evening, with Bry standing guard at the door, and I hadn't laid any new. Vadra's pot sat on the shelf by my bed with the wax still on it. Seventy-one years and four months, bare from the wrist to the elbow, and it lit the cistern like the open door of a furnace.

Behind me, I heard Hollis breathe in sharply as the forest of pillars came up out of the dark around us.

"Saints," he whispered. "I didn't see it, last time. I was too busy drowning."

We waded. Severin went beside me with the lamp held high. Hollis went behind him with the lens on his head, wrapped in its blanket, held there with both hands like a woman carrying washing from the river. Bry went last, and kept her eyes on the dark between the pillars the whole way, and I knew she was watching for a golden head.

Nobody came.

The iron door with the clock faces was standing open a finger's width, the way I'd left it. The steps. The corridor. The torch in its bracket had burned out; Severin lit it again from the lamp. The corner. The second corner.

The little square room.

It was exactly as we'd left it. The iron rings in the walls. The stone bench. The cuffs hanging open on their short chain from the ring where Bry had been. And on the floor beside them, where I'd dropped it, the broker's needle, black, as long as my hand.

Bry stopped in the entrance. She stood there for a moment with her arms folded and her jaw set and looked at the ring, and the cuffs, and the place on the floor where she'd sat. Then she walked across the room and sat down on the stone bench with her back to the wall, and folded her arms tighter, and said, "Go on, then, Penhallow. Before I change my mind about watching."

---

Hollis unwrapped the lens.

He held it in both hands, the thick clear disc of glass in its plain brass frame, and stood in the middle of the room and turned slowly in a circle, the way Vadra had taught him that afternoon. Looking at the walls. The ring. The bench. The gap in the far wall where the passage went on, deeper, into the dark.

"Two weeks back," he said. "Eleventh bell to the second, near enough. Through all this stone." He looked down at his tin cuff. "Right."

"Hollis," I said.

"Don't."

"I wasn't going to." I came and stood in front of him. "I was going to say thank you."

He looked at me. Then his long freckled face did the thing it did, the joke coming up over the top of whatever was underneath, and he said, "Don't thank me yet. I've never cast anything bigger than a candle. I might Echo the wrong night entirely and we'll all have to stand here watching a rat eat a biscuit."

"Penhallow," said Severin.

"Yes, yes. I know." Hollis took a breath. He pushed his tin cuff up his forearm, off his count, and handed it to me without looking, and I held it. Six years. Two months. A short ladder of gold on a long pale arm.

He lifted the lens in front of him at arm's length, and closed his eyes, and cast.

I'd watched a lot of workings by then. I'd felt Severin's come up out of his wrist like a river. This was different. This was small and careful and slow, a thin line of gold drawn up out of Hollis's arm, out of the top of his short count, and into his hands, and into the glass. The air didn't ring like a struck bell. It hummed, low, like a held note on a cello. And the lens began to glow.

And the room filled with ghosts.

---

They came up out of the stone like smoke. Faint at first, and then clearer, small and pale and faintly gold, like the figures in the Library of Echoes. Silent. Every one of them silent.

Two figures, in the entrance to the room. A tall golden one, and a stumbling one in grey, being pulled by the wrist.

Lysander. I'd have known him in any light, in any smoke. The way he moved. The tilt of his head. He dragged the figure in grey across the room to the ring on the wall, and she fought him; I watched her fight him, the ghost of Bry kicking and twisting the way she'd taught me when we were eleven. And he laughed. I couldn't hear it. I could see it, his golden head thrown back, his mouth open. He forced her down against the wall and lifted her wrists to the ring and put the cuffs on, and drove the pins through the hasps.

On the stone bench, the real Bry had gone very still with her arms locked around herself. She didn't look away.

And then the third figure.

She'd been there the whole time. I hadn't seen her at first, because she was standing so still in the gap of the far wall, by the passage that went on into the dark, and because she was so small. A slight shape in black, with a cloud of pale hair pinned up loosely, as if she'd done it herself in a hurry and liked the effect. In her left hand, a small box. As I watched, she lifted her right hand from the box to her mouth.

Behind me, Severin made a sound I'd never heard him make.

On the floor, the ghost-Lysander had the black thread out. He knelt beside the ghost-Bry and threaded her, rough and quick, with the long black needle, and tied off the knot. Small. Flat. A double loop with the tail tucked under. Then he took her wrist in his hand and drew.

I watched it come out of her. A rope of gold, out of her arm and into his hand, and her count going out, rung by rung, from the elbow down. I watched the ghost-Bry's head fall forward onto her chest.

And the small figure in black came away from the passage and crossed the room, unhurried, eating, and stood beside Lysander, and watched.

Her lips moved. Silently. Lysander looked up at her and answered. She laughed; I saw her shoulders shake, prettily. She put another violet in her mouth.

Then she bent down beside the drained ghost of Bry, and reached out one small hand, and touched her face, the way she'd touched Anni's cheek in the Refectory. Like you'd touch a kitten.

And in bending down, she turned her face to the torchlight.

Round. Sweet. Two dimples. Her honey-gold hair coming loose from its pins. And around her throat, catching the ghost-light from the torch, a silver chain, and on it a pendant shaped like a clock hand.

Lady Aurelia Thane. Keeper of the Ledger. Clear as day, in gold smoke, in the lens in Hollis's shaking hands.

She straightened up. She said something else to Lysander, and he nodded. He cut the black thread with a little silver knife and dropped the needle on the stone beside Bry's hand, carefully, where it could be found. And then the two of them turned and walked away together, side by side, through the gap in the far wall, into the passage, into the dark. Deeper.

The ghosts faded.

The lens in Hollis's hands went on glowing, very faintly, like a coal. And inside it, in the depths of the glass, very small, the whole thing had begun again from the beginning. Two figures in the entrance. A tall golden one. A stumbling one in grey.

Hollis's knees went.

Severin caught him before he hit the floor. Got an arm around him and held him up, and Hollis hung there with his head down, gasping, the lens still clutched against his chest.

"Hollis." I had his cuff in one hand. I grabbed his arm with the other and turned it to the torchlight. Six years. A notch and a half. "Hollis, you said a month and a half—"

"It went a bit over," he said into Severin's coat. "Water's terrible for Echoes. Everything goes wobbly." He lifted his head. His face was grey and wet, and he was smiling. "But we got it. Didn't we? Tell me we got it. Tell me it wasn't a rat with a biscuit."

"We got it," said Severin quietly. He was looking at the lens. At the small figure in black going round and round inside it, eating violets. His face had no color in it at all. "We got her."

On the stone bench, Bry put her face in her hands.

---

I helped Hollis sit down on the bench beside Bry. Bry put her arm around him without looking up, and he leaned against her, and they sat there together, the two of them, with the lens between them on the stone. I put his tin cuff back on his arm, over his six years, and closed the clasp.

And the thread at my wrist pulled.

It had been pulling the whole time. Since the cistern. Since the clock-faced door. Warm, and steady, like a hand around my wrist, toward the gap in the far wall. Toward the passage. Toward where Aurelia and Lysander had gone, in the ghost-light, side by side. Deeper.

And under the floor, the beat.

It was louder here than it had ever been. So deep and so slow that it didn't feel like a sound. It felt like the stone breathing.

I walked to the gap in the wall.

"Tamsin." Severin was beside me. He had the lamp. He didn't tell me not to go. He didn't say *not that way*. He just came, half a step behind me, with the light held up so I could see.

The passage sloped down. The walls changed as we went: the rough black rock with the tool marks gave way to dressed stone, smooth and fitted, and then to carved stone, and the carvings were clock faces again, like the iron door, dozens of them, overlapping, cut into the walls on both sides and into the arched ceiling overhead, every one with a single hand, every hand on eleven. The air got warmer. Not colder. Warmer, the deeper we went, like walking toward a fire.

"We're under the tower," said Severin, very low. "Directly under. The pendulum shaft goes down through the rock from my room. I've always wondered where it ended." He swallowed. "I think it ends here."

The passage ended.

Not in a room. In a door.

It filled the whole end of the passage, from wall to wall and floor to arched ceiling. It wasn't iron. It wasn't wood. It was silver. Solid silver, smooth and seamless, without a hinge or a handle or a lock or a single mark on it anywhere, so bright in the lamplight and the light from my arm that it looked like a wall of still water standing on end.

And it was singing.

That's the only word for it. The air in front of it hummed, high and sweet and endless, like a fingertip run around the rim of a glass. Like a working that had been cast a very long time ago and never stopped. I could feel it on my skin. On my teeth. In the thread at my wrist, which had gone so warm it was almost hot, and was pulling me forward, toward the silver, so hard I had to lean back against it.

Severin put his hand on my shoulder. I could feel him shaking.

"Tamsin," he said. "Wait."

I didn't wait.

I put my left hand flat on the silver door.

It was warm. Warm as skin. And under my palm, through the metal, through whatever was on the other side of it, I felt something.

A heartbeat.

Slow. Very slow. A long, deep beat, and then nothing for a long time, and then another. The beat I'd felt coming up through the stone in the tunnel, and the cistern, and the little square room. But not through stone now. Through my hand. Right there, on the other side of the silver.

I'd felt Severin's heartbeat under mine for three months. Two drums, close together, one slow and one fast, never quite in time.

This was not like that.

Under my palm, the slow beat changed. It came again, sooner. And again, sooner still. Quickening, beat by beat, the way a sleeper's heart quickens when they hear their name called from a long way off, until it was keeping pace with mine. And then it caught up.

The same beat. The same instant. As if my own heart were being struck, very softly, from the other side of the door.
