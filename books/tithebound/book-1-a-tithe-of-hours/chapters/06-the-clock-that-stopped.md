# Chapter Six

## The Clock That Stopped

Classes went on.

That was the thing I couldn't get over. By the second bell the basket was gone, the flagstones under the clock had been sluiced down with buckets until they shone, and the wrights had gone back up to their rooms to dress and come down again in their sashes, and the Meridian took up its day exactly where it had left off, like a clock that had been jostled and kept ticking. A boy was dead. The Ledger considered the matter closed. There was a lecture on the Theory of Hours at nine.

Severin didn't send for me. When Steward Rook unlocked the Hall after breakfast, there was a folded square of paper waiting on my pillow, the handwriting small and upright and very black.

*Fenn. The Library of Echoes. These, to my rooms by the sixth bell.* And under it, a list of four titles, each one longer and duller than the last. *On the Allotment of Counts. A Treatise on the Mechanisms of the Great Horologe. The Ledger Statutes, Annotated. Lanthorn Before the Drowning.*

No signature. He didn't need one.

"What's it say?" said Bry from her bed. She hadn't gotten up. She was lying on her back again, staring at the ceiling, with one arm over her eyes.

"He wants books."

"Books," said Bry, as though it were a word in another language. "A boy's dead and he wants books."

I folded the note and put it in my pocket, next to Kit's map.

---

The Library of Echoes ran the whole length of the Meridian's north wing, a long gallery under a barrel-vaulted ceiling of dark wood, with shelves on both sides climbing up into the shadows and ladders on brass rails to reach them. It was the quietest place I'd ever been. The kind of quiet that's built on purpose, with thick rugs and heavy doors and a librarian at a high desk by the entrance whose only job, as far as I could tell, was to look at you until you lowered your voice.

Between the shelves, set into the walls at intervals like windows, were the echoes.

I didn't understand what they were at first. They looked like round mirrors, each about as wide as a cartwheel, framed in tarnished brass. But when I came close to the first one, I saw that the glass wasn't silvered. It was clear, and slightly thick, like the bottom of a bottle, and inside it, somewhere in the depths of it, something was moving.

People. Small and pale and faintly gold, like figures painted on smoke. Two men in old-fashioned coats were facing each other across a floor laid out like a clock face. One of them lifted his hand, and the other went still, frozen mid-step. Then the first walked up to him, and took his wrist, and the frozen man's arm went dark from elbow to wrist while I watched. Then it started again from the beginning. The two men facing each other. The hand rising. Over and over, without a sound.

There was a small brass plate under the frame. *The Duel of Marchbank and Lisle, the Dial, Year 61 After the Drowning. Echo cast by Magister Oren Tull at a cost of two years, four months.*

I stepped back from it with my skin crawling.

"Horrible, aren't they?" said a voice behind me. "Grandmother Penhallow used to take me to see that one on my birthday. As a treat. To teach me the importance of never losing."

Hollis Penhallow was leaning against the end of a bookshelf with his hands in his pockets. He looked terrible. His long face was grey under the freckles and his eyes were red around the rims, and he had the look of someone who hadn't slept and had decided to make a joke about it before anyone else could.

"Miss Fenn," he said. "I hoped it was you. I've lost Pell. Well. Not lost. He's taken Anni down to the kitchens to make her eat something, and he made it very clear I wasn't invited, which was fair. I'm no good with crying people. I start crying too, and then there are two of us, and nobody gets any toast." He took one hand out of his pocket and gestured vaguely at the shelves. "So I came here. It's where I go when I'm useless."

"You're not useless."

"I'm a Penhallow. It's practically our family motto." He looked at the echo in its brass frame, at the two small gold ghosts going round and round. Then he said, without any joke in it at all, "I met him once. Your friend. Mallory. On the bridge, yesterday. He told me my sash was nice."

I didn't say anything.

"It's mended," said Hollis. "Here. You can see the stitches. Nobody ever says it's nice." He swallowed. "Anyway. What are you looking for? Let me carry it. It's not as though I'm using my tithe for anything."

I gave him the list. He read it, and his eyebrows went up and up until they almost vanished into his hair.

"*On the Allotment of Counts.* Saints. Is he trying to bore you to death? Is that the method? It would be very Thane. No blood on the carpets."

"Do you know where they are?"

"I know where *everything* is. Useless people have to be good at something." He tucked the list into his pocket and led me away down the gallery, past echo after echo, little ghost-plays turning forever behind glass: a woman in a tower window, a crowd on a bridge, a man kneeling at the foot of the Horologe with his arm held up. "Don't look at them too long," Hollis said, without turning around. "They get into your dreams."

---

He found the first three books in about ten minutes, climbing ladders with the casual ease of someone who'd spent a lot of time alone in libraries, and passing them down to me one by one. They were enormous. *The Ledger Statutes, Annotated* was so heavy I had to hold it against my hip with both arms.

"And the last one's down here." He jumped off the ladder and led me around a corner into a narrow bay, darker than the others, where the books were older and the air smelled of dust and mouse. "*Lanthorn Before the Drowning.* Nobody reads this. I'm amazed it's not been eaten."

"Why doesn't anyone read it?"

"Because nobody cares what happened before the Drowning. That's the whole point of the Drowning. It's where history starts." He ran a long finger along a shelf of crumbling leather spines. "Here."

He pulled it out and opened it, blowing dust off the first page, and then stopped and turned it around to show me.

It was a picture. An engraving, black and white, very fine, spread across two pages. I recognized the Hours Bridge at once, though the lamps were different. On the bridge stood a row of men and women in long robes, their faces stern, their arms raised. Below them, in the river, there were people. Dozens of them. Their heads and hands just above the water, their mouths open. And behind the bridge, enormous, rising over everything, was the Horologe, and its single hand pointed at eleven.

*The Drowning of the Wells*, said the line of copperplate underneath. *After the fresco in the Ledger House.*

"Do they teach you this in the Wick?" said Hollis.

"Some of it."

"Officially," he said, settling into the role of a lecturer, though his voice had gone quieter, "wellsprings were a plague. People whose counts refilled. Endlessly. The more you took from them, the more they had. Sounds wonderful, doesn't it? It wasn't. Apparently it drove the whole city mad. Families went to war over them. Wells were kidnapped, chained, sold, fought over. Whoever held a well could live forever, so everyone wanted one, so everyone killed for one." He tapped the line of robed figures on the bridge. "And then the Founders, in their great wisdom, decided enough was enough. They rounded up every wellspring in Lanthorn and drowned them in the Sallow in a single night, to save the city from itself. And they stopped the great clock at the hour it was done, so nobody would ever forget." He shrugged. "*Never again the well.* It's carved over the door of the Ledger House. Three feet high. In gold."

I looked at the faces in the water. The engraver had made them all look the same. Mouths open. Hands reaching.

"And the counts?" I said. "After. Where do counts come from?"

"God," said Hollis. "The Allotment. Every baby gets what heaven gives it, and the Ledger writes it down." He smiled with one side of his mouth. "God's very generous to the Gilt, have you noticed? Wonderful sense of the deserving."

"Not to you."

"Ah, well, we're the exception. We're the proof that heaven has a sense of humor." He closed the book and handed it to me. "Penhallows used to be as long as anyone. Then my great-great-grandfather sold the family's years to House Thane to pay a debt on a racehorse. The horse died, of course. It's always the horse. And we've been short ever since." He leaned against the shelf. "Every other House's portrait gallery is the same six people for two hundred years. Ours is the busiest gallery in the Gilt. Painters adore us. We're the only steady work they've got."

I laughed. I didn't mean to. It came out of me sharp and surprised, and he looked so pleased about it that I laughed again.

"There," he said. "That's better. I'm useless, but I'm not *entirely* useless."

I looked down at the book in my arms, at the clock in the engraving with its single hand pointing at eleven, and something came back to me. Not a story. Just a voice. Low, in the dark, from the end of my bed.

*The clock didn't stop, Tamsin. Don't let anybody tell you it stopped. It's holding its breath.*

"My mother used to tell it differently," I said.

"Did she?" Hollis tilted his head. "How?"

I opened my mouth to tell him, and realized I didn't know. That was all I had. One line, in the dark, and a crooked finger tracing circles in the air.

"I don't remember," I said.

---

Bry found us on our way out. She came through the heavy doors of the library like a ship coming into harbor, and the librarian at the high desk stared at her until she lowered her voice, which she didn't.

"Crane wants a book," she said. "Don't know what. Wrote it down." She shoved a crumpled slip at Hollis, who seemed to her, I suppose, the nearest thing in the room to a librarian. "Find it, Penhallow, before I have to read it."

Hollis took the slip. Then he looked up at her face, and his own went very still.

Her lower lip was split, swollen and crusted at one corner. Along her jaw, under the ear, there was a bruise the size and shape of a man's thumb.

"Bry," I said.

"I fell."

"On what?"

"On a Crane," said Bry. Then she shook her head hard, once, like a dog shaking off water. "It's nothing. He didn't draw. He's careful about that now, after yesterday, he won't draw where people can see. He just likes to hold on." Her mouth twisted. "He's got a grip like a vice, the golden bastard. Smiling the whole time."

"I'll kill him," said Hollis, mildly, examining the slip. It was such an odd thing to hear, in that light voice, that Bry actually looked at him properly for the first time. "Not today, probably. I'm terribly busy today. But I'll put it on my list."

"Get in line, Penhallow."

"Bry." I put the books down on a reading table, because my arms were shaking. "Last night. Where was he?"

She looked at me sharply. "Why?"

"Just tell me."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she lowered her voice, which for Bry meant dropping it to the level of an ordinary person's. "He sent for me. Midnight, near enough. Rook came down and got me, same as she got you the first night. Took me up to his rooms." She swallowed. "He wasn't there."

"Wasn't—"

"Wasn't *there*. Empty room, fire lit, a note on the table saying wait. So I waited. Stood there like a hatstand for an hour, more, didn't dare sit down." She touched the bruise on her jaw without seeming to notice she was doing it. "He came back near dawn. Soaked to the knees. Smelling like the river." She paused. "And he was cheerful. That's what I remember. He came in whistling. He gave me *this*"—the jaw—"because I said he looked like a drowned cat. And then he sent me back down. Didn't draw. Didn't want anything. Just wanted me to stand in his room all night so I'd be standing there when he came back."

The hair rose on the back of my neck.

"Why would he want that?" said Hollis slowly.

"So somebody could say he was in his room all night," I said. "If anybody asked. His tithe was there, so he must have been."

Bry stared at me. Hollis stared at me.

"Burn me," said Bry, very quietly.

"Lysander Crane," said Hollis, "smells like money and murder. Always has. I said that when we were eight, at a christening, and my mother sent me home in disgrace." He looked down at the slip in his hand. "I'd quite like to be wrong."

And I stood there between the two of them with the four heavy books on the table in front of me, and the faces in the river on the open page, and I thought: wet to the knees. Both of them. Lysander, coming in whistling at dawn with river water to his knees. And Severin, coming down the tower steps with his hair wet and his trousers soaked, and blood on his glove.

Two of them had been in the water last night. Maybe more. And only one of them had told me, before anyone was dead, to stay away from it.

---

I went up the tower at the sixth bell with the books.

Four hundred and twelve steps to Rook's landing. Two hundred more to the top. My arms were screaming by the time I got there, and I was glad of it. Being angry with my arms was easier than the other thing.

The door opened by itself.

He was at the velvet table again. The lamp was lit, and the clocks were ticking, and the great dead gears hung in the dark overhead like they always did. But he wasn't working. He was sitting with his hands flat on the velvet in front of him, looking at nothing.

And he wasn't wearing gloves.

It was the first time I'd seen both his hands bare. The left one was long and pale and nicked across the knuckles like before. The right one was wrapped in clean white linen from the knuckles to the wrist, and there were spots of red coming through the linen across the palm.

I put the books down on the end of the table, one by one, so they landed hard. He didn't look up until the last one.

"Thank you," he said.

"There was blood on your glove this morning."

"Yes."

"Whose?"

"Mine."

"Prove it."

He lifted his bandaged hand a few inches off the velvet and let it fall again. That was all.

"That proves you've got a bandage," I said. "Where were you last night?"

"Not killing Joss Mallory."

"You told me not to go near the cisterns." My voice was getting louder. I let it. "Two nights ago. Before anyone was dead. You looked up from your little clocks and you told me, and the very next night he's on the sluice grate with his arm gone blank. *How did you know?*"

"Because I've lived here three years," said Severin, "and you've lived here three days."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one that won't get you killed."

I wanted to throw *The Ledger Statutes, Annotated* at his head. I actually picked it up. Then I put it down again, because it weighed as much as Kit and I wasn't sure I could throw it far enough.

"There was a second thread on his wrist," I said instead. "Cut. A Ledger knot. Flat, double loop, the tail tucked under. Somebody from the Gilt tied it. And somebody pulled sixty-six years out of him in one breath, with a working, which means a wright, which means one of *you*. And somebody had the sale written in the Ledger before the Greycoats were even out of bed." I leaned on the table. "Your family keeps the Ledger."

"Yes," said Severin.

"Is that all you're going to say? *Yes?*"

"You've laid out the facts very clearly. I don't disagree with any of them."

"Joss Mallory," I said, and my voice cracked straight down the middle and I hated it, "had sixty-six years, and a girl named Hetty, and he was going to call his baby Ostrander."

"I know."

It was so quiet. That was what stopped me. Not cold, not mocking. Just quiet, like a door closing very softly in another room.

"You don't know anything about him."

"Hetty Brannock," said Severin, looking down at his hands. "Of Saltmarket. She works the dipping vats at the Candleworks, the night shift. Her mother has a cough." He paused. "The Grace was for her mother. Nine years. Joss Mallory asked Fitch Ostrander for ten and Fitch's father said no."

I stared at him.

"Is that supposed to make it better?" I said. "That you read the list?"

"No." He lifted his eyes to mine. "Nothing makes it better."

The clocks ticked. A tall one in the corner struck seven, and a little carriage clock on the mantel answered it with four, and somewhere behind me the cuckoo jerked out of its door and cried three times and went back in. Neither of us moved.

Then I saw the watch.

It was at his elbow, on the velvet, a little apart from everything else, as though it had been set down carefully and left alone. It wasn't like the cheap tin one he'd been mending the first night. It was gold, small and fine, a lady's watch, the kind that hangs from a chain on a ribbon. The crystal was smashed into a star. The hands had stopped at twenty past four. And on the case, in curling script worn almost smooth by somebody's thumb, there was a single engraved letter.

*L.*

I reached for it. I don't know why. To see it properly, I suppose.

"Put that down."

His voice hit me like a slap. I'd barely touched it. I took my fingers away.

He picked it up in his bare left hand. He looked at it for a moment with an expression I couldn't read. Then he pulled open a drawer in the table, and put it inside, and closed the drawer. Gently. As though something were sleeping in there.

"Who's L?" I said.

He didn't answer. He turned back to the velvet, and picked up a pair of tweezers, and the conversation was over. I could feel it ending through the thread, the way you feel a door shut in the wind.

I went to the door. It opened by itself, like always.

"I'll find out," I said, from the threshold. "Who did it. Whether you help me or not."

"I don't doubt it."

"And if it was you—"

"If it were me," said Severin Thane, still bent over his table, in exactly the voice he'd use to remark on the weather, "if I were the one draining tithes, Fenn, I'd have started with the one who lies to me."

He looked up.

He was looking straight at my inked arm.
