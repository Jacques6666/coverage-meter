# Chapter Nine

## The Dial

I didn't answer him.

I stood on the Hours Bridge in the fog with my mouth open and the answer right there behind my teeth, and before I could decide whether it was going to be the truth or a lie, he said, "Don't."

I closed my mouth.

"Don't tell me," said Severin. "If you tell me, I'll know. And then I'll have to decide what to do about it." He was quiet for a moment. The river went on sucking at the piers below us. "I'd rather not have to decide tonight."

He turned and walked toward the island, and after a moment I followed him, because there was nowhere else to go.

The gatehouse was shut. He knocked on it once with the side of his fist, and a warden opened the little door in the big one, and saw who it was, and opened the big one too, all the way, bowing. At the foot of the Tithe Hall stairs, Steward Rook was waiting with her lamp and her keys and her face like a closed fist. She looked at me. Then she looked at him.

"She was with me," said Severin.

Rook looked at him for a long time with her good eye. Then she stepped aside without a word and let me through the iron door, and locked it behind me, and I heard her and Severin say nothing to each other on the other side for a long moment before his footsteps went away up the stairs.

In the dark, from the next bed, Bry whispered: "Well?"

"I didn't drown."

"Shame. I'd worked out a whole speech." A pause. "Was that *him* out there?"

"Go to sleep, Bry."

"Burn me," said Bry, with a great deal of satisfaction, and rolled over.

---

After that, for nearly three weeks, nothing happened.

That's not true. Everything happened. Classes and weighings and bells and meals; autumn coming in hard off the river, the first frost on the Tithe Court flagstones, the wrights in their fur-lined coats and us in our grey wool with our sleeves pushed up and our arms going blue. But nothing happened the way Joss had happened. No alarm bells. No bodies. The Meridian ticked along like one of Severin's clocks, and after a while, the way you do, I started to believe it would keep ticking.

Severin and I did what we'd agreed to do. When he cast in class, I faked it. A stilling in Theory of Hours, eleven hours. A quickening in Applied Workings to close a cut on his own thumb, four days. A small unwinding to mend a cracked inkwell, because Magister Vadra asked him to, a week and two days. Each time, I stood beside him with my hand clutched at my wrist like a tithe whose draw aches, and dulled and grayed and feathered, and nobody saw. Each Seventhday, Steward Rook held my arm up to the lamp and wrote my count in her book a little shorter than the week before. Every night, I aged myself under the blanket by hand.

He didn't summon me to the tower. He didn't speak to me outside of class except to say "Fenn" when he wanted me to stand up, and "Thank you" when he wanted me to sit down. Through the thread, his heartbeat stayed slow and even and far away, like a clock in a locked room.

And he never drew. Not a minute. I'd lie awake waiting for it some nights, the tug on the line, the hand on the rope. It never came.

Whatever he was spending, it wasn't me.

---

Lysander Crane challenged him on the twentieth day of the term, at supper, in front of the whole Refectory.

He did it beautifully. I'll say that for him. He waited until the room was full and the soup was being served, and then he stood up at the Crane table with a glass of wine in his hand, golden as a saint in the lamplight, and turned toward the top of the hall, and said in his warm, carrying, delighted voice:

"Cousin. I don't believe I've ever seen you on the Dial."

The Refectory went quiet a table at a time, the way it had when Lady Aurelia walked in.

Severin, at the Thane table, didn't look up from his soup.

"You haven't," he said.

"Not once. In three years." Lysander turned to the room, spreading his free hand as if to share a joke with everybody. "The Thane heir. The finest caster of his year, they tell me. Hasn't stepped onto the Dial since—when was it? Ah, yes." He smiled. "Since his last tithe went home in a box."

Along the wall, beside me, Bry made a sound in her throat like a dog about to bite.

"One begins to wonder," said Lysander, "whether he's afraid of what it would cost her."

He tipped his glass toward me. Every head in the room turned and looked at me, standing against the wall in my grey with my arm bare. I made my face into a wall.

Severin set his spoon down.

He did it the way he did everything: precisely, quietly, parallel to the edge of the bowl. Then he looked up at his cousin down the length of the Refectory, and there was nothing in his face at all.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Noon."

---

The Dial is in the open air.

I hadn't known that. I'd imagined it as a room, like the Lantern Theatre, but it was a court of its own on the west side of the island, sunk into the rock like a bowl, open to the sky, with tiers of stone seats rising around it and the river wind howling across the top. The floor at the bottom was a perfect circle of white marble thirty paces across, laid out exactly like a clock face: twelve great brass numerals set into the stone around the rim, and at the center a bronze boss like the hub of the hands.

The whole Meridian came. Every wright, every tithe, every magister, servants packed in at the top of the tiers. And in a gallery cut into the rock on the north side, under a striped awning, there were people I didn't know: men and women in furs, with the stillness of the Long-Lived, come across the bridge from the Gilt to see the Thane heir fight for the first time in three years.

"They bet," said Hollis in my ear. He'd come down from the Penhallow seats to stand with me at the rim of the floor, where the tithes stood. Pell loomed behind him like a wall. "On the tithes. Not the wrights; the wrights are too dull to bet on. They bet on whose tithe goes darker, and how fast. That's the real game. The fight's just the excuse." He nodded toward the gallery. "See the man with the ear trumpet? Lord Ostrander's great-uncle. Two hundred and nine. He once bet a whole year on a tithe going below forty, and when she didn't, he had the duel stopped and declared void. They had to give everyone their money back."

"That's disgusting."

"It's the Gilt." He was pale under his freckles. "Tam. The tithes stand on the rim. Here. All through it. And everybody watches your arm."

"I know."

"I mean *everybody*."

"I know, Hollis."

He looked at me for a moment longer, worried, and then Pell put one big hand on his shoulder and steered him back up to the seats.

The rules of the Dial are simple, which is what makes it dangerous. Two wrights start at opposite sides of the clock, one at twelve and one at six. Their tithes stand outside the circle behind them, at the rim, tethered. The wrights draw and cast. They can still each other, hasten themselves, push the time around them like water. A duel ends when one of them steps out of the circle, or yields, or is stilled for a count of ten.

What you're really fighting, Magister Vadra had told us in class, is the other wright's tithe. Whoever's tithe runs dry first loses. That's why the Gilt bets on the tithes.

Severin walked out onto the marble and took his place at twelve. He'd taken off his coat. He was in black shirtsleeves, with the silver cuff gleaming on his left forearm and his gloves on. He didn't look at the gallery. He didn't look at me.

I stood at the rim behind him, with my sleeve rolled up and my count bare to the sky and six hundred people looking at it, and a twist of ash in my right palm, and the ink pot in the pocket of my jacket, and my thumbnail black with tallow to the quick.

Across the Dial, at six, Lysander Crane stepped onto the marble like a man stepping onto a dance floor, and smiled up at the gallery, and bowed. The Long-Lived applauded. Behind him at the rim stood Bry, with her jaw set and her arm bare: fifty-seven years, ten months, a little dimmer at the tip than she'd been a week ago.

A magister I didn't know, a stooped old man in Lisle white, raised a brass bell.

"At the stroke," he said. "Begin."

He rang it.

---

I'd seen workings. I'd never seen a duel.

Lysander moved first. He drew on Bry. I saw it, a gold rope pulled out of her arm so hard she rocked on her feet, and then he wasn't at six anymore. He was at three. He'd crossed a quarter of the circle in the time it took me to blink, a golden blur that left a sound behind it like tearing silk. A hastening. And then, without stopping, he flung his hand out at Severin and the air between them *rippled*, like heat over a road, and rang.

Severin was gone before it reached him.

He was at ten. I hadn't seen him go. Where he'd been, the ripple struck the marble and the marble *aged*, a patch of white stone the size of a cartwheel going grey and cracked and crumbling in an instant, a hundred years of weather in a breath. The gallery gasped.

And the thread at my wrist lit up like a struck match.

Not a pull. It never pulled. It was that other thing, the thing I'd felt in the Lantern Theatre: a current, hot and fast, rushing past my wrist on its way somewhere else. Somewhere that wasn't me.

*Hastening. Hours per second.* Severin had taught me nothing, but Magister Vadra had, and I'd done the sums on my bed at night. A hastening across a third of the Dial, maybe a day. Dodging an aging working at that distance, maybe two.

My hand was at my wrist. Ash. Spit. Thumb. Dull it. Two days. Three. Feather the edge.

On the Dial, they were both moving now, too fast to follow. Gold streaks crossing and recrossing the clock face, and the air ringing like a hall full of glasses, and every few seconds a patch of marble going grey and old as some working missed. Lysander was laughing. I could hear him over the wind. Bry was swaying at the rim behind him with her teeth gritted, and every time he moved, the rope of light came out of her arm and her count dimmed.

The tip of mine had to keep pace. Not with Bry. With what Severin was spending. And I didn't know what he was spending. I was guessing.

Four days. A week.

Every eye in the gallery, Hollis had said. I could feel them. Six hundred people watching the tips of two counts on two bare arms, and the betting going on up there in low voices, and the man with the ear trumpet leaning forward on his cane.

Two weeks.

The sweat started at the back of my neck. Then it was running down my sides under the wool, and my palms were wet, and the ash in my right hand had turned to paste. I could feel the ink at the top of my count growing slick and warm.

Three weeks. A month. The first notch at the wrist had to go. I pressed my blackened thumbnail into it and dragged, and it went grey, too fast, too clean, the way nothing goes out on its own.

On the Dial, Severin stilled Lysander's left arm in mid-swing, and Lysander tore himself free of it with a working that made Bry cry out loud and drop to one knee at the rim, and the gallery roared.

And I looked down at my own arm, and saw gold.

Near the elbow. Between the sixty-first line and the sixty-second, where I'd laid the ink thinnest that morning because the pot was precious. The sweat had lifted it. A bright thread of seventy-one-year gold was showing through the grey, like sun coming through a crack in a shutter, and as I watched, the crack widened.

I put my hand over it. Too fast. My hand was supposed to be at my wrist, where the draw aches. Not halfway up my arm, clamped there as if I were hiding something.

On the far side of the Dial, mid-stride, Lysander Crane glanced across at me.

I don't know how. In all that blur and ringing and gold, with Severin coming at him from nine, he glanced across the whole width of the clock and looked straight at my hand on my arm, and I saw his eyes narrow.

My heart stopped.

And in my wrist, through the thread, Severin's heartbeat *jumped*.

He'd felt it. My panic. It must have hit him like a fist through the line. He was at nine, gathering himself for something, a working I could feel building in the thread like pressure behind a dam, and he turned his head, just slightly, and looked at me.

At my hand. At what was under it.

Then he looked back at Lysander.

And he stopped.

I mean he stopped moving. Right there at nine, in the middle of the Dial, in the middle of the duel. He let the working he'd been building drain away through the thread into nothing. He stood with his hands at his sides.

Lysander didn't hesitate. He flung out his arm, and the ripple came across the marble, gold and ringing, and hit Severin full in the chest.

He went still.

Frozen. Like the water in the Lantern Theatre. One foot half lifted, his dark hair blown sideways by a wind that had stopped, his face perfectly calm. A man made of glass.

The old Lisle magister raised his bell. "One," he called.

The gallery had gone silent.

"Two."

Lysander was walking toward him across the Dial, slowly now, with his hands spread wide, smiling up at the Long-Lived as though he were accepting flowers.

"Three. Four."

Behind him, at the rim, Bry was on both knees with her arm pressed against her stomach. Her count had gone dim almost to the first notch.

"Five. Six. Seven."

I stood at twelve, with my hand clamped over my arm, and nobody was looking at me anymore. Nobody at all. They were all looking at the Thane heir, frozen in the middle of the clock, losing to a first-year.

"Eight. Nine."

Under my hand, slowly, so as not to be seen, I pressed the slick ink back down over the gold. Thumb. Feather. Gone.

"Ten," said the Lisle magister. "The duel goes to Crane."

---

The stilling broke the moment Lysander let it. Severin's lifted foot came down on the marble. He stood there for a moment as if checking that all of him had arrived. Then he walked off the Dial.

The gallery was in uproar. Not cheering. Not booing either. A sound like a hive that's been kicked. Up under the awning, I could see the Long-Lived leaning together, talking fast behind their hands, and the man with the ear trumpet laughing so hard his great-nephew had to hold him up. All along the tiers, wrights were on their feet, staring. Lysander stood alone in the center of the clock with his arms raised, turning slowly to take it all in, golden and glowing, the cousin who'd beaten the Thane heir on the Dial.

Severin didn't look at any of it. He picked his coat up from the rim, where he'd left it, and put it on, and buttoned it to the throat, and walked toward the stairs.

He passed me without slowing.

"Fix your arm," he said, so quietly that only I could hear.

Then he was gone up the steps, through a crowd of wrights who parted to let him through and then closed up behind him, whispering.

I stood there.

Three weeks I'd thought he was a monster with a secret tithe in a cellar. Three weeks I'd lain awake waiting for the pull. And he'd just stood still in the middle of the Dial in front of the whole Meridian and half the Gilt, and let a first-year Crane freeze him like a fly in honey, and lost, because my ink was running and somebody had looked.

He'd felt my fear through the thread, and he'd thrown it. All of it. Three years of being untouchable. Gone in a count of ten.

For me. For my arm.

I didn't understand. I couldn't make it make sense. I was still standing at twelve, trying to, when the crowd began to drain up the stairs and somebody brushed past me close. Close enough to touch.

Warm breath on my ear. The smell of wine and expensive soap.

"He's never lost before, you know," said Lysander Crane, softly, sweetly, for me alone. His hand came to rest on the small of my back, lightly, just for a moment, and I felt every one of his fingers through the wool. "Not once. Not at anything. And he threw it for *you*."

He laughed under his breath. I could feel him smiling.

"When my cousin tires of you, little tithe," said Lysander, "I'll be waiting."
