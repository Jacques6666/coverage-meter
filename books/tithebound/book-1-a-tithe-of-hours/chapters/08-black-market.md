# Chapter Eight

## Black Market

The laundry goes out on Seventhday night.

I'd worked that out by the fourth day, the way you work out anything in a new place if you need to badly enough: by watching. Every Seventhday after supper, two old men from a laundry in the Wick came over the Hours Bridge with a big flat-bedded cart and a tired brown horse, and loaded it at the kitchen door with sacks of the Meridian's dirty linen, and drove it back across before the tenth bell. The gatehouse wardens at the island end of the bridge waved it through without looking. Nobody looks at laundry.

I lay in the bottom of the cart under a sack of wrights' nightshirts that smelled of expensive soap and other people's sweat, and listened to the wheels go over the joints in the bridge stones, twenty-four lamps, twenty-four joints, and tried not to think about what Steward Rook would do if she counted beds at the eleventh bell and found mine empty.

She'd find it full. I'd stuffed it with my spare grey and my jacket, and Bry had sworn on her mother's teeth that she'd say I was sick and sleeping. She hadn't asked where I was going. She'd only looked at the empty crust of the ink pot in my hand, and then at me, and said, "Be back before the bell or I'm telling her you drowned."

"Thanks."

"I'll make it a good drowning," Bry had said. "Very tragic. I'll cry."

The cart stopped at the bottom of Broker's Row to let the horse drink at the trough. I slid out from under the nightshirts and over the tailboard and into the fog, and nobody shouted, and then I was standing on the cobbles of the Wick for the first time in a week, and the smell of it hit me, tallow and river and coal smoke and fish, and I had to put my hand on a wall for a moment.

I hadn't known I'd missed it. I hadn't known you could miss a smell.

---

Kit was awake. Of course he was. He was sitting at our one table in the window with a candle stub and a sheet of paper, drawing, and when I came in he looked up and dropped his pencil and made a noise like a kettle and hit me in the stomach so hard with his whole body that we both nearly went over.

"You're *back*—"

"I'm not back. I'm visiting. I've got an hour." I held him anyway. He felt bigger, which was impossible, it had only been a week. "Let me look at you."

"Look at *this*." He pulled away, and before I could say anything he'd yanked his left sleeve up to the shoulder and was holding his arm out under the candle like a prize.

I'd known. Severin had told me, that first night, at his velvet table. *The Grace was paid into your brother's count at sunset.* And I'd heard him, and I hadn't believed him, not really, not in the place under my ribs where I keep the things I actually believe.

Thirty-one gold lines.

They ran from Kit's thin wrist halfway up his forearm, close and bright, stacked like a ladder you could climb. I'd seen a hundred long counts. I'd seen seventy-one years every morning of my life in my own arm. I had never in my life seen anything as beautiful as my little brother's thirty-one years under a candle stub on Tallow Lane.

"A man came," said Kit. "From the Ledger. In a grey coat, with a box. On the same day, at sunset, Mrs. Achterberg was here. He had a silver needle and it didn't even hurt." He considered. "Well. It hurt a bit. Like a wasp. But then it went warm. Tam, I'll be *forty-one*. Forty-one! That's ancient! That's older than Mr. Wren!"

I sat down on the edge of the cot. I couldn't do anything else. My legs had stopped.

"Tam?" Kit came and stood in front of me, uncertain now. "Tam, why are you crying? It's good. It's *good*."

"I know," I said. "I know it's good. I'm crying because it's good."

"That's stupid."

"I know."

He sat down beside me and leaned his whole weight against my side, the way he did when he was small, and we sat there for a while with the candle guttering and the fog at the window, and he talked. He told me Mrs. Achterberg had made him a pie. He told me the chandler's dog had had puppies. He told me his lungs had been good all week, not a single bad night, he'd counted. And then he said, in a different voice, with his face pressed into my sleeve:

"Is he nice?"

"Who?"

"The Thane. The one who bought you. Mrs. Achterberg said it was the Thane heir and she crossed herself."

I thought about a room full of ticking clocks, and a bandaged hand, and a voice saying *nothing makes it better*.

"No," I said.

Kit thought about it.

"He paid thirty, though," he said.

"Yes."

"Then he's a bit nice."

I didn't have an answer for that. I put my arm around him and held on, and over his head I looked at the drawing on the table, still wet. It was a new map. It was the Meridian again, but from above this time, as a gull would see it, the island and the bridge and the court and the tower. He'd drawn the Tithe Hall under the river, and the cisterns under that, black and wavy, and under the tower, deep in the rock, the little circle with the heart in it.

"Do you still have it?" he said, without lifting his head. "My map? The first one?"

"Here." I touched my shirt, where it lay against my ribs, soft as cloth now.

"You have to give it back. After."

"After," I said.

---

I left him asleep at the tenth bell with the candle out and the blanket tucked under his chin, and I stood on the landing outside our door in the dark and took the rag out of my pocket, and spat on it, and took my ink off.

All of it. Ten years. I'd buy new; I'd put it back on fresh before I crossed the bridge. But to buy new I needed money, and to get money I needed to sell, and to sell, a broker needs to see the line go dark.

It took a long time. Tallow ink doesn't want to come off; that's the point of it. By the time I'd scrubbed my arm clean to the elbow, my skin was red and stinging, and the landing was lit up gold like there was a lamp burning on it.

Seventy-one years and four months. In the dark, on Tallow Lane, it was so bright I could see the grain of the wood on our door.

I pulled my sleeve down to the wrist and my hood up to the eyes and went down into the street.

---

Pruett's was halfway down Broker's Row, a narrow shop with a bow window full of brass scales and old ledgers to make it look respectable. It wasn't. Pruett paid three crowns a year, which was top rate on the Row, and he didn't ask questions, which was why he could pay three crowns: he made it up on what he sold the years for, up the hill. I'd never sold to him. He was too close to home, too close to Gannet. I'd always gone to Saltmarket.

There wasn't time for Saltmarket.

The bell over the door jangled. Pruett looked up from behind his counter, a thin man with oiled grey hair and spectacles pushed up on his forehead and fingers stained purple from the stamp pad.

"Selling?"

"One year."

"Name."

"Nell Pike."

He wrote it in his book. He didn't believe it. Nobody on the Row ever gives their real name; that's what the Brokers' Privilege is for. "Arm."

I pushed up my sleeve.

Pruett's spectacles came down off his forehead onto his nose. He looked at my arm for a long moment. Then he looked up at my face, under the hood, and then back down at my arm.

"Long one, ain't you," he said.

"Long enough."

"Seventy-one. Don't see that every day." He reached under the counter for his needle, a broker's needle, thicker than a binder's, black with use. "Haven't I seen you before?"

"No."

"You've a look of someone."

"Everybody's got a look of someone."

He grunted, and took my wrist, and set his needle against the top of my count, the seventy-first line, and began to draw.

A broker's needle is slow. That's the difference between a broker and a working. Pruett didn't have to cast anything; he just let it bleed, the way you'd let sap out of a tree, and caught it in a little glass vial on a silver chain that hung from the needle's end. It took a quarter of an hour. I stood there with my arm on his counter and watched the top line of my count go out. Brown at the ends first. Then grey. Then dark. And the vial at the end of the needle filled with a thin gold light, like honey with the sun behind it, until it glowed.

It's the worst feeling in the world. I've done it a dozen times now and it never gets better. It isn't pain, exactly. It's loss. A year of my life, a whole year, mornings and suppers and winters and whatever I would have said to whoever I would have been, going into a little glass bottle on a broker's counter so that some lord in the Gilt could have one more year of being two hundred.

I always throw up after. I didn't, that night. I didn't have time.

"There," said Pruett. He corked the vial and wrote in his book. "Nell Pike. Seventy-one years, four months, sold one, seventy left." He counted three silver crowns onto the counter and pushed them toward me with one purple finger. "Pleasure."

I took them and pulled my sleeve down and went.

Outside, I stood in the fog with my back against his bow window, breathing. Under my sleeve, at the top of my count, the dark line was already starting to warm. I could feel it, the way you feel a cold hand slowly coming back to life in your pocket. By midnight it'd be half back. By morning, whole.

*Seventy-one years, four months, sold one, seventy left.* Written in Pruett's book. And next week, or next month, Pruett would send his book up the hill to the Ledger office to be copied into the great Ledger itself, the way every broker had to by law, and a clerk would write it down.

I'd never thought about that before. I'd never had to.

---

Mother Gannet's shop was at the very end of the Row, where it meets the river, so close to the bridge that the bottom step of the first lamp was practically on her doorstep. It had no sign. It didn't need one.

She was in her chair behind the counter where she always was, where I'd stood beside her for seven years: an enormous woman in a dozen shawls, with a face like a sack of flour and small black clever eyes, and rings on every finger, and a long count, I knew, because she'd bought most of it. She looked up as the bell rang, and her eyes went small with pleasure.

"Well," said Mother Gannet. "Look what the river spat out. They let you out of the big house already, Tam? Or did you swim?"

"I came with the laundry."

She laughed, a low wheeze like a bellows. "That's my girl. What do you want?"

"Ink. A full pot."

"Two crowns."

"It was a crown and a half in the summer."

"It was summer in the summer." She held out her ringed hand. I put two crowns in it. She reached under the counter without looking and came up with a fat clay pot sealed with wax and set it in front of me. Then she didn't let go of it.

"Tam," she said.

I waited.

"Greycoats came down the Row last week," said Mother Gannet. "Three days before Tithe Day. Went into every broker's shop from here to the Sump. Very polite. Very quiet. Asked to look at the books."

My hands had gone still on the counter.

"They weren't looking for anybody who'd sold," said Gannet. "Everybody sells. They were looking for somebody who sells the same thing twice. A long count, they said. Comes in, sells a year or two, goes out. Comes back a month later, or goes to another shop, and the book says the same count as before. Like nothing was ever taken." Her black eyes didn't leave my face. "Strange thing to be looking for, I said to them. Never heard of such a thing. Nobody's count goes back up. Not since the Drowning."

"No," I said. My voice came out perfectly steady. I was proud of that. "Not since the Drowning."

"That's what I told them." She let go of the pot. "And they went away. But they'll be back. They had the look of men who'd been sent, and who'd go on being sent until they found it." She sat back in her chair, and her shawls settled around her like a hen's feathers. "There was a woman like that, once."

"Like what?"

"Long count. Sold to me twice in one week, years and years ago, when I was younger than you'd credit. Same count both times. To the notch." Gannet folded her hands over her belly. "I noticed, because noticing's my trade. And I never wrote it down twice, and I never told a soul, and I never saw her again."

"Who was she?"

"Ah," said Mother Gannet, "well, now. That's not for sale."

I stood there with the pot of ink in my hand. Outside, on the bridge, I could hear the wardens calling the half hour.

"You worked my counter seven years, Tam," said Mother Gannet. "Seven years. And I never saw your arm. Not once. Not in summer, not when you scalded yourself on the kettle that time and I wanted to look at it. A counter who never shows her count." She tilted her head. "I'm not asking. I'm too old to ask. But whatever you are, girl, don't be it on the Row."

I didn't say anything. I couldn't think of anything that wasn't a lie, and she'd have known it was a lie, and I didn't want to lie to her. That was new.

She jerked her chin at the curtain behind the counter. "Back room," she said. "Five minutes. I'm not looking."

---

I inked myself by the light of my own arm in Mother Gannet's back room, among the crates and the jars and the bales of black-market thread, as fast as I've ever done it and as well. Thin coats. Feathered ends. Ten years gone under the brush; sixty-one left, honest as a magistrate. The sold line at the top was coming back already, warm under the grey. Nobody would ever know.

When I came out, Gannet had her eyes closed, and her hands folded over her belly, and her mouth was moving as if she were counting something under her breath. She didn't open them.

"Mind the bridge," she said. "There's been fog on it all week."

---

There was.

It came up off the Sallow so thick that I could see only three lamps at a time ahead of me, three soft yellow globes hanging in grey wool, and behind me the Wick had vanished as though somebody had wiped it off a slate. My footsteps sounded too loud. The river sounded too close. I walked fast with my hands in my pockets, one of them closed tight around the pot of ink and the other around the last silver crown, and I counted lamps.

Eleven. Twelve.

Halfway.

The eleventh bell began to ring.

It came across the water from the Meridian, huge and slow, one stroke after another, and I started to run. Fourteen. Fifteen. The wardens shut the gatehouse at the last stroke and didn't open it again till dawn, and the river gets cold at night, and Steward Rook would be standing at the iron door of the Hall with her lamp and her keys and her milky eye, counting beds, and Bry would be telling her I was sick, and Rook would know—

Ahead of me, the lamp at the sixteenth post went out.

Not blown out. There was no wind. It just dimmed and died, the way a candle dies when somebody pinches the wick. Then the seventeenth. Then the eighteenth. One after another, marching away from me into the fog, until the bridge ahead was nothing but grey dark and the sound of the bell.

I stopped.

Behind me, the fifteenth lamp went out too.

I stood on the Hours Bridge in the dark with my heart slamming and the bell still tolling, and I thought of Joss, walking barefoot out of a locked room. I thought of a Ledger knot, small and neat and flat. I thought: *it's me. They've found me. Gannet said they'd be back and they're here.*

Something moved in the fog to my left.

I didn't wait to see what it was. I turned to run back toward the Wick, and a hand closed around my arm above the elbow, hard, and pulled, and I was off my feet and slammed back into stone, into one of the little half-round alcoves set into the parapet of the bridge where people step out of the way of carts. A body pressed me back into the curve of it. A gloved hand came down over my mouth.

I bit it.

I bit as hard as I could, through leather, and I brought my knee up the way Bry taught me when we were eleven, and I'd have done worse, I'd have gone for the eyes next, except the second heartbeat in my wrist was suddenly so loud I couldn't hear anything else.

Slow. Even. Right there. Right against me.

"Be *still*," hissed Severin Thane in my ear.

I went still.

Lantern light swung across the mouth of the alcove. Two bridge wardens, in their long cloaks with the Meridian clock on the breast, came walking out of the fog from the island end, one of them holding the lantern up and the other carrying a long brass snuffer on a pole.

"Every second lamp at the eleventh," the one with the snuffer was grumbling. "Every second one. And I says to him, what's the use of every second one, in a fog like this, you can't see your hand, and he says to me—"

The lantern swung toward us.

Severin let go of my mouth and stepped out of the alcove into the light, as easily as if he were stepping out of his own front door, and the two wardens stopped dead.

He was in a plain dark coat, a Wick coat, worn at the cuffs, with the collar turned up. No sash. I couldn't see his silver cuff; his sleeve was pulled down over it. If I'd passed him on Broker's Row, I wouldn't have looked twice. But the wardens knew him. You could see them know him. The one with the lantern went white, and the one with the snuffer took his hat off so fast he nearly dropped the pole in the river.

"My lord—"

"My tithe has been on an errand for me in the Wick," said Severin. "She's returning late at my instruction. Make a note, if you need to make a note."

"No, my lord. No need, my lord."

"Then good night."

They went. They went so fast, back toward the island, that the lantern light bounced away into the fog like a ball, and in less than a minute there was nothing but the dark and the river and the last strokes of the bell dying away over the water.

I came out of the alcove. My legs were shaking. I didn't let them.

"You," I said.

"Me."

"You *followed* me."

"I felt you leave." He held up his left hand, palm toward me, the hand with the thread. I couldn't see it in the dark, but I knew where it was. "The thread stretches, Fenn. Did nobody tell you? When you go over the water, it pulls. Like a line with a fish on it." He lowered his hand. "I felt you go, about an hour after supper. I came after you."

"Why?"

He didn't answer that. Instead he took a step toward me. Not touching. Just close.

"You went to Pruett's," he said.

My stomach dropped through the bridge into the Sallow.

"I stood across the Row in a doorway and I watched you go in. With your sleeve down and your hood up. And I watched you come out a quarter of an hour later with three crowns in your hand, and stand with your back against his window like you were going to be sick." His voice was very quiet. "Pruett pays three crowns a year. Top rate on the Row."

I said nothing. I couldn't.

"A Wick girl with seventy-one years doesn't sell," said Severin. "She hides. She inks herself down and keeps her head down and prays nobody with a black thread and a sack ever knocks on her door. Selling is the one thing she'd never do. Selling's how they find you." He paused. "Unless she knows she'll get it back."

The fog moved between us. Somewhere below, the river sucked at the piers. On my arm, under the fresh ink, the year I'd sold an hour ago lay warm and half-lit and coming home.

"How many times," said Severin Thane, "have you sold that same year, Fenn?"
