# Chapter Twenty-Nine

## The Heart of the Clock

He went for her.

I felt it before I saw it, the way I'd felt every working he'd ever cast: a surge in the thread at my wrist, hot and huge, his life welling up out of him like a river breaking its banks. And then he wasn't in front of me anymore. He was a streak of gold across the white marble, a hastening so fast it tore the air, straight at the small figure in black lace with her face turned up to mine.

She lifted one finger.

That was all. She didn't turn her head. She didn't stop smiling at me. She lifted one finger of her right hand, lazily, the way you'd lift a finger to call a waiter, and the streak of gold stopped.

He was frozen in the air beside her. Mid-stride. One foot off the marble, his bare left arm reaching for her throat, his face white and his teeth bared and his dark hair flung sideways by a wind that had stopped. A man made of glass. The second time I'd seen him like that. The first time he'd done it to himself, for me.

"Oh, darling," said Lady Aurelia, softly, without looking at him. "Really."

She reached out with her left hand and took his frozen wrist. His bare one. Above the silver thread. Over his seven gold lines.

"A little lesson," she said.

And drew.

I felt it go. Through the thread. Through him. Into her. It wasn't a river this time; it was a hook, sunk into him and pulled. One year. I felt it tear out of him and the thread at my wrist jerked like a rope with a body falling on the end of it. Two. I heard myself make a sound. Three.

She let go.

The stilling broke, and he came down out of the air and hit the marble on his knees and didn't get up. His arm was still held out in front of him, shaking. I looked at it. I couldn't not look at it.

Four lines. Two notches.

"*Stop!*"

It came out of me so loud it hurt my throat. I was on my knees beside him. I didn't remember getting there. I had my hands on him, on his shoulders, on his face, and he was gasping, short shallow breaths like a man who'd been held under water, and his skin was cold.

"Stop," I said again, to her, without looking up. "Stop. I'll come. I'll come with you. Leave him. Leave all of them."

"Of course, dear," said Lady Aurelia Thane. "I'm not a monster."

---

She gave her orders the way she'd given them at Thane House on the Long Night, sweetly, without raising her voice, and everyone on the Dial obeyed. Captain Marr was to see her heir taken to the infirmary; he'd had a shock. The Houses were to convene in the Ledger House at the sixth bell to hear the Keeper's account of the Crane boy's crimes. The Greycoats were to release the tithes they were holding, Kettle and Penhallow included, unharmed. The Reckoning was concluded. The well was in the Keeper's custody under the Founders' law, and would be dealt with as the Founders had dealt with them.

"*Never again the well,*" said someone in the gallery, and someone else said it after him, softly, like an amen.

Severin's hand found my wrist. His fingers closed around it, over the silver thread, cold and shaking and as hard as he could make them, which wasn't very hard.

"Tamsin," he said. It came out in pieces. "Don't. Please. I'll—"

"You said *ours*," I said.

He looked up at me. His eyes were the grey of tallow ink. They were wet.

"In the tower," I said. "You said it's ours, not yours alone. This is ours." I put my hand flat on his chest, over his heart, the way I had at the velvet table three nights ago. Through my palm and through the thread at the same time, I felt it, fast and ragged and frightened. "Let me carry this one."

His hand tightened on my wrist. Then, slowly, finger by finger, it opened.

Captain Marr's Greycoats came and lifted him. He let them. He didn't take his eyes off me the whole time they were carrying him across the marble, and I didn't take mine off him, and through the thread his heartbeat went further and further away until it was a drum in another room again.

As Marr passed me, he stopped. Just for a moment. His long tired face was grey.

"I'm sorry, counter," he said, very low.

Then he went on.

I looked for Magister Vadra. She wasn't on the Dial anymore. She wasn't at the rim, or on the magisters' tier. The place where she'd stood was empty, and so was the place on the marble where the glowing lens had been.

I looked for my friends. Bry was on her feet between two Greycoats with blood running from her nose, glaring at the dais as if she could set it on fire. Hollis was beside her, white as paper, being held by the arm. Pell was at the rim with his huge hands in fists at his sides and Hollis's primrose crushed in one of them. All three of them were looking at me.

I couldn't think of anything to say to them that would fit across that much white marble. So I just looked back. Bry nodded, once. Hollis put his free hand over his heart.

"Come along," said Lady Aurelia.

---

She took me down herself.

Not through the cistern, or the tunnel, or the clock-faced iron door. Through the Meridian. Across the Tithe Court under the clock, with two Thane guards in black and silver behind us, and a Ledger binder in grey carrying a velvet cushion with a silver needle on it, and the whole college watching from the windows. In at a door at the base of the Horologe that I'd walked past every day for a term and never noticed, set flush into the black stone. Down a stair.

It was nothing like the cistern stair, or the narrow wet spiral of the Thane passage. It was wide and dry and carpeted in dark red, and lit every few steps by lamps in silver sconces shaped like clock hands, and the walls on either side were carved, all the way down, with clock faces. Every one with a single hand. Every hand on eleven.

"My own stair," said Lady Aurelia, conversationally, going down it ahead of me with one small hand trailing on the carved wall. "The Keeper's stair. Every Keeper since the Founders has used it. Nobody else may." She glanced back at me over her shoulder, and her dimples came out. "You can't imagine how long I've waited to bring you down here. Ten years! Ten years of Wick children. So *tiresome*. Every autumn, testing them one by one, and none of them ever right."

I didn't say anything. I walked down the red carpet behind her, with the guards behind me and the binder's footsteps behind them, and my bare left arm blazing gold in the lamplight, and I counted the steps, because counting is what I do. And under my feet, through the carpet, through the stone, the beat came up.

Slow. Deep. Louder with every step.

"You were very good, you know," said Lady Aurelia. "With the ink. I nearly didn't see it. In the Refectory, your arm was perfect. I only knew because of the number. Sixty-one." She laughed softly. "Far too generous for a Wick girl. And then, of course, when I brushed it—" She held up her thumb and looked at it fondly. "Tallow ink has a very particular feel. I've known it for two hundred years."

Two hundred and eleven steps.

"And then the Long Night," she said. "Oh, I was so pleased. I nearly wept. Three years, poured into my foolish boy, and your count filling up behind them like a cistern after rain." She sighed with pleasure. "I knew then. But I wanted the Dial to know too. I wanted them all to see. It matters so much, dear, that people *see*."

Three hundred and four steps.

The stair ended.

---

We were in the passage. The one I'd walked down three nights ago, with Severin half a step behind me with a lamp. I knew it by the carvings, and by the warmth, and by the singing: that high sweet endless note, like a fingertip on the rim of a glass, coming from the end of it.

And there was the door. Solid silver, without a seam or a hinge or a lock, standing like a wall of still water from floor to ceiling, singing.

Lady Aurelia walked up to it and laid her small bare palm flat on the silver.

It opened.

Not like a door. It *parted*, down the middle, the two halves of the silver drawing back from each other like water drawing back from a dropped stone, without a sound. And the singing stopped. All at once. The silence afterward was so complete that I could hear my own blood.

"Go on," said Lady Aurelia, stepping aside, gesturing with one hand, like a hostess at the door of her ballroom. "After you."

I went in.

---

It was a room. Round. Vast. Bigger than the Refectory, bigger than the Lantern Theatre, bigger than the ballroom at Thane House. The walls went up and up into the dark, black stone carved all over with clock faces, and I couldn't see a ceiling at all; the dark just went on overhead forever, like the inside of a well.

Down through the middle of that dark, from somewhere so high above that it was lost in shadow, came a shaft of black iron, as thick as an oak tree. And at the bottom of the shaft, hanging motionless at the height of a tall man's head above the floor, was the pendulum.

I'd seen it every night for three months through the grate in Severin's ceiling, far above, hanging still in the dark over the dead gears. A disc of black iron as wide as a cartwheel. I'd seen it in Vadra's engraving, in black and white, two hundred and twenty years old.

And under it, on the floor, was a woman.

She was kneeling. Her arms were held out and up at her sides, the way you'd hold out your arms to catch a child running toward you. They were held there by thread. Silver thread, dozens of lines of it, so fine they looked like lines of light, running from her wrists, and her ankles, and a ring around her throat, up into the dark. Up past the pendulum. Up toward wherever the clockworks were. And other threads, fewer, running out sideways from her wrists across the floor to the walls, and into the stone, and through it, going somewhere I couldn't see.

Her head was bowed. Her hair hung down over her face, long and dark and streaked with grey, almost to the floor.

And her arms were light.

Not lines. Not a ladder of gold from wrist to elbow, rung after rung, the way every count I'd ever seen was made. There were no rungs. There was no end. Her arms blazed, from her fingertips to her shoulders, with one continuous unbroken gold, so bright it hurt to look at, and the light ran up over her shoulders and down her back and spilled off her onto the black stone floor around her like water, like a pool, like something overflowing that would never stop overflowing.

The Founders' Well.

I'd seen it in an engraving in Vadra's study. I'd thought it was three hundred years old. I'd thought it was a story.

"The Founders didn't drown them all," said Lady Aurelia Thane behind me, softly. "Of course they didn't. What a waste that would have been. They kept the best one. Down here, under the clock. And when she wore out, after a hundred years or so, they found another. And another." She came up beside me, eating a violet. "It runs in families, you know. Wells. Mothers and daughters, grandmothers and granddaughters, all the way back. Most of them go their whole lives without ever knowing what they are. A well only shows when something is taken from it." She sighed. "So you have to take things. That's the only way to find one."

I couldn't speak. I couldn't move.

"The last one wore out eleven years ago," said Lady Aurelia. "Poor old thing. A hundred and forty years she lasted. I was quite sad. And I spent a whole year looking for the next one, every broker's book in the city, every long count that sold. And then a broker on the Row told me about a woman who'd sold the same three years twice in a single week, under two names, with the same count to the notch both times." She smiled. "A woman with a little crooked finger."

The woman under the pendulum hadn't moved. Her head was still bowed. Her hands, held up and out by the silver threads, were open, palms upward, as if she were offering something.

The little finger of her left hand was bent sideways at the middle joint. Crooked. The way a finger sets when it's been broken and nobody's splinted it right.

"Ten years," said Lady Aurelia, "she's fed me. Me, and the Long-Lived who pay me for the privilege. She's fed the clock. She's fed the Ledger." She tilted her head, studying the kneeling woman the way you'd study a horse that hadn't won. "And ten years, something has stolen half of her. Every drop I took, half of it went somewhere else. Down some thread I couldn't find. Into someone I couldn't see." She turned and looked at me with her warm brown eyes, gold-flecked, delighted. "Now I know where it went."

The thread at my wrist was burning.

Not the silver one. Not Severin's. There was another thread, there, at my wrist, that I'd never seen and never known about and had felt every day of my life since I was nine years old, as heat, as a hand closing around my wrist in the dark. And it was pulling. It was pulling so hard I thought my arm would come out of its socket. Toward the floor. Toward the pendulum. Toward the woman kneeling under it in her pool of endless light.

My feet moved.

I walked across the black stone. The light from her arms washed over my boots, and it was warm, warm as a bath, warm as a hand. Nobody stopped me. I heard Lady Aurelia say something to the guards, softly, and laugh. I didn't care. I walked until I was standing in front of her, close enough to touch. Close enough to see that her hair was dark, under the grey, the same dark as mine. Close enough to see her thin wrists in their silver threads, and the bones of them, like a bird's.

My voice came out of me as small as it had been when I was nine, sitting up all night with a baby in my arms, waiting for a door.

"Mama?"

The woman under the pendulum lifted her head.

Slowly. As if it were very heavy. Her hair fell back from her face. It was thin, and grey-skinned, and older than it should have been, and so much like Kit's that I couldn't breathe.

Her eyes opened. Dark. Too big for her face. The kind of eyes that get bigger when they're frightened, as though they're trying to see the whole bad thing coming at once.

They found me. They looked at me for a long, long moment.

"Tamsin," said my mother. Her voice was a thread. "I told you never to come here."
