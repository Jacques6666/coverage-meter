# Chapter Twenty-One

## The List

I felt him coming.

I was still standing at the velvet table with the black folder open in my hands and that last line in front of me, *No*, in his small upright writing, when the thread at my wrist went hot. Not warm. Hot. And under it, his heartbeat, which had been held shut away from me for five days, came slamming back in so hard and fast that I put my hand on the table to keep my balance.

He was running. Up the tower. I could feel every step of it through my wrist.

I didn't move. I didn't put the folder back. I didn't try to hide. I stood there with the pages in my hands and the clocks ticking all around me and waited, and counted, because counting is what I do. I'd got to two hundred and eighty when the door swung open behind me.

He stopped on the threshold. I heard him stop. I heard him breathing hard, the way you breathe after six hundred steps at a run.

I didn't turn around.

"Falls?" I said. "No."

He didn't answer.

"*T. Fenn. Seventy-one years, four months.*" My voice came out very flat. "Fresh ink. It's the last line on ten years of lists in your handwriting, Severin. Every long count at every Market for ten years. *Falls? Yes.* And then a date, and *Broker Seven*, and a box." I turned around then. "And then me."

He was standing just inside the door with one hand on the frame. His coat was open. His face was white.

"Tamsin," he said.

"Don't." I held up the folder. "Don't say my name like that. Tell me what this is."

He closed the door behind him. He didn't come any closer.

"It's evidence," he said.

"Of what?"

"Of her." He took a breath. "Those are her lists. Not mine. I copied them. Every one of them, for two years, out of the ledgers in her study at Thane House. Every Market, she writes down the long counts. Every name over sixty. And then, through the autumn, she tests them, one by one, and writes the answer in the column. *Falls? Yes.* And then the sale to Broker Seven, and the drowning." His voice was very steady and very quiet. "I copied them so that someday, if I could ever get them in front of someone who'd listen, there'd be a record. In a hand that wasn't hers. So she couldn't burn it."

I looked down at the pages. Ten years of names.

"And the years before you started copying?"

"I went back. I found them. She keeps everything." He swallowed. "She's very orderly."

"And Broker Seven?"

"Broker Seven," said Severin, "is the number the Ledger uses in the sale books when the buyer is the Ledger itself." He paused. "When the buyer is the Keeper."

The clocks ticked. Every one of them, all around the room, out of time with each other.

"Her," I said.

"Her."

---

"Then this." I turned to the last page and held it up so he could see. "Whose is this?"

He looked at the last line. His face didn't change, but I felt it through the thread: something going through him like a blade.

"Mine," he said.

"Why?"

"I wrote it the night I came back from Thane House." He looked at the line, not at me. "Then I sat at this table until the sun came up, trying to decide whether to burn it."

"*Why would you write it at all?*"

"Because if anything happens to me," said Severin, "the person who opens that drawer needs to know what she was looking for, and who she found. Vadra, or Marr, or Penhallow. Someone. So they know what to protect." His voice cracked, just slightly. "So it doesn't just stop with me."

I stood there with the folder in my hands.

"Your name was on her list," he said. "This year's. On Tithe Day. *J. Mallory, sixty-six. A. Rusk, sixty-three. T. Fenn, sixty-one.*" He was looking at me now. "Three long counts this year. She wrote them in her ledger the night before the Market. I copied it the next morning, at breakfast, at Thane House, before I came over the bridge."

My hands had started to shake. The pages rattled.

"That's why," I said.

"Yes."

"That's why thirty."

"That's the second reason," said Severin. "I could only buy one of you."

---

The room tilted.

I put the folder down on the velvet because I couldn't hold it anymore. I put both hands flat on the table and leaned on them and looked down at the grey cloth, at the place where the little tools used to lie in their rows, and I tried to breathe.

"You knew," I said.

"I knew they might die."

"*You knew.*" I looked up at him. "You stood in the Tithe Court and watched Joss go to Ostrander for nine years and punch the air. You watched Anni hum her hymns. You knew they were on her list and you knew what she did to the people on her list, and you walked past them every day for weeks, and you didn't say a *word*."

"What would I have said?"

"*Anything!*"

"To whom?" He didn't raise his voice. He didn't have to. "Tell Joss Mallory that the Keeper of the Ledger was going to have him drained to the bone before midwinter? And then what? Where would he go? He's a tithe. He's House property for the term. If he runs, he's a thief, and they hang him. If he tells anyone, who believes him? A Wick boy who says the Keeper's a murderer, on the word of the Thane heir, who's got a rumor about every tithe he's ever had?" His jaw was tight. "I've been trying to stop it for three years, Tamsin. Three Markets. I've gone down to that sluice every time the river groaned. I've been too late every time. I can't stop her. I can't prove anything. I can't save them." He stopped. "This year I thought, if I can keep just one. Just one, this time. The longest. Because she'd want the longest most."

"So you picked me."

"I picked you."

"Over Joss. Over Anni."

He flinched. I saw it. It was the first time I'd ever seen him flinch from anything, and it went through me and out the other side.

"Yes," said Severin.

I couldn't look at him. I looked at the window, at the grey afternoon, at the river beyond with ice creeping out from the banks.

*His last tithe went home in a box.* Everybody had said it. Everybody had been so sure. The Thane heir, cold as the river, who'd bought a Wick girl for thirty years for reasons nobody could understand.

He'd bought me to keep me alive. And he'd let the other two go, because he couldn't buy three.

I didn't know what to do with that. I didn't know where to put it.

---

"The six hours," I said, when I could speak. "At Thane House. With her. Tell me."

He was quiet for a long moment.

"She knew," he said. "She saw the ballroom. She felt it. She's strong enough to feel a working's flow from across a room, and she felt three years go the wrong way down my thread, and she felt yours come back." He looked at his own wrist. "She asked me to come to her study the next morning. I went. She was eating violets. She said, *Darling, your little Wick girl refills.*"

The room was very cold.

"I lied," said Severin. "I told her it was Orrin. That I'd found the well last year, in my own tithe, and hidden him from her, and he'd died of a fever at Deepwinter before I understood what he was. That what she'd felt in the ballroom was me, my own count, a trick of the working. That the well was dead and she could stop looking."

"Did she believe you?"

"She laughed." His mouth twisted. "She said, *Orrin Fisk lives in a cottage in Calder Bay with a wife and a cough and a count that falls like rain. I've known for a year, darling. I let him go because he was nothing.*" He was quiet. "She's always known. Everything I ever did. She just never cared, because none of it ever mattered to her."

"And then?"

"And then she made me an offer."

I waited.

"At the Reckoning," said Severin, "in front of the Long-Lived, I'm to perform the great working. Every final-year does; that's the tradition. And I'm to draw it from my tithe. All of it." His voice didn't change. "Everything you have, in front of the whole Gilt. If your count falls, you were never what she was looking for, I've proved myself a Thane, and she names me her heir in front of everyone. The Ledger. Thane House. All of it."

"And if it doesn't fall."

"Then you're hers."

The clocks went on ticking. The cuckoo jerked out of its little door and cried four times and went back in.

"And you said yes," I said.

"I said yes."

I'd known he was going to say it. I'd known since Bry, in the dark. *There's two ways to make sure nobody looks at something.* I'd known since Lysander in the corridor. *He came out looking like a man who's sold something.* I'd known since the door stayed shut against me for five days. It still hit me like the floor of the Lantern Theatre hitting Bry's head.

"Why?"

"Because if I'd said no," said Severin, "she'd have taken you that night. From my old room at the top of the east wing, with you sitting on the end of my bed waiting for me to come back. And I couldn't have stopped her." He met my eyes. "Yes bought us until the Reckoning. Three weeks. That's all I could get."

"You should have told me."

"I was going to fix it first."

"Fix it *how?*"

His jaw worked.

"I don't know yet," he said.

---

I stood there and looked at him. At his white face and his open coat and his hand still on the door frame, as if he needed it to stand up. At the silver cuff on his left arm, over his seven years.

He'd shut the door against me for five days. He'd let me lie awake in the Tithe Hall with my hand over my wrist, listening to his heartbeat held away from me, while Bry said *there's two ways* and Lysander said *what do you think he bought you for*. He'd let me wonder whether he'd sold me. Because he was going to fix it first. Because he'd decided, alone, at a velvet table in a room full of clocks, what I could bear to know and when.

The way he'd decided, alone, that he could buy one of us. The way he'd decided, alone, to burn his own life for five years so he'd never have to draw. The way he'd decided, alone, to stand still in the middle of the Dial.

"You lie so well," I said.

He didn't answer.

"You lied to her for six hours. You lied to me for five days, by shutting a door. You lied to the whole Meridian for three years with a cuff on your arm." I heard my voice start to shake and didn't stop it. "How am I supposed to know when you've stopped?"

"Tamsin—"

"You keep *deciding*. For me. What I can carry. Who gets bought and who gets left. What I'm allowed to know." I picked up the folder off the velvet and held it against my chest, the way I'd held the books in the corridor against Lysander. "Stop deciding what I can bear."

He took a step toward me. I took a step back. He stopped.

"I'm taking this," I said. "The lists. If you want them back, you can ask."

"Keep them," he said quietly. "They were always for you."

I walked past him to the door. It opened for me before I touched it.

"I set it to your hand," he said, behind me. "The night after Tallow Lane. So you could come in. Whenever you wanted. Even if I wasn't here."

I stopped in the doorway with my back to him.

"I know," I said. "You should have told me that too."

I went down the stairs.

---

I didn't go back to the Hall. Not straight away. I went down to the bottom of the tower and out into the court, and stood under the clock in the cold with the folder under my coat, and looked up at the iron hand pointing at eleven, and didn't think of anything at all for a long time.

Then I went to find Hollis.

I gave him the folder in the library, at the dusty table at the back, and told him what it was. All of it. Not about me. Not yet. But the lists, and the Keeper, and Broker Seven. He read every page with his long freckled face going whiter and whiter, and when he'd finished, he closed it very carefully and sat with both hands flat on top of it, the way Vadra had sat with her hand on the engraving of the Founders' Well.

"Grandmother Thane," he said, very quietly. "*Grandmother Thane.*"

"Yes."

"I used to sit on her knee. At christenings. She gave me violets." He swallowed. "Tam. What do we *do* with this?"

"I don't know yet."

He looked at me. "That sounds very like something someone else would say."

I didn't laugh. I put my head down on the dusty table, on my folded arms, and closed my eyes, and he put his hand on the back of my head, lightly, the way you'd touch a cat that might bite, and left it there, and didn't say anything else.

---

At the tenth bell, Rook came down to the Hall and said, "Kettle. Crane wants you."

Bry was sitting on her bed. She didn't say anything. She stood up slowly, the way she did everything now, and pulled on her jacket, and went to the iron door. As she passed me, she touched my shoulder, once, without looking down.

"Don't wait up," she said. "I'll be ages. He's got such a lot of *practicing* to do."

I watched her go up the stairs behind Rook's lamp.

I didn't sleep. I lay in the dark with my hand over my wrist and the thread hot and miserable under my fingers, and Severin's heartbeat in it, not shut away anymore. Open. Right there. Going too fast. He wasn't sleeping either.

The eleventh bell came, and Rook locked the door and sat in her chair by it with the lamp at her feet.

Midnight. One.

At some point I must have drifted, because a hand on my shoulder woke me, and it was enormous, and shaking.

Pell was kneeling beside my bed in the dark, his huge gentle face grey in the count-light from under my blanket.

"Tam," he whispered. "It's two. Rook's asleep in her chair." He swallowed. "Bry hasn't come back."
