# Chapter Three

## A Room Full of Clocks

Steward Rook didn't speak on the stairs.

We went up out of the Tithe Hall, across the corner of the empty court under the stars, and in at a different door, and then up again: up and up a stair that wound inside the walls of the Horologe like the thread inside a screw. Her lamp swung. Our shadows climbed the curved stone ahead of us and fell away behind. Every hundred steps or so there was a landing and a door, and a line of light under some of them, and once the sound of somebody laughing, a boy's voice, careless and warm. Then more stairs.

I counted, because counting is what I do when I'm frightened. At four hundred and twelve, Steward Rook stopped on a landing to breathe, and leaned one hand on the wall, and looked at me with her good eye.

"Final-years have the tower," she said. "He has the top. Nobody else wanted it."

"Why not?"

"You'll see." She pushed herself off the wall. Then she seemed to change her mind about something, and turned back, and the lamp lit her face from below so the milky eye glowed like a moon. "I packed a box for that boy's last tithe, Fenn. I'd as soon not pack another. Whatever he asks you, think about that before you answer."

She climbed on. I stood on the landing for one more breath with my hand around my wrist, feeling the second heartbeat go steady and slow and very close now, close as the next room.

Then I followed her.

---

At the top of the stair there was a door of black wood with no handle on the outside. Steward Rook knocked once, and it opened inward by itself, or seemed to, and she stepped back and nodded me through without a word, and shut it behind me.

The first thing was the sound.

It was ticking. Not one clock: hundreds. They were everywhere. They stood on shelves that ran around the curved walls from floor to ceiling, and on the mantel above a cold fireplace, and on the deep stone windowsills, and on the floor in stacks; tall clocks and squat ones, carriage clocks with brass handles, clocks with painted faces of moons and ships and saints, plain tin kitchen clocks like the one Mrs. Achterberg had across our landing, pocket watches laid open on squares of cloth like shellfish. Every one of them was going. None of them agreed. The sound was like rain on a roof, or like insects in a summer field, a thousand tiny dry steps all walking somewhere at once.

The second thing was the ceiling. There wasn't one, not really. There was an iron grate, and above the grate, filling the whole dark height of the room, were the works of the Horologe.

I had to tip my head back to see them. Gears the size of cartwheels. Gears the size of houses. Great toothed iron wheels locked into one another, and shafts as thick as trees, and somewhere up in the dark a pendulum the length of a ship's mast hanging straight down, perfectly still. Everything was furred grey with three hundred years of dust. Cobwebs hung between the teeth like curtains. Nothing moved. The whole vast engine of it just hung there over our heads, stopped, and under it a room full of little clocks went ticking away as if they could make up for it.

"Most people," said a voice, "look at the clocks first."

He was sitting at a worktable at the far side of the room, under the only lamp. The table was covered in black velvet, and on the velvet lay rows of tiny tools: tweezers, screwdrivers no longer than my fingernail, files, a set of brass calipers. He had a jeweler's loupe screwed into one eye, and in his gloved hands he held an open watch, and he didn't look up.

"I looked at the clocks first," I said.

"No. You looked at the clocks for two seconds and at the door for three, to see whether it opens from the inside, and then up." He set a tweezer down, exactly parallel to the others. "Sit."

There was a chair on my side of the table. I didn't sit.

He did look up then. He took the loupe out of his eye, and without it his face was just a face: young, pale, very tired around the eyes, which were exactly as grey as I remembered. He looked at me standing there in my scratchy grey uniform with my arms folded, and something moved at the corner of his mouth that wasn't quite a smile, and didn't stay long enough to become one.

"As you like," said Severin Thane. "Show me your arm."

"You've seen my arm."

"I've seen sixty-one years and four months of it. Show me the rest."

"There isn't any rest."

He put the watch down on the velvet. He did everything like that, I'd notice later. Precisely, quietly, as if every movement had been measured in advance and he was only carrying out the instructions.

"Miss Fenn. In six hours the sun will come up, and I can send down to the Ledger office for a binder to perform a true reading on my tithe. It's my right. A true reading costs a minute and reads every year a person has, lit, dark, or painted over." He paused. "And then the binder will know what I know, and the binder will write it in the Ledger, and everyone who reads the Ledger will know it too. Or you can show me, and it will stay in this room."

Behind me a cuckoo clock went off, absurdly, a little wooden bird jerking out of a little wooden door to cry the wrong hour three times. Neither of us looked at it.

I rolled up my sleeve.

He picked up the lamp and came around the table.

He stopped close. Close enough that I could feel the warmth of the lamp on my bare forearm and smell the clock oil on his gloves. Close enough that the second heartbeat in my wrist was suddenly not a sound in another room at all but right there, a slow, even drum under my own quick one, so loud I was sure he must be able to hear mine the same way.

He didn't touch me. I noticed that. He bent over my arm with the lamp tilted to rake light across the skin, the way Mother Gannet used to check a count for paint, and he looked at it for a long time, and he didn't touch me once.

"Tallow ink," he said at last. "Good work. The ends are feathered. Gannet's?"

I'd been braced for a lot of things. Not that. "How do you know Mother Gannet?"

"Everyone who needs ink knows Mother Gannet." He straightened up. "How many under there?"

"None."

He just looked at me.

"Ten," I said.

"Seventy-one years." He said it slowly, as if tasting it. "In the Wick."

"It happens."

"It happens once in a generation. And when it happens, the family usually sells it. A count like that is worth a house in the Gilt. Thirty years on the open market. Forty." He set the lamp down on the edge of the table. "You hid it."

"Long counts get noticed in the Wick. Long counts get a knock on the door at night from a broker's men with a black thread and a sack." That was true, as it happened. It just wasn't the reason. "I'm not the first person to ink down. You know I'm not. You knew what it was the second you looked."

"Yes," he said. "That's what interested me."

"And the minute?"

I hadn't meant to say it. It was out before I could stop it, and I could have bitten my own tongue in half.

His eyes came up to mine. "What minute?"

"In the court. You said—" I made myself shrug. "You said my count went up."

"Did it?"

"No. Fresh ink has a sheen, when it's wet. It catches the light like count-light does. From across a yard it looks the same." I'd worked a counter for seven years. I knew how to say a thing so it sounded like the most boring fact in the world. "The binder drew his minute, I sweated, the ink went shiny. You saw a shine."

He considered this for so long that three different clocks struck three different hours.

"Perhaps," he said.

And he let it go. Just like that. He turned back to his table and sat down and picked up a tiny screwdriver, and I stood there with my sleeve still rolled and my heart going like a hammer, and I understood two things at once. The first was that he didn't believe me. The second was that, for some reason, he didn't need to.

"I don't care what's under your ink," he said, bending over the watch again. "I care that you can put it there."

"What?"

"Tomorrow, after breakfast, you have Tethering. Magister Vadra will have every wright in the first-year class draw on their tithe in front of the others. It's tradition. The final-years attend to set an example." He fitted the screwdriver into something smaller than a grain of rice and turned it a quarter turn. "I'm going to cast a stilling on a cup of water. I'll hold it still for ten breaths. That will cost a day and six hours."

He said it as calmly as a man reading out a recipe.

"And your arm," he said, "is going to show it."

It took me a moment. Then it took me another moment, because the first one hadn't been enough.

"You want me to fake it."

"I want your count to fall by exactly what my workings cost, every time I cast, in front of anyone who cares to look. The Meridian expects to see the Thane heir's tithe being used. The Steward's book expects to see it every Seventhday." He set the screwdriver down. "So it will."

"And where does your day and six hours come from? If not from me?"

"That isn't part of the arrangement."

"Arrangement."

"You ink. I don't draw." He looked up. "Ever. Not a minute."

The clocks ticked. Up above us, in the dark, the great stopped engine of the Horologe hung over my head like a held breath.

Not a minute. It was the most extraordinary thing anyone had ever said to me, and I didn't believe a word of it. In the Wick there's no such thing as a wright who doesn't draw. There's only a wright who draws from somewhere you can't see. And a wright who needs the Meridian to *think* he's drawing from his tithe, while he gets his time from somewhere else, is a wright with somewhere else to hide.

Another tithe, I thought. A secret one. Some poor girl in a cellar on the Gilt with a black thread round her wrist, feeding him by the year so his bought tithe can keep her pretty count for show.

My stomach turned over.

"Why?" I said.

"That isn't part of the arrangement either."

"Then I'm not sure there's an arrangement."

That not-quite-smile again. Gone before it landed. "You're bargaining."

"I'm a Wick girl," I said. "Bargaining is the only thing we get to do for free."

"Go on, then."

I sat down. I hadn't decided to, but my knees made the decision for me. I pulled the chair up to the other side of his velvet table, across from his rows of little tools, and I put my inked arm flat on the cloth between us, and I looked him in the eye the way I used to look at a broker who was trying to cheat a widow on her husband's last two years.

"One," I said. "You never draw. Not a minute, not a second, not for an emergency, not because you forgot. If I ever feel you pull on this thread, the arrangement is over and I'll walk into Clerk Penn's office and tell him exactly what the Thane heir's been doing with his time."

"You don't know what I've been doing with my time."

"I know you don't want anyone to know. That's enough to start with."

He inclined his head a quarter inch. It might have been agreement. It might have been respect. It was hard to tell with him.

"Two. If I'm caught, if somebody sees the ink and I go to the gallows for theft from a House, that's mine. You don't know anything about it. But my brother keeps the Grace. All thirty years. Nobody takes it back."

"The Grace was paid into your brother's count at sunset," he said. "By a Ledger binder, with a silver needle, the safe way. Nobody can take it back. Not even me."

I stared at him.

"You can check," he said. "I imagine you will."

I didn't know what to do with my face. So I didn't do anything with it. "Three. You tell me why thirty."

"Because Lysander bid fifteen."

"That's not a reason."

"It's the only one you're getting tonight."

"And how did you know Kit's name?"

He didn't hesitate. "Christopher Fenn. Ten years old. Born with eleven years. Weak lungs. They fill every winter, and last winter a physic in Saltmarket charged you four silver crowns to drain them. He draws maps. The two of you live in two rooms above Tobias Wren's chandlery on Tallow Lane, the second floor, the window that sticks." A pause. "I read the candidate lists a week ago. I wanted to know whom I was buying."

My hands had gone flat on the velvet. I made them stay there. "You wanted something to hold over me."

"Yes."

He just said it. No apology. No sneer. Just *yes*, the way you'd answer if someone asked whether it was raining.

"Everyone in this city has leverage on everyone else, Miss Fenn. I've only told you what mine is, so you needn't waste time wondering." He picked up the open watch again and turned it in the lamplight. "Now you have mine and I have yours, and we can both stop pretending."

"I haven't got yours."

"You've got the arrangement. If you walk into Clerk Penn's office, I'm finished." He said it as if it were a fact about the weather. "That's quite a lot of leverage for a girl who's been at the Meridian one day."

I looked at the watch in his hands, because I didn't want to look at his face anymore. It was a cheap thing. That's what surprised me. Tin case, gone dull, the crystal cracked across in a star. The kind of watch you could buy for two coppers off a tray on Broker's Row. He'd taken the back off it and he was rebuilding its insides, in a room full of clocks, under the dead heart of the biggest clock in the world, with tools that probably cost more than our whole building.

"Why have you got so many clocks?" I said.

He glanced up, as if the question surprised him. "I like to hear them."

"They're all wrong. None of them say the same time."

"None of them are wrong." He set a tiny brass wheel into the watch with the tweezers, and it caught, and something inside began to tick, very faintly, the smallest voice in the room. "They're all keeping time perfectly. They just started at different moments."

He closed the back of the watch. He set it down on the velvet, ticking.

"Time is the only honest thing in Lanthorn," said Severin Thane. "It runs out at the same speed for everyone. It's people who cheat."

"That's easy to say," I said, "with a cuff on."

The room went very still. Not the clocks. The clocks didn't care. But he did. He went still the way the Long-Lived in the gallery had been still: all at once, entirely, as if someone had cast a working on him.

I had looked at his left arm when I said it. I hadn't meant to. The silver cuff ran from his wrist to his elbow, wide and plain and heavy, and it was the only thing in the room that wasn't ticking.

Then he breathed again, and the moment was gone.

"Tomorrow," he said, quite pleasantly, "a day and six hours. You can't ink a day. A day's just a shine at the tip."

"Then I'll dull it."

"With what?"

"Spit and ash," I said. "And a thumb. Like Mother Gannet does when a buyer's rushing her."

He looked at me for a moment longer than I liked. Then he gave that quarter-inch nod again, and turned back to his table, and picked up the next watch from the row, and it was so plainly a dismissal that I was on my feet and halfway to the door before I'd quite decided to go.

It opened inward when I reached it. There must have been a mechanism. Behind me, the clocks went on ticking. Above me, the great dead gears hung in the dark.

I had my hand on the frame when he spoke again, without looking up.

"One more thing, Fenn."

I waited.

"Don't go near the cisterns at night."

I turned. He was bent over the velvet with the loupe back in his eye, the lamplight on the side of his face, utterly absorbed, as if he'd said nothing at all.

"Is that a threat?"

"It's advice," said Severin Thane. "It's the only thing you'll ever get from me for free."
