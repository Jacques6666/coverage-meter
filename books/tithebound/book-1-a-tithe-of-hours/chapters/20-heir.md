# Chapter Twenty

## Heir

He didn't come back.

I waited in his boy's room at the top of the east wing until the lamp burned down to a blue bead and went out, and then I waited in the light of my own arm. I sat on the end of the narrow bed with my back against the wall and my knees pulled up under Celandine Penhallow's silk, and I watched the door. Somewhere below me, Thane House was quieting. Carriages on the drive. Voices in the corridors, fewer and fewer. A woman laughing, high and brittle, on a staircase. And then nothing but the wind against the window and, far off across the river, the faint sound of the Meridian's bell telling the hours to nobody.

At some point I remembered that I couldn't walk out of that room the way I was.

I had the thimble pot in the pocket Hollis had sewn into the seam of the skirt. I got it out, and broke the wax, and looked inside. Enough for one coat. Not four. One, thin, over ten years, and stretched so far that by the end I was scraping at the clay with my fingernail and the last line was more grey smear than ink.

From across the room it would pass. Up close, under a lamp, under the eye of a Steward or a binder or a small woman in black lace with a thumb that brushed once across the top of your count, it wouldn't.

I put the empty pot back in my pocket and sat there in the dark again, with my arm dull and my hands in my lap, and waited for him.

At dawn, a footman knocked.

"The carriages are going back to the island, miss," he said, without looking at me. "Lord Severin remains at Thane House today. He asked that you be sent back with the others."

---

It was the last carriage, and I was alone in it. I sat in the corner with the grey cloak pulled tight over the silver dress and my left arm hidden in my lap under the folds, and watched the Gilt slide past the window in the grey morning, and then the Wick, and then the bridge. Nobody at the corners this time. Nobody holding up lanterns. Just the fog, and the river, and the clock with its one hand on eleven.

In the Tithe Hall, everyone was asleep except Bry. She was sitting on her bed in the plain grey dress that didn't fit, with her boots still on, and when I came in she looked up at me, and at my face, and didn't ask anything at all. She just moved over on the bed.

I sat down next to her. She put her arm around my shoulders. Her knuckles were split and swollen where she'd hit the masked man in the ballroom.

"Did you really punch a man in the stomach in the middle of Thane House?" I said, after a while.

"Twice," said Bry. "The first one didn't count. He was frozen."

---

Severin stayed at Thane House for two days.

They were long days. The story of the Long Night had crossed the bridge before we had, and by the time the Meridian woke up on the first morning, it had grown in the telling. Unwound assassins in the Gilt. Masked men with needles. Tithes dragged screaming into the dark. The Thane heir stilling the whole of Thane House with a single working, to save them all. *Hundreds* of people. *Minutes*, some said; *a quarter of an hour*, said others. Nobody had seen anything like it in a hundred years. There was a new song by supper. It didn't rhyme *Severin* with *never win* anymore.

And on the second day, the Ledger struck back.

Greycoats in the Wick. Not three or four, going politely from shop to shop the way Gannet had said, but dozens of them, in squads, kicking in doors on Broker's Row and Candle Row and down into the Sump, looking for the Unwound. Wat Sorrel brought the news to the library at study hour, with his weasel's face gone grey: his mother's court searched, twenty men taken from the Candleworks in one morning, a black-thread binder dragged out of a cellar in Saltmarket by his hair. Captain Marr was leading the raids. Wat said he looked like a man who hadn't slept since midsummer.

"What about the Row?" I said. "Gannet's?"

"Shut," said Wat. "Shuttered up tight. Nobody's seen her." He looked at me. "And your Kit's fine. My mam says there's a man in the room over the pie shop who doesn't come out, and when the Greycoats came up Tallow Lane, he did come out, and he said something to the sergeant, and they went away again." He shrugged. "So someone's looking after him."

I put my hand over my wrist.

And the Ledger posted a decree, on the door of the Refectory, in Clerk Penn's copperplate. *Until further notice, by order of the Keeper, no tithe of the Meridian may leave the island for any reason.* The laundry cart would be searched. The gatehouse would be manned day and night.

I stood in front of it with my empty ink pot in my pocket and read it three times.

---

He came back on the third morning. I saw him from the court: a black carriage on the bridge, and then him, getting out of it at the gatehouse and crossing the flagstones toward the tower with his hands behind his back, not looking left or right.

I felt him before I saw him. His heartbeat, coming back into my wrist from wherever it had been. And then I felt something I'd never felt from him before, not even in the first weeks, when I'd thought he was a monster with a cellar.

It was far away. Deliberately. Like a door being held shut from the other side.

In class that afternoon, in the Lantern Theatre, he sat in the top row, in the empty space, and didn't look at me. When Magister Vadra called on him, he went down to the circle and cast a quickening on a wilted rose, two days and four hours, and I stood beside him with my hand clamped to my wrist and faked it, ash and spit and thumb, with the last scraping of ink under my nail. His glove was on my wrist the whole time. His fingers didn't tighten. He didn't say *breathe*. When it was done, he let go of me and went back up the stairs without a word.

That night, Rook didn't come down at the ninth bell.

The next night, she didn't either.

On the third night I climbed the tower myself, six hundred and twelve steps in the dark, and stood at the top in front of the black door, and waited for it to open.

It didn't.

I put my hand flat on it. The wood was cold. Behind it, very faintly, I could hear the clocks, hundreds of them, ticking. And under them, through the thread, his heartbeat, so close I could have reached through the door and touched it, slow and even and shut away from me.

I stood there for a long time. Then I went back down.

---

"He went to her," said Bry, in the dark. "The morning after. Didn't he."

I didn't answer.

"Everyone's saying it. Six hours, he was in her study. With the door shut. And then he went back to his old rooms and nobody saw him for two days." She was quiet. "What did he say to her, Tam?"

"I don't know."

"He told you he'd make it so nobody ever looked at your count again." Bry's voice was very careful. "That's what you said. When you came back. *He said he'd make certain.*"

"I know what he said."

"There's two ways to make sure nobody looks at something, Tam. You hide it." She paused. "Or you hand it over to somebody who'll lock it up where nobody else can see."

I lay there with my hand over my wrist and didn't say anything at all.

---

Lysander found me on the fifth day.

I was alone in the long corridor outside the Lantern Theatre, coming back from an errand to the library with a stack of books for a Lisle wright whose own tithe had a fever. The corridor was empty. That was my mistake. I'd forgotten, in five days of feeling a door held shut through my wrist, to watch for the other thing.

He stepped out of a window bay in front of me, golden in the grey afternoon light, smiling.

"Little tithe," he said. "I've been hoping to catch you alone."

I stopped. I didn't drop the books. I held them against my chest like a shield, and I looked past him at the end of the corridor, at the stairs, and calculated how far.

"Oh, don't," said Lysander. "I'm not going to hurt you. I'm not even going to touch you. My cousin was very clear on that point, in front of a great many people, and I'm a reasonable man." He leaned his shoulder against the wall, easy, relaxed, a cat in a patch of sun. "I only want to talk."

"I don't."

"No, I imagine not. He's not talking to you either, is he? I can tell. You've got that look. Like a dog that's been left out in the rain." He tilted his head, studying me. "Did he tell you? What Grandmother promised?"

I said nothing.

"No. Of course he didn't." Lysander smiled. "She's got a little contest going. Has had for years. Ten, I think. Ever since she lost something very precious to her." He examined his fingernails. "Nobody knows what it was. Something old. Something she'd had for a very long time, and then one winter, it started to go missing. A little at a time. And she's been looking for it ever since." He looked up at me. "And she's promised that whoever brings it to her gets the Ledger."

The corridor was very cold.

"The Ledger," I said.

"The Ledger. The heirship of House Thane. Everything. My cousin was the heir by blood, of course, but blood's very negotiable at Thane House. Grandmother's made that clear for years." He shrugged. "I thought I was winning, I'll be honest. I've been very diligent. Very hardworking. I've worked my way through a great many long counts on her behalf, looking for the one that doesn't fall." He said it the way you'd say *I've been taking riding lessons*. "And then my cousin goes to her study the morning after the Long Night, the morning after he stood in her ballroom holding up a Wick girl with one arm and the whole of Thane House with the other, and he's in there for six hours." He smiled, slow and sweet. "And he comes out looking like a man who's sold something."

"He wouldn't."

"Wouldn't he?" Lysander pushed off from the wall. He didn't come closer. He just looked at me, with real curiosity, as if I were a puzzle he'd been given and was enjoying. "He bought you for thirty years, little tithe. The most anyone ever paid. The day after Grandmother's list came out." His smile widened. "What do you think he bought you *for*?"

I couldn't answer. My mouth wouldn't work.

He watched me not answer for a moment, with great satisfaction. Then he gave me a little bow, the kind you'd give a lady on a dance floor, and stepped back into the window bay to let me pass.

"Do give my love to Kettle," he said. "Tell her I'll be wanting her tonight. Tenth bell." He smiled. "She's got such a lot left to give."

---

He was at the Ledger annex the next afternoon. I saw him cross the court from the library window: black coat, hands behind his back, through the green door by the gatehouse where Wat Sorrel copied the weighing books. An hour, at least. Wat said they kept the old registers in the back, and the Thane heir sometimes came to read them, and sat for hours.

I went up the tower.

I didn't think about it. If I'd thought about it, I wouldn't have gone. I went up the six hundred and twelve steps as fast as I'd ever climbed them, with my heart going and my hand clamped over my wrist as if I could keep him from feeling it, and at the top I stood in front of the black door and put my hand flat on it.

It opened.

I stood there with my hand still raised. It had never opened for me, not when he wasn't there, not once. It had stayed shut against me for five days. And now, with him on the other side of the island in the Ledger annex, it swung inward at the touch of my palm as easily as if it had been waiting.

I went in.

The clocks were ticking. The great dead gears hung in the dark. The velvet table was bare except for the slate and the grey book of the Table of Hours, closed, with a cup on top of it, empty, as if someone had been sitting there drinking tea alone.

I didn't let myself look at any of it. I went to the table and pulled open the drawer.

Liesl's watch. Twenty past four. I didn't touch it.

Under it, there was a folder. Black leather, thin, tied with a ribbon. I'd never seen it before. I picked it up and untied the ribbon, and my hands were shaking so much it took three tries.

Inside, there were pages. A dozen, maybe more. All in his handwriting, small and upright and very black. And each one was a list.

They were dated. Ten of them, one for each year, going back. Each one headed with the year and the words *Market, long counts*. And under that, names. Wick names, five or six to a page. A count beside each name. And beside each count, a column with a heading in the same small upright hand.

*Falls?*

And under it, beside every name, *yes*.

And then, beside some of the names, a second note, in the margin. A date. And two words.

*Broker Seven.*

I turned the pages. I couldn't stop. Ten years. Corin Ashe, sixty-five, *falls? yes*, *Broker Seven*. Teodor Brack, sixty-two, *yes*, *Broker Seven*. Saskia Venn, seventy. Hen Pallister. Mira Holt. Every name on Wat's list was here, every one, in his hand, with *yes* beside it and the date they'd died.

The last page was this year's.

*Market, long counts.*

*J. Mallory. Sixty-six years. Falls? Yes.* And in the margin, in the same hand: *Broker Seven*, and the date of the night Joss walked barefoot out of the Tithe Hall.

*A. Rusk. Sixty-three years, five months. Falls? Yes. Broker Seven.*

And under them, one more line. The last line on the last page. The ink was different from the rest: darker, fresher, as if it had been written in the last few days.

*T. Fenn. Seventy-one years, four months.*

*Falls?*

And beside it, in his small upright hand, the answer.

*No.*
