# Chapter Nineteen

## Seventy-One Years, Four Months

She clapped five times. I counted.

The sound of it carried down the whole length of that frozen room, small and dry and perfectly clear, like someone tapping a spoon on a glass to call a table to order. When she stopped, the silence afterward was worse.

"Bravo, darling," said Lady Aurelia Thane. "*Bravo.* I haven't seen a stilling like that since your great-grandfather, and he had to sit down for a week afterward." She tilted her head, and the silver clock hands of her diadem caught the gold light from the fountain. "Now do let them go, before somebody's heart gives out. Half these people are older than the house."

Severin didn't move. His arm was still raised above his head. I could feel it shaking against my back, through the velvet.

"Severin," said his grandmother, gently, the way you'd speak to a child who's holding something sharp. "Let them go."

He let them go.

It wasn't like the working going out. It was like the ballroom coming *in*, all at once, crashing back into itself the way water crashes back when a dam breaks. The screaming started again exactly where it had stopped, mid-shriek, so that the woman who'd been calling a name finished the second half of it into the sudden light. The crystal flute that had been hanging in the air fell the last three feet and smashed. The gold streams from the silver woman's hands splashed down into the basin. Pell's chair came down on the head of a masked man with a crack like a log splitting. Bry's fist finished going wherever it had been going, and the man she'd hit folded up around it and went down. And Hollis, with his arms spread wide in front of the Vey girl, blinked, and looked around, and said in a high, clear, astonished voice that carried over everything, "Oh, *hell*."

Lysander finished his laugh.

He finished it into thin air, with his golden head thrown back and his hand closing on nothing at all, and then he stumbled forward a step, off balance, at the servants' door with the stairs going down. He turned. He saw his empty hand. He saw me, five paces away, held against Severin's chest.

For one instant, his face was naked. I saw what was in it. Then it was gone, and he was smiling, and smoothing back his hair with the hand that had held me.

"Thank the saints, cousin," said Lysander Crane. "I was trying to get her to safety. In all that dark. Anything could have happened to her."

Severin turned his head and looked at him.

He didn't let go of me. He didn't raise his voice. But all around us, people who'd been running and shouting a moment ago went quiet one after another, the way the Refectory had gone quiet, and turned to look.

"Touch her again," said Severin, very clearly, "and I'll still your heart."

Lysander's smile didn't move. But it stopped reaching his eyes.

---

Then there were lights. Footmen with lamps, running from every door, and Thane guards in black and silver coming in behind them with drawn swords, and the masked men were on the floor, all of them, some still and some groaning, and the guards were dragging them up and pulling the cloth off their faces. They were Wick faces. Ordinary ones. Men I could have passed on the Row any day of the week. One of them, I was nearly sure, had sold sausages at the bottom of Candle Row when I was a girl.

Lady Aurelia came down off her dais.

She walked through her ballroom slowly, with a footman holding a lamp at her elbow, and the crowd drew back from her as she passed. She stopped beside one of the masked men, who was on his knees between two guards with his hands tied behind him, and looked down at the white slash across the cloth that had been pulled off his face and was hanging around his neck.

"Unwound," she said. Sadly. Softly. As if she were sorry for him. "In my own house. On the Long Night." She shook her head, and the gold through her lace sleeves rippled. "Take them to the Ledger's cells. We'll find out who sent them."

"*Nobody sent us,*" said the man on his knees. "*It was the Unwound. Free the tithes. We were told—*"

The guard beside him hit him across the mouth with a gloved fist, and he stopped.

*We were told.*

Lady Aurelia didn't seem to have heard. She'd already turned away. She was looking at us.

At me.

I was still standing in the circle of Severin's arm. My hands were shaking. All of me was shaking, in great hard shudders that came up from my knees, and I couldn't make it stop, and my left arm, with my count under the silver lace and the ink, was pressed tight between my body and his.

Under the ink, I could feel it filling. Slowly. Like a bucket. The last of the years I'd poured into him, coming home.

Her warm brown eyes moved over me. Over my face. Over my shoulder. Over my arm. They rested there, on the silver lace at my elbow, on the grey ink below it, for a long moment.

"Your little Wick girl looks faint, Severin," said Lady Aurelia Thane. "Such a frightening night for her. Take her upstairs. Your old rooms are made up." She smiled, both dimples. "I'll send up some tea."

---

His old rooms were at the top of the east wing, at the end of a long cold corridor hung with portraits of the Long-Lived. He didn't speak all the way there. He walked fast, with his hand around my wrist, and I walked fast beside him, and the eyes in the portraits followed us down the hall.

He opened a door at the end and pulled me through it and shut it behind us. Then he turned the key in the lock, and stood with his back against the door and his head tipped back against the wood and his eyes closed.

It was a boy's room. That was the first thing I saw, in the light of the single lamp somebody had left burning on the desk. A narrow bed with a dark blue cover. A window looking out over the whole of the dark city. Shelves on every wall, and on the shelves, clock parts: gears and springs and little brass wheels, rows of them, sorted by size, the way a child sorts treasure. A desk scored all over with initials cut into the wood with a penknife, and among them, in one corner, small, two letters twined together. *L.V.*

Severin opened his eyes.

"Take it off," he said.

I looked at him.

"The ink." His voice was quiet and hoarse and very steady. "All of it. Please."

There was a washstand in the corner with a jug and a basin and a cloth. I went to it. I poured the water, which was so cold it hurt, and dipped the cloth, and then I stood there with the dripping cloth in my hand for a long moment, looking at my arm in the lamplight. Sixty years and ten months, by Rook's book. Thin coats, feathered ends. Nine weeks of growing old by hand.

I started at the elbow.

Tallow ink doesn't want to come off. I've said that. It came off anyway, the way it had on the landing on Tallow Lane the night I sold to Pruett, slowly, smearing, grey on the cloth and grey in the water and then, underneath it, gold. Line by line. The top one first. Then the next. Then the next. And the room got brighter with every one, the gold light spreading across the shelves of clock parts and the scored desk and the dark blue cover of the narrow bed, until there wasn't any need for the lamp at all.

I scrubbed the last of it off the tip of my count, where the ash and the spit had been, and put the cloth down in the basin, and turned around, and held my arm out to him.

Seventy-one years. Four months. Not a day less.

The light of it lay across his face.

He didn't come closer. He stood against the door and looked at my arm for a long time, the way he'd looked at it in the tower the first night with the lamp tilted across it, the way he'd looked at the inside of a watch through his loupe. Then, slowly, without taking his eyes off it, he reached across with his right hand and flicked the clasp of his silver cuff, and slid it off, and let it drop to the floor with a heavy clink.

He held out his own arm in the gold light from mine.

All that grey. All that dark, from the elbow down, rung after rung. And at the wrist, near the thread, a row of gold.

Seven lines. And two notches.

"I was at nine," said Severin. "When we walked into that ballroom. Nine years and some months. I held that room for five." He looked at his arm. "I should have four left."

I didn't say anything.

"You gave me three," he said. "Didn't you. Three years. Maybe more."

"Yes."

"And your count didn't move."

"It moved," I said. "It went down. And then it came back." I swallowed. "It always comes back."

---

I told him everything.

I sat on the end of his narrow boy's bed with my arm lit up in my lap, and he sat on the floor with his back against the door and his own arm bare beside him, and I told him. All of it, the way I'd planned to tell it at the velvet table in the tower, except that I'd thought I'd tell it calmly and in order, and instead it came out of me in pieces, too fast, tumbling over itself.

Seventy-one years and four months, since I was nine. The February night our mother kissed me on the forehead and told me to mind the baby and walked out into the fog. My wrist burning in the dark, hot and then cold, like a hand closing around it. The morning after. Every morning after. Never a day less.

The first time I sold, for Kit's lungs, at a broker's in Saltmarket, and threw up in the yard, and woke up whole. Fifteen years since then, under six names, in four districts, and every one of them home by breakfast. Pruett. Gannet's warning: Greycoats on the Row, asking about counts that don't drop, asking about somebody who sold the same year twice. *There was a woman like that, once.*

Wat's list. Eleven tithes in ten years, every one of them long, every one in the first term. And the first one the autumn after my mother went into the river.

Vadra's engraving. The woman under the pendulum, chained with silver thread, her arms blazing. The ring of robed figures, drinking. *The Founders' Well.*

My mother's crooked finger.

The iron gate in the tunnel under the river, and the slow beat coming up through the stone, and the thread at my wrist going warm, answering it.

He didn't interrupt. Not once. He sat on the floor with his head back against the door and listened, and the only thing that moved in his face was his eyes, and when I got to the gate in the tunnel, and the beat, they closed, and stayed closed for a long time.

When I'd finished, the room was very quiet.

"I didn't know," he said at last. "I need you to know that. I didn't know it was you. On the bridge, in the tower, I knew there was something. I knew your count did something it shouldn't. I didn't let myself know what."

"Why not?"

"Because of what my grandmother's been looking for." He opened his eyes. "For two years I've known she was hunting something. I've seen her notes, in her study, in the Ledger annex. Lists of counts. Every long count that comes through the Market, every year, and beside each one, a column. *Falls?* And then a yes." He swallowed. "Every one of them, yes. Every name. And then, a few weeks later, a sale to Broker Seven. And a box."

My whole body had gone cold, even in the light of my own arm.

"I thought she was mad," said Severin. "I thought it was an old woman's madness. That she'd lived so long she'd started believing the old stories, the wells and the Drowning, and was looking for something that didn't exist. I thought the tithes were dying because she was mad and nobody would stop her." He looked at me. "I couldn't prove anything. I couldn't stop her. I could only go down to the sluice every time the river groaned and get there too late. And I didn't let myself think, not once, not on the bridge, not in the tower, that the thing she was looking for might be real." His voice cracked, just slightly, down the middle. "Because if it was real, and it was you, then I'd bought you for thirty years and brought you over the bridge and put you in her house. And I didn't know how to live with that."

"You didn't know."

"I should have."

"You didn't *know*, Severin."

"She's looking for you," he said. "Tamsin. She's been looking for you for ten years. And tonight, in that ballroom, she watched you pour three years into me and not lose a minute."

---

I don't know which of us moved.

I've thought about it a great deal since. I think it was me. I think I got up off the end of the bed and crossed that small room in the light of my own arm, and I think he was already on his feet by the time I reached him, and then it didn't matter anymore who'd moved first, because we were both there.

It wasn't gentle. I'd thought about it, I admit, nights in the Tithe Hall with my hand over my wrist, and in my thoughts it had always been gentle. It wasn't. It was furious. I was furious. I was furious because he'd spent five years of his life in a single breath in a ballroom full of people who drank Wick years out of a fountain, for me, and I was furious because I'd given him three and he'd let me, and I was furious because his grandmother had stood on her dais and clapped, and I was furious because Joss and Anni were dead and Bry was down to thirty-one and there was a woman with my mother's hands chained under a clock in an engraving older than the Ledger. And he was furious too. I could feel it through the thread, and through his mouth, and through his hands, one bare and one gloved, holding my face like it might break.

The thread was ringing. I didn't care.

When we stopped, we didn't stop far. He put his forehead against mine, the way he had on the balcony, and we stood there breathing, in the gold light, with his bare arm around me and my bare arm around him, his seven years against my seventy-one.

"You have to leave," he said.

I went still.

"The Meridian. Tonight. Before the sun comes up." His voice was very low and very fast. "Gannet can get you out. Vadra said so, didn't she? A woman at the end of Broker's Row who sells ink. She knows people. You can be on a barge to the south by morning, with a new name, and nobody—"

"No."

"*Tamsin.*"

"No." I pulled back far enough to see his face. "If I run tonight, she'll know by morning it was me. She'll know it for certain, instead of just watching me and wondering. And the first place she'll look is Tallow Lane."

He didn't answer.

"Kit can't run," I said. "His lungs. He can't sleep on a barge in the cold. And Bry's down to thirty-one, and Lysander's going to drain her at the Reckoning in front of everyone. And there's something under that gate in the tunnel, Severin, something with a heartbeat, and it knows me. I felt it." My voice shook. "I'm not going anywhere."

He looked down at me for a long time. In the light from my arm, his eyes were the grey of tallow ink, and something was moving in them that I couldn't read.

"Then I'll have to make certain," he said quietly, "that nobody ever looks at your count again."

"How?"

He didn't answer.

He bent and picked up his silver cuff from the floor, and put it back on over his seven years, and closed the clasp. He kissed me once more, on the forehead, very lightly, the way my mother had kissed me on the last night I ever saw her. Then he turned the key in the lock, and opened the door, and went out into the long cold corridor with the portraits.

I stood in the doorway and watched him go.

He didn't turn toward the stairs. He turned the other way, toward the west wing of Thane House, where the lamps were still lit and the doors were tall and black.

Toward his grandmother.
