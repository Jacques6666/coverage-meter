# Chapter Twelve

## Liesl

He didn't reach for the cuff.

That's what I keep coming back to, when I think about that night. He could have. It was right there on the velvet beside the slate, a hand's width from his fingers. He could have snapped it back on and said *that isn't part of the arrangement* and sent me down the stairs, and I'd have gone, and we'd both have pretended in the morning that I hadn't seen anything.

He didn't. He sat with his bare arm lying on the table in the lamplight, dark from the elbow to the wrist except for those nine gold lines, and he looked at the watch in front of him, and he let me look.

"Everyone's dying," he said at last. "I'm only honest about the rate."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't meant to be."

"Nine *years*, Severin." I couldn't stop looking at it. All that grey. "How many did you start with?"

"Enough."

"How many have you burned?"

"All the ones that are dark." He turned his arm over on the velvet, slowly, so that the count faced the ceiling, as though he were showing me a hand of cards he'd already lost. "Every working I've cast since I was sixteen, I've cast with my own time. Five years. The Meridian expects a great many workings from the Thane heir." A pause. "And some workings are expensive."

I thought of the Dial. The hastenings, the stillings, the gold streaks crossing and recrossing the marble. I'd been inking weeks off my arm while he spent them. Weeks of *his*. I thought of the First Draw, the water hanging in the air like glass for ten breaths, a day and six hours, and the warmth I'd felt welling up out of his wrist. Not mine. His.

I thought of three weeks lying awake waiting for the pull.

"I thought you had someone," I said. My voice had gone strange. "Somewhere. Another tithe. In a cellar on the Gilt, with a black thread on, that you were draining to keep me pretty for the weighing." I swallowed. "I thought you were *worse* than the others. Cleverer. I thought that was what the arrangement was for."

He looked up at me then. And for the first time since I'd known him, he looked surprised.

"No," he said. "No. There isn't anyone."

"There's you."

He didn't answer that.

---

"Why?" I said.

The clocks ticked. The cuckoo jerked out of its little wooden door and cried twice and went back in. Up in the dark over our heads, the great stopped gears hung where they'd hung for three hundred years.

"Her name was Liesl," said Severin.

He said it to the watch, not to me. His voice was very level. The way you'd read a list out loud.

"Liesl Varga. Her mother was a laundress at Thane House. She came into the kitchens at twelve, as a scullery maid. I was eleven." The corner of his mouth moved. "She taught me to cheat at cards. She taught me to swear. She told me I looked like a wet heron when I was sulking and I was always sulking. She was the only person in that house who ever called me Sev." He stopped. "She had a watch. Her mother's. Cheap gold plate, with an *L* on the case, on a blue ribbon. She wore it everywhere. She used to wind it when she was nervous."

I didn't say anything. I didn't move.

"Thane heirs begin training at fifteen. At home, before the Meridian. Workings, the Table, tethers. I was good at it." He said it without pride. "But I wouldn't draw. My grandmother brought tithes to the house for me to practice on, Wick boys and girls she'd bought for the season, and I wouldn't touch them. I'd cast with my own time instead. Little things. It didn't seem like much." His hand lay very still on the velvet. "My grandmother decided I was soft. She said a Thane who can't draw can't hold the Ledger, and a Thane who can't hold the Ledger is no Thane at all. She said I needed to understand what it felt like, so it would never surprise me again."

"Severin—"

"She had a binder thread Liesl to me in the long gallery at Thane House. The day after my sixteenth birthday. Liesl had thirty-four years." He was looking at the watch. Only at the watch. "And then my grandmother came and stood behind me and put her hand over mine. On the thread. Like this." He laid his own right hand, bare, over his left wrist. "And she said *draw*. And I said no."

The lamp hissed softly.

"She didn't need me to," he said. "That's what I didn't know, at sixteen. If you hold someone's hand on a thread, and you're strong enough, you can draw *through* them. Use them like a straw. She took my hand and she pulled. Through me. Everything Liesl had. All thirty-four years. And I felt every one of them go through my arm, and out of my hand, and into her."

I put my hand over my mouth.

"It took about a minute," said Severin. "I watched her count go out. Like a row of candles. One after the other, from the top. She was looking at me. She didn't say anything. I think she was too surprised." He paused. "And then she fell, and the watch hit the floor, and the crystal broke. Twenty past four in the afternoon. That's when it stopped."

The clocks went on ticking all around us. Hundreds of them. None of them agreeing.

"My grandmother let go of my hand," he said, "and fixed my collar, and said, *There. Now you know what it feels like. Now it won't surprise you.* And went to have tea."

He took his right hand off his wrist. He opened the drawer in the table, the one he'd closed so gently the night I'd asked about the *L*, and took out the little gold watch with the smashed crystal, and set it on the velvet between us.

Twenty past four.

"I've been trying to fix it for five years," he said. "Every other clock in this room, I've fixed. Every one. I buy them broken, off trays on the Row, and I fix them, and I make them go." He looked at it. "That's the only one I can't. I take it apart and put it back together and it won't start. There's nothing wrong with it. It just won't go."

I couldn't speak.

"So I don't draw," said Severin. "I haven't drawn on anyone since. Not a minute. Every working I cast, I cast with my own time, and my grandmother doesn't know, because if she knew, she'd do it again. With you, or the next one, or whoever's on the end of my thread. She'd stand behind me and put her hand over mine." His voice didn't change at all. "That's what the arrangement is for, Fenn. She reads the Steward's book every Seventhday. She needs to see my tithe's count going down. So it goes down."

---

I don't remember deciding to touch him.

I remember that his arm was lying on the velvet between us, bare, with the nine gold lines at the wrist and all that cold grey running up to the elbow. I remember thinking that nobody had seen it in five years. That he'd been carrying it around under a silver cuff in a room full of clocks, alone, with a watch that wouldn't go. And then my hand was on it.

Just laid there. Flat. Over the dark lines, below the elbow, my ink-stained fingers on his skin.

It was the first time I'd touched him. Really touched him. Not his glove on my wrist, not the binder's needle between us, not his coat brushing my shoulder. Skin.

The thread lit up.

I mean it *lit*. I felt it, a jolt of warmth at my wrist that ran up my arm and into my chest, and his heartbeat, which had been a drum in another room for six weeks, was suddenly right there under my palm, under my own, the two of them so close together I couldn't tell which was which. I heard him breathe in. Sharp. As if I'd hurt him.

"Don't," he said.

He didn't pull away.

I didn't move my hand. He didn't move his arm. We sat there in the lamplight with the clocks ticking and my palm on his dying count, and neither of us said anything for a long time.

"There was blood on your glove," I said at last, quietly. "The morning they found Joss."

"Yes."

"Tell me."

He closed his eyes.

"I've been watching the cistern gate for three years," he said. "At night. Since the year after I came here. Since I saw the first list." His eyes stayed shut. "The sluice opens from the inside. When it opens, the river makes a sound under the island. You can hear it from up here, if you're listening. A kind of groan." He was quiet for a moment. "I heard it, the night Joss went. Near two. I went down. I got there too late. He was already on the grate. I tried to pull him off it, in case—" He stopped. "In case. I cut my hand on the bars."

"And you left him there."

"If a Greycoat had found the Thane heir in the cistern at two in the morning holding a dead tithe, what do you think the Ledger would have written down?"

I didn't answer.

"I always get there too late," said Severin. "Every year."

"Who's doing it?"

He opened his eyes and looked at me, and I knew he was going to lie before he did it.

"I don't know."

"You've got a guess."

"Guesses get people killed. You said so yourself. On the Dial." He looked down at my hand on his arm. "I'm not going to guess at you, Tamsin."

My name. My real name, the whole of it. Nobody had called me Tamsin since my mother.

Something in my chest turned over and didn't turn back.

---

He moved first, in the end. Not away. He turned his arm over, slowly, under my hand, so that my palm slid down off the dark lines and came to rest on his wrist, on the nine gold ones, on the silver thread. And then his bare fingers closed, very lightly, around my wrist in return. Around my thread. Around my count, ink and all.

We sat like that. Each holding the other's wrist. Like two people taking each other's pulse.

"Now you," said Severin.

I went still.

"What's your count?" His voice was very quiet. "Your real one."

There it was. The question I'd been waiting for since the bridge. Since the Tithe Court. Since he'd leaned down at the threading and said *I watched it go up*.

I could tell him. I could feel how easy it would be, right there, with his pulse under my fingers and mine under his. I could say *it doesn't move, Severin. It hasn't moved since I was nine. I sold a year to Pruett and it came home before morning and I've sold fifteen and they all came home and I don't know what I am.* I could put it down on the velvet between us next to Liesl's watch. He'd shown me his. It would only be fair.

*Somebody's hunting long counts.*

*If I wanted one particular thing, and I didn't know who had it—*

*Your arm is my arm. If you're caught, I'm caught.*

*She reads the Steward's book every Seventhday.*

His grandmother. Small and sweet, with dimples, eating candied violets, and her thumb brushing once across the top of my count. *Seventy-one. Forgive me.*

"Seventy-one years, four months," I said. "You've seen it."

He didn't move. "And tomorrow?"

"Seventy-one years, four months," I said, "less a day."

He looked at me for a long time. The lamp lit his face from one side, and his eyes were the grey of tallow ink, and I knew that he knew. I could feel him knowing it, through the thread, through his fingers on my wrist. He knew I was lying, and he knew I knew he knew, and he looked at me with an expression I didn't understand at all, something that might have been hurt and might have been patience and might have been something else entirely.

"All right," said Severin.

That was all. *All right.* He let it go. He let *me* go, his fingers opening around my wrist, and he picked up his cuff from the velvet and slid it back on over his dying arm and closed the clasp, and the nine gold lines disappeared under silver.

I'd have rather he shouted.

---

"Go," he said. "It's late. Rook will have locked the door an hour ago, and I'll have to walk you down and knock her up, and she'll look at me the way she looks at me."

"How does she look at you?"

"Like she's measuring me for a box." He stood, and picked up the lamp. "Come on."

I stood too. My legs felt strange. My hand felt strange; I could still feel his pulse in it, as if I'd brought some of it away with me. I looked at Liesl's watch, lying on the velvet with its broken crystal and its hands stopped at twenty past four, and I wanted to say something about it, and I didn't know what.

He was at the door. It swung open for him by itself.

And somewhere far below us, under the tower, under the island, the river groaned.

It came up through the stone and through the floor, so low that I felt it in my feet before I heard it: a long, deep, grinding sound, like a great door opening slowly on old hinges, with the whole weight of the Sallow behind it. The little clocks on the shelves trembled. A pocket watch slid an inch across the windowsill.

Severin stopped dead in the doorway with the lamp in his hand.

I'd never seen his face do what it did then. All the color went out of it at once.

"The sluice," he said.

And then, before either of us could move, the alarm bell began to toll.

It came from right above us, through the grate, through the dead gears, so loud in that room that the clocks rang with it and I put my hands over my ears. Huge. Bronze. Rolling out over the island and the river and the sleeping Wick, one stroke after another after another.

*Anni's the longest now,* Pell had said.

I was already running for the stairs.
