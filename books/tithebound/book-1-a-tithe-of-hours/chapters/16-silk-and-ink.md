# Chapter Sixteen

## Silk and Ink

The lessons started again the night after Tallow Lane.

He didn't explain. Rook came down at the ninth bell with her mouth twisted and said "Fenn. The tower," and I went, and the grey book was open on the velvet table to the page where we'd stopped, and there was a cup of tea at my elbow, still hot this time. He didn't say anything about Lysander, or the story, or why he'd changed his mind. He just said, "A quickening. A wound the length of a finger, half an inch deep," and I said, "Three days, four hours," and he said, "Good," and we went on from there.

Something had changed, though. I could feel it in the thread. His heartbeat didn't stay far away anymore. It sat right there under mine, close, steady, all evening, and some nights when I looked up from the page I'd find him already looking at me, and he wouldn't look away as quickly as he used to.

We didn't talk about Tallow Lane. We didn't talk about the iron gate in the tunnel, or the slow beat in the stone, or the circle with the heart in it on Kit's map. We didn't talk about Liesl. We didn't talk about his arm. Every night I sat beside him at the velvet table with my sleeve rolled down over my ink, and he sat beside me with his cuff on over his nine years, and we did sums.

On the fourth night, without looking up from the book, he said: "There's a man across the lane from your brother's window. In the room over the pie shop. He'll be there until spring."

I put my chalk down.

"One of yours?"

"One of mine. Not my grandmother's. Mine." He turned a page. "If a grey coat comes up Tallow Lane again, he'll know before it reaches the door."

I didn't know what to say. So I said, "Thank you," and it came out rough, and he nodded once without looking at me, and we went on with the sums.

The Greycoat with the red face didn't come back.

---

Frostmonth came in with ice.

The Sallow froze along its edges, a crust of grey ice creeping out from the banks a little further every night, and the windows of the Tithe Hall went white with frost from the inside, so that the green river-light that came through them in the mornings was cloudy and strange, like light through milk. We slept in our jackets. Rook brought in a second brazier and kept it burning all night, and we pushed our beds closer to it, so that the Hall became a little huddle of iron frames in the middle of all that empty stone, with the two cold beds left out at the edges where Joss and Anni had been.

Every Seventhday, Rook weighed us.

"Kettle," she'd say, and hold Bry's arm up to the lamp, and then not say anything for a long while.

Forty-four years, the first week of Frostmonth.

Thirty-seven, the second.

Thirty-one, the third.

Bry stopped talking much. She'd go up the tower at the tenth bell when Lysander sent for her, and come back after midnight, and lie down on her bed facing the wall without taking her boots off. In the mornings she moved slowly, like an old woman, and once I saw her stop on the stairs with her hand flat on the wall and her eyes shut, and stand there for a long time before she went on. She'd been the strongest person I knew. She'd broken my nose when we were seven.

"He's not even using it," she said one night, in the dark, so quietly I almost didn't hear. "Not for workings. He just takes it. Like he's filling a jar. He says he's *saving up*." A long pause. "For what, Tam? What does anybody need twenty years in a jar for?"

I knew what for. I'd done the sum the night Anni went. A stilling over a whole Hall, Steward and all.

"Rook says if you go below ten, you go home," Bry said. "Short. If you can walk." Her voice had gone flat and strange. "Maybe that's the way out. Maybe I should just let him."

"Don't say that."

"He won't, though. That's the thing. He won't let me get to ten." She laughed, a horrible small sound. "He watches. Every night. He knows exactly how much I've got. He'll keep me just above it till the Reckoning, so he's always got some to take. And then at the Reckoning he'll take the rest in front of everybody, for his great working, and the Long-Lived will clap, and I'll go home in a box with a coat and a blessing." She was quiet. "Burn me, Tam. I hope there's a ham."

I lay there in the dark and felt my own count under my sleeve, seventy-one years and four months, and I thought about black thread.

It was easy to buy. Gannet sold it in the back room, on bales; I'd seen it there the night I inked myself by the light of my own arm. A Wick binder could thread two people in a quarter of an hour, if you didn't mind the scars and the one-in-three chance that something went wrong. And then I could pour years into Bry the way Lysander poured them out. Ten. Twenty. All she'd lost and more, and by morning I'd have every one of them back.

And the next Seventhday, Rook would hold Bry's arm up to the lamp and find a count that had gone *up* overnight, and everyone on the island would know that somebody in the Tithe Hall had years to give away.

*Somebody's hunting long counts.*

I lay awake until the brazier burned down to white, listening to Bry breathe.

---

"Tithes," said Hollis, "wear grey."

"I know."

"Grey silk, for the Long Night. It's tradition. The tithes go to Thane House as their wrights' shadows, and stand behind them all evening, and look tragic and decorative, like a sort of elegant fog." He held up one long finger. "*But*. Nobody ever said it had to be *ugly* grey."

We were in his rooms. I'd never been in a wright's rooms except Severin's, and Hollis's were nothing like them. He had two rooms on the third landing of the tower, small ones, the cheapest, he told me cheerfully, because Penhallow paid the lowest fee in the Meridian and got exactly what it paid for. They were crammed with things: books, mostly, and a battered harp with three strings missing, and a stuffed owl in a glass case wearing a tiny knitted scarf, and piles of clothes on every surface. Pell was sitting on the floor by the fire with a basket of pins, looking enormously content. Bry was in the only good chair, with her boots up on the fender, watching, and she'd come. That was the important thing. She'd come, and she'd almost smiled when she saw the owl.

"His name's Lord Featherstonehaugh," Hollis had told her. "He was my great-aunt's. She said he was the only man in the Gilt who never interrupted her."

And now Hollis was holding up a dress.

It was grey. That was all I could say for it at first, because I'd never had words for things like it. It was grey the way the sky is grey just before dawn over the river, when there's silver underneath it, waiting. It was silk so fine it moved like water when he held it up, and fell in long straight folds from a narrow waist, and the bodice was stitched all over with tiny seed pearls in a pattern like frost on a window.

"My aunt Celandine's," said Hollis. "She wore it once, to a ball at the Ostranders' in the Year 274. She was nineteen. She died the next spring." He said it lightly, the way he said everything about Penhallows. "We have a great many dresses at Penhallow House. Our women don't live long enough to wear them out. They're all in a cedar room on the top floor, hanging in rows, like a ghost party." He held it out to me. "Celandine would want somebody to wear it. She was terribly vain. There's a portrait. You can see it in her eyes."

"Hollis, I can't—"

"You can. Pell, the pins." Pell held up the basket. "Bry, tell her she can."

"She can," said Bry, from the chair. "She's going to look like a duchess at a funeral. It'll be the best thing that's happened this year."

I put it on behind a screen painted with herons. It was cold against my skin, and then not, and it fit almost everywhere, as though Celandine Penhallow had been a Wick girl too, all ribs and elbows. When I came out from behind the screen, nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Pell said, "Oh," very softly.

"It needs taking in at the waist," said Hollis, briskly, but his voice had gone a little thick. "And the hem up an inch. You're shorter than Celandine. Most people are, she was practically a lamppost. And the sleeves—" He frowned.

The sleeves came down to the wrist. Celandine had been Gilt. She'd have worn a cuff underneath.

"Tithes show their counts," I said. "Even at the Long Night. Rook said."

"I know. So we'll take them up to the elbow." Hollis took a pin from Pell's basket and gathered the silk at my upper arm, frowning, turning me toward the fire. "And we'll put silver lace at the edge. Here. So you're showing it, technically, and nobody can say you're not. But nobody will be looking at your arm." He stepped back. "They'll be looking at the rest of you."

I looked down at my arm, bare from the elbow in the firelight. Sixty-one years under the grey of Gannet's ink. I'd lay it fresh on the night. Thin coats. Feathered ends.

Thane House. Lady Aurelia. The Long-Lived.

*Seventy-one. Forgive me.*

"Tam?" Hollis was watching my face. "You've gone the color of the dress."

"I'm fine."

"You're terrified."

"I'm fine, Hollis."

He looked at me for a moment longer. Then he knelt down on the hearthrug with the pins and started on the hem, chattering about Celandine and the Year 274 and the Ostranders' ballroom, which had apparently been decorated entirely in swans, live ones, which had got loose and attacked the Lisle ambassador. Pell laughed so hard he dropped the pin basket. And Bry, in the good chair, with her boots up on the fender and her thirty-one years on her arm, put her head back and laughed too, really laughed, for the first time since the First Draw.

I stood there in a dead girl's dress in front of the fire with three people I hadn't known two months ago, and I made a decision.

I'd tell him.

After the ball. After Thane House, after his grandmother, after we'd got through the Long Night with my ink intact and both of us still breathing. I'd go up the tower and sit down at the velvet table and roll up my sleeve and take it off, all of it, every line, and show him. Seventy-one years and four months. Not a day less, not since I was nine. I'd tell him about Pruett and Saltmarket and the fifteen years that came home. I'd tell him about the night my wrist burned. I'd tell him about the iron gate, and the beat in the stone, and the woman in the engraving with my mother's crooked finger.

He'd shown me his. He'd shown me nine years and a broken watch.

It was time.

---

The Long Night is the longest night of the year, and the Gilt celebrates it by refusing to go to bed.

That's how Hollis explained it, anyway. On the solstice, the sun goes down over the Sallow at a little after the fourth bell, and doesn't come up again until nearly the ninth the next morning, and the Long-Lived spend every minute of the dark at Thane House, dancing and drinking and celebrating the fact that they'll see another one, and another, and another.

"It's like a birthday party," said Hollis, "for people who've had too many birthdays. Everyone gets terribly drunk and someone always falls in the fountain."

The wrights crossed to the Gilt in carriages from the gatehouse at the sixth bell. The tithes went with them.

I stood in the cold at the bottom of the Hours Bridge with the others, in the dark, under the lamps, in Celandine Penhallow's dress. Hollis had taken it in at the waist and up at the hem and cut the sleeves off at the elbow and edged them with silver lace like frost. Pell had pinned up my hair with Hollis's pins, and Bry had stood behind him the whole time telling him he was doing it wrong. Over it I wore a borrowed cloak of plain grey wool, against the cold, but the cloak was open, and the silk underneath caught the lamplight and held it, silver under grey, like river ice at dawn.

My count was bare from the elbow down. Sixty years and ten months, near enough, by Rook's book, under ink I'd laid that afternoon in four thin coats, so carefully that my hand had cramped. I had a twist of ash in a pocket Hollis had sewn into the seam of the skirt for me, and a pot of ink the size of a thimble beside it.

All around me the gatehouse court was full of wrights in black and their Houses' colors, furred and jeweled and laughing, their breath smoking in the frost, and tithes in grey behind them like shadows. Bry stood behind Lysander in a plain grey dress that didn't fit, with her shoulders back and her chin up and her face like the side of a cliff. Pell loomed behind Hollis, who was in plum velvet that had been mended so cleverly you could hardly see where. Carriages were drawing up, one after another, black lacquer and gilt and steaming horses.

The Thane carriage was the last. It was black, entirely black, with silver on the wheels, and when it stopped the footman got down and opened the door, and Severin came across the court toward it from the tower steps.

He was in black. Of course he was. But it wasn't the plain black he wore every day. It was a long coat of black velvet cut close, with silver at the collar and cuffs, and the silver Thane cuff on his left forearm polished until it shone, and a silver chain across the breast. His hair had been cut. Not much. Just enough. He looked like the portrait in a long hallway that you can't stop looking at.

He walked up to the carriage. He turned, to look for me, to tell me to get in.

And he stopped.

I was standing under the lamp by the carriage door with the grey cloak open and the dress catching the light. I didn't know what my face was doing. I didn't know what to do with my hands. I could feel his heartbeat in my wrist, and it had gone very strange: one hard beat, and then nothing, nothing, nothing, as if it had forgotten how, and then another.

He looked at me. For a long time. He looked at the dress, and the lace at the elbows, and my hair pinned up with Hollis's pins, and my face. His lips parted as though he were going to say something. He didn't say anything.

For once in his life, Severin Thane had nothing to say at all.

The footman coughed. Somewhere behind us, a horse stamped. Across the court, I saw Lysander turn his golden head and look at us, and smile.

Severin's eyes came back to my face. He seemed to remember himself, slowly, the way a stilled man comes back when the working breaks. He held out his gloved hand to help me up into the carriage, and when I put my hand in it, his fingers closed around mine, hard, and didn't let go even when I was up the step.

"Stay where I can see you tonight," he said, very low.

And then, more quietly still, so quiet I almost thought I'd imagined it: "Please."
