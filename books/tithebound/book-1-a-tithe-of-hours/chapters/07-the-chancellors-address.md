# Chapter Seven

## The Chancellor's Address

Everyone else's count falls on its own. Mine I have to push.

That's what I learned in my first week at the Meridian, more than anything Magister Vadra taught. Every night after the eleventh bell, under my blanket, by my own light, I took my arm and I aged it. One day each night: a little ash, a little spit, a thumb at the tip, so that the shine at the end of my count would look a day dimmer than it had the night before, exactly as it would have if I'd been anybody else. On the night after the First Draw, a day and six hours more for Severin's stilling. I kept the tally in my head the way I used to keep Gannet's books, every hour accounted for, every cost carried forward.

It's a strange thing, to grow old by hand. Some nights I'd lie there afterward with my thumb grey and think about everybody else in the Hall, their counts quietly burning down in the dark without their having to do anything at all, and I'd envy them. I know how that sounds. I envied people for dying.

By Seventhday, my ink pot was a crust and a smear. I'd scraped the last of it out with the end of the brush the night before and fixed the edges where sweat had lifted them, and there was nothing left in the bottom but a grey film you could see the glaze through.

The first weighing was at dawn.

---

We lined up along the aisle between the beds with our left sleeves pushed up to the elbow, the way we'd lined up in the Tithe Court a week before. There were eleven of us now. Nobody had moved into Joss's bed. Nobody had moved his boots, either. They were still there, side by side, toes to the wall. Steward Rook walked past them without looking down.

She had her book open on her arm and a stub of pencil, and she went down the line one by one, taking each wrist and holding it up to the lamp and squinting at it with her good eye while the milky one roamed over your face. She read each count aloud as she wrote it.

"Darrow. Forty-nine years, two months. Unused."

"Penhallow doesn't draw, Steward," said Pell, apologetically, as if it were his fault.

"I can see that." She wrote. "Rusk. Sixty-three years, five months. Three days light at the tip. Lisle's been at you."

Anni nodded, humming under her breath.

"Kettle." Rook took Bry's wrist and tilted it, and something passed over her face too fast to catch. "Fifty-seven years, eleven months, and a bit. A month gone in a week." She looked at Bry. Bry looked straight ahead at the wall. "Crane," said Rook, very flatly, and wrote it down.

Then me.

Her hand was rough and dry and warm. She held my wrist up to the lamp, and I made myself breathe slowly, in through the nose and out, the way you breathe when a broker's examining your arm for paint and you've got nothing to hide.

The milky eye drifted across my face. The brown one went down my count, rung by rung, from the elbow to the wrist.

It stopped near the top.

"You're damp, Fenn."

I was. I could feel it. The Tithe Hall was cold enough to see your breath, and I was sweating like a horse, a fine slick of it on the inside of my arm where the skin was thinnest. Right where the ink was.

"I can't read through sweat," said Rook. She pulled a rag out of her belt, a grey square of linen that looked like it had cleaned a hundred floors, and held it out to me. "Wipe it."

The world narrowed to the rag in her hand.

Tallow ink is greasy. Rub it hard and it smears like soot on a windowpane, and then you've got grey streaks running down across lit gold, and a Steward with thirty-one years' practice looking straight at it. Don't touch it at all and she'd know something was wrong with my arm, because who doesn't wipe when they're told?

I took the rag.

I patted. The way you'd pat a burn, or a new tattoo, or a baby's face. Small quick dabs, the cloth barely touching, from the wrist up, careful never to drag. I could feel Rook watching me do it. I could feel Bry, beside me, watching too.

"It stings," I said. "Still. From the threading."

"It's been a week."

"I heal slow."

Rook looked at me a moment longer. Then she took the rag back, and took my wrist again, and held it to the lamp.

It held. Mother Gannet's ink, thin coats, feathered ends, dried hard over seven nights. It held.

"Sixty-one years," said Rook, "four months. Near enough a week light at the tip, and a day besides." She wrote. "Your wright's using you."

"Yes, Steward."

"Not much."

"He's careful."

"Careful," said Steward Rook. She said it as though she were turning the word over to see what was written on the bottom. "Hm."

She moved on.

I didn't let my breath out until she'd reached the end of the line. When I did, it shook all the way down.

"You're a terrible liar," said Bry, out of the side of her mouth.

"I'm a wonderful liar."

"You heal slow? Tam. You healed a broken nose in a week when you were eight. I was *there*. I broke it."

"Rook doesn't know that."

Bry was quiet for a moment. Then: "Rook doesn't need to know that. Rook's not stupid." She looked down at my arm, and back up at my face. "Whatever it is, you'd better get better at it."

At the door, Steward Rook snapped her book shut.

"The Chancellor addresses the college at noon," she said. "In the Refectory. Every tithe attends. Clean grey. Clean faces. And if any one of you so much as coughs while she's speaking, I'll have your skin for a tablecloth." Her milky eye swept the line. "She's been told about Mallory. She'll want to see that the rest of you are in good order."

---

The Refectory was the biggest room I'd ever been in, bigger than the Lantern Theatre, bigger than the chapel in Saltmarket where they held the big funerals. It was long and high, with a roof of dark wood beams carved into the shapes of gears, and seven long tables running down it under seven hanging banners: Thane black and silver at the top, then Crane gold, Vey green, Ostrander blue, Marchbank rust, Lisle white, and Penhallow plum, faded, at the bottom near the door.

The wrights sat at the tables. The tithes stood along the walls behind them, in a grey line, like a frame around a picture.

I'd been put behind the Penhallow table, because Severin's place at the top of the hall had no room behind it for anybody but Thane stewards. Hollis twisted around on his bench as I took my place.

"Miss Fenn. You're with the riffraff. Welcome." He scooted along so he was right in front of me. "Best seats in the house. You can see everything from down here and nobody can see you."

"Is it always like this?"

"Only when the Chancellor comes. Usually it's quite cozy. Usually Lysander throws bread at me." He nodded down the room. "Look at the top table. The cuffs."

I looked. Every wright I could see wore one. Silver and gold and enamel, jeweled at the wrist, worked with vines and stars and House crests, each covering the left forearm from wrist to elbow. I'd noticed them, of course, I'd seen them in the Tithe Court. But I'd thought they were only jewelry.

"In the Gilt," said Hollis, "showing your count is the most vulgar thing you can possibly do. It's like discussing your income at dinner. Or your bowels. Cuffs on from the age of seven. I was caught bare-armed in the garden once when I was nine, and my aunt fainted. Actually fainted. They had to bring salts." He tapped his own cuff, the plain dented tin one. "Of course, it helps that most of us have nothing much to show."

"And the tithes have to roll their sleeves up."

"Ah, well. Tithes aren't people. Tithes are *assets*." He said it in a high, plummy voice that was obviously somebody's mother. Then, in his own voice, quietly, not looking at me: "Sorry. That's not funny."

"It's a bit funny."

"It's a bit funny," he agreed. "That's the worst part."

At the top of the hall, a door opened.

The whole Refectory rose. Benches scraped back, all seven tables at once, the noise like a wave hitting stone. Along the walls the tithes straightened. Beside me I felt Anni stop humming mid-note.

She came in with Clerk Penn on one side and two Thane stewards in black and silver on the other, and she was so small.

That was the first thing. I'd seen her in the gallery on Tithe Day, in black lace, eating something out of a silver box, but she'd been sitting down and far away. Standing, she barely came up to the stewards' shoulders. She was slight as a girl, with a cloud of honey-gold hair pinned up loosely, as though she'd done it herself in a hurry and liked the effect, and a round, sweet, pretty face with dimples, actual dimples, in both cheeks. She looked thirty. She looked like somebody's favorite aunt, the one who brings the good sweets at Midwinter and lets you stay up late.

She was two hundred and forty-seven years old.

And she wasn't wearing a cuff.

Her gown was black lace from the throat to the floor, and the sleeves were black lace too, fitted close down to the wrist, and through the lace, from her wrist all the way up her arm and over the curve of her shoulder and, I could have sworn, down across her back beneath the gown, her count was shining.

I had never seen so much gold on a living body. It wasn't a ladder of lines. There were too many lines for that; they ran together into a single glow, packed so close and so bright that the lace over them looked like ironwork laid over a furnace. It was like looking at Lanthorn from the river on a winter night, every window in the Gilt lit up at once.

"Lady Aurelia Thane," said Hollis, very softly, in front of me. "Keeper of the Ledger. Chancellor of the Meridian. Grandmother to the Thane heir, five times removed, give or take." He didn't take his eyes off her. "She shows hers because nobody else can afford to. It's the most vulgar thing in Lanthorn, and every single person in this room wants to be her."

She reached the top of the hall and turned to face us, and smiled, and both dimples came out at once.

"Oh, do sit down," said Lady Aurelia Thane. "You look like a row of tin soldiers. I'm not the Queen."

The Refectory laughed, nervously, and sat.

---

She spoke for perhaps a quarter of an hour. I remember almost none of it, and all of it.

Her voice was warm and low and musical, the kind of voice that made you lean in to hear it better. She talked about the Meridian and its history, and about the Founders, and about how hourcraft was a gift that the Gilt held in trust for the whole city. She talked about the duty of the long to the short, and about how every working cast in these halls was cast, in the end, for the good of Lanthorn. She said *my dears* a great deal. Every so often she reached into a small silver box on the table beside her and took out something small and purple, and put it into her mouth, and chewed it thoughtfully while she talked.

"Candied violets," whispered Hollis. "She eats them constantly. Has them sent from the south by the crate. Grandmother Penhallow says she's been eating them since before the Drowning, which is a lie, but only just."

"And to our tithes," said Lady Aurelia, turning toward the walls, toward us, with her hands spread wide as though she wanted to gather us all up, "the Wick's most precious gift to the Meridian. My dears. I hope you know how deeply we value you. Every hour you give is an hour of Lanthorn's future. Every working your wrights cast with you is a working cast *by* you, in a way. You are the wells from which this city drinks." She smiled. "Not literally, of course. We don't do that sort of thing anymore."

Polite laughter from the tables.

Then her face grew grave, and the room grew grave with it.

"I am told," she said, "that this week we lost one of you."

Along the wall, no one breathed.

"The Mallory boy," said Lady Aurelia gently. "Ostrander's. Such a bright, sweet boy, I'm told. So *eager*. And so very foolish." She shook her head, and the gold through her lace sleeve rippled like a lit river. "He sold what was not his to sell, my dears. A tithe's count belongs to their House for the term. That is the law, and it is a kind law, because it protects you from exactly the temptation to which he gave way. He sold his House's property to a back-alley broker for forty crowns and a black thread, and then, I suppose, he could not live with what he had done." She sighed. "We forgive him. We pray for him. The Ledger has sent his mother a coat and a blessing." A small pause. "And a ham."

Down the line of tithes, somebody made a sound. I looked. It was Bry, with her jaw locked so hard I could see the muscle jump under the bruise.

I stood there with my arms at my sides and my sleeve rolled up and my heart going like a drum, and I thought: *a ham*. Joss Mallory, with his ears and his grin and his gull-voiced girl and his child who was going to be called Ostrander, boy or girl, and a Ledger knot cut on his wrist, and every one of his sixty-six years pulled out of him in a single breath. *A ham.*

At the top table, Severin sat with his gloved hands folded in front of him, looking at the tablecloth. He hadn't moved since she'd started speaking. Through the thread I could feel his heart going, slow, even, controlled.

Too controlled. Like somebody holding a door shut.

"Now," said Lady Aurelia brightly, clapping her small hands together, so that the room seemed to wake. "Enough of sad things. I should like to meet them. The new ones. Do you mind, Magister?"

Magister Vadra, at the end of the Vey table, inclined her head without a word.

And Lady Aurelia Thane came down off the dais and began to walk along the wall.

---

She took her time. She stopped at every tithe. She asked their names and their Houses, and listened to the answers as if they were the most interesting things she'd heard all year. She took their wrists, every one of them, in her small bare hands, and turned them to the light, and looked at their counts, and said something kind. "Forty-nine! What a lovely sturdy number." "Sixty-three. And such a pretty little hymn you were humming, dear. Do you sing in a choir?" Anni went scarlet and couldn't answer. Lady Aurelia laughed, delighted, and touched her cheek with one finger, the way you'd touch a kitten.

Bry, she looked at for a long moment without speaking. Then she said, "Crane's," in a soft, considering voice, and moved on.

Then me.

Her fingers closed around my wrist.

They were cold. That's what I remember most. Not icy, not like a corpse, just cool and dry and very light, like being held by a moth. She lifted my arm to the light that came slanting down from the high windows of the Refectory, and she looked at my count. At the ink. At the lines.

Up close, her eyes were brown and warm and flecked with gold. Her breath smelled of violets and sugar. There was a fine powdering of sugar on her lower lip.

She looked at my count for a long time. I counted. Eleven heartbeats. Twelve.

Her thumb moved. Very lightly, so lightly I barely felt it. It brushed, once, across the top of my count, across the place near my elbow where the last of my ink lay over ten bright years like snow over a road.

The ink held.

"Seventy-one—" said Lady Aurelia Thane.

Everything in me stopped.

She paused. She tilted her head, and the dimples came out, and she laughed, light and silvery, a little embarrassed.

"Forgive me," she said. "*Sixty*-one. My eyes are older than they look."

All along the tables, the wrights laughed with her, the easy, flattered laugh of people who'd been let in on a joke. I couldn't make a sound. I couldn't make my face do anything. I couldn't even make my arm stop shaking in her small cold hand, and I knew she could feel it shaking, and I knew she was enjoying it.

"Sixty-one," she said again, softly, to me alone, and her thumb rested at the top of my count and didn't move. "What a generous count. For a Wick girl."

"Grandmother."

I hadn't heard him come. Nobody had. One moment the top table, the next he was simply there, at her elbow, tall and black and gloved, between her and the rest of the room, close enough that his sleeve almost brushed my shoulder. The thread at my wrist went hot.

Lady Aurelia looked up at him with an expression of enormous fondness.

"Darling," she said.

"She's mine," said Severin.

He didn't raise his voice. He didn't need to. The whole Refectory went still, the way the Tithe Court had gone still when he said *thirty*. At the Penhallow table, Hollis had frozen with his cup halfway to his mouth.

For a moment, Lady Aurelia didn't let go.

Then she did, all at once, lightly, opening her fingers like someone releasing a bird, and she laughed again, and patted my wrist twice as though we'd been having a lovely talk.

"Of course she is," she said. "You paid enough for her. *Thirty years!* I very nearly choked on my violet." She reached up and straightened his collar, the way you'd straighten a small boy's collar before church. He let her. He stood perfectly still and let her. "You always did like the expensive toys best."

She turned to go. Then she turned back, as though she'd just remembered something, and looked up at him with her sweet round face and her warm brown eyes.

"Your last tithe lasted four months, Severin," said Lady Aurelia Thane. "Let's see if this one lasts the winter."
