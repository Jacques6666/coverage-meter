# Chapter Four

## First Draw

"Well?" said Bry.

It was the gray hour before breakfast, and the Tithe Hall smelled of wet stone and wool and twelve people who had slept badly. I'd been back in my bed for perhaps two hours. I'd spent most of them staring at the vaulted ceiling with my wrist on my chest, listening to a heartbeat that wasn't mine.

"Well what?"

"Don't." She was sitting on the edge of her bed lacing her boots, and she didn't look up. "Half the Hall watched you go up those stairs at midnight, Tam, and the other half heard about it from the first half. Joss has been awake since five making up stories. In the latest one you're already dead and he's comforting your ghost."

"I am not," said Joss, across the aisle, with his mouth full of the heel of yesterday's bread. "I was comforting your *brother*."

"What did he want?" said Bry.

I thought about the room full of ticking clocks, and the dead iron gears hanging over it, and the lamp tilted across my bare arm.

"He wanted to look at me," I said.

"Look at you how?"

"Like a clock he wanted to take apart."

Bry's fingers went still on her laces. Then she pulled them tight with a snap and stood up. "If that's a joke, it's a bad one."

"It isn't a joke."

She looked at me for a long moment, the way she'd look at somebody across the ring, trying to work out which hand the punch would come from. Then the iron door scraped open and Steward Rook came in with the breakfast trays, and there wasn't time to say anything else.

While the others were crowding the trays, I knelt by the brazier at the end of the Hall where the night's coals had burned down to white, and I scooped a pinch of fine ash into a twist of paper and tucked it up my sleeve. Then I ran my thumbnail around the inside rim of my ink pot until there was a dark crescent of tallow under the nail.

A day and six hours.

You can't ink a day. He was right about that. A day's not a line, it's a shine, a faint gold breath at the very tip of your count, and when you lose one it doesn't go dark, it just goes a little dimmer, the way a lamp goes dimmer when somebody turns down the wick. To fake a day you don't paint. You dull. Ash and spit, rubbed thin, and a thumb to blend it, and you do it in the time it takes a buyer to blink.

Mother Gannet used to do it with a customer standing right in front of her. I'd seen her do it a hundred times. I'd done it myself, maybe a dozen. Never with a whole room watching.

---

Magister Ines Vadra taught Tethering in the Lantern Theatre, which was a round room halfway up the east wing with tiers of benches climbing its walls and a dome of yellow glass at the top that turned the morning into honey. In the middle, at the bottom of the tiers, there was a circle of bare floor, and in the circle there was a plain wooden table, and on the table there were a candle, a feather, a bowl of water, and a glass jug.

The wrights sat on the benches. We stood behind them, one tithe to each, like servants at a banquet.

I found Severin in the top row, where the final-years sat, with an empty space on either side of him on the bench as if nobody quite wanted to sit close. He was in black again. He didn't look at me when I took my place behind his shoulder. He didn't look at anyone. He was watching the table at the bottom of the theatre as though it were a problem he'd already solved.

The second heartbeat settled into my wrist, slow and even, and I hated how quickly I'd stopped noticing it was strange.

Magister Vadra came in by the lower door. I'd expected someone like Steward Rook, old and gray and hard. She was hard, but that was the only part I'd guessed right. She was tall and straight-backed, perhaps fifty, with iron-dark hair coiled at her neck and a long, severe, handsome face, and she wore a dark green gown with sleeves that came all the way down over her knuckles. When she spoke, I heard it at once, under all the polish: the Wick. The flat vowels of the river flats, ground down and buried, but still there. Like a count under ink.

Joss leaned back from behind the Ostrander boy in front of him and whispered to me sideways. "She was a *tithe*. Thirty years ago. Only Wick-born magister in the whole Meridian. Married a Vey."

"Mr. Mallory," said Magister Vadra, without turning around, "if you'd like to deliver the lecture, I'm sure your wright would be happy to sit in the circle and be drawn on in your place."

Joss went the color of a brick. Somebody laughed.

"A tether," said Magister Vadra, turning to face us, "is a promise you make with someone else's body."

The laughter stopped.

"Everything else I teach you this year is detail. How to pull smoothly. How to pull without scarring. How to measure a working so you take only what it costs and not a minute more. How to feel your tithe's count through the thread so you know when to stop." She walked slowly around the table, trailing one sleeved hand along its edge. "You will be surprised how many of you never learn the last one."

Her eyes went up the tiers of benches, wright by wright, and I saw more than one of them look away.

"First Draw," she said. "Tradition. Every novice draws on their tithe in front of the class, so that everyone in this room understands what is being spent. The working is small. A candle, a feather, a bowl. The cost is measured in hours. I expect you to take hours." She paused. "Not days. Hours. Ostrander. You'll begin."

The Ostrander boy, whose name was Fitch and who had a soft, worried face, went down to the circle with Joss behind him. Joss was grinning, because Joss always grinned, but I saw his hands open and close at his sides.

Fitch Ostrander took Joss's left wrist, very gently, as though it might break. He laid his other hand above the candle's wick. He closed his eyes.

I'd seen time drawn in broker's shops all my life. I had never seen it drawn for a working, and it was different. In a shop, the time just goes: out of your arm and into the broker's book, quiet as a pickpocket. Here, it *went somewhere*. The thread at Joss's wrist lit up silver, and a thin bright line of gold ran up out of his count, through the air, into Fitch Ostrander's hand, and down his fingers, and the wick of the candle quickened: dark, then glowing, then a small, clean flame rising out of nothing.

At the tip of Joss's count, the shine dimmed. Barely. A few hours. You'd have needed to be looking for it.

"Barely felt it," said Joss, loudly, to the whole theatre, and grinned up at us with his jug ears pink, and half the tithes laughed in pure relief.

"Adequate," said Magister Vadra. "Vey."

It went on. A Vey girl quickened a feather into curling, browning, crumbling to dust in her palm. A Marchbank boy stilled the water in the bowl so that when he tipped it, the water stayed level in the air, a glass coin. Each time, the thin gold line ran out of a tithe's arm and into a wright's hand, and each time the shine at the tip of a count dimmed by a few hours, and each time the tithe put on a brave face and walked back up the steps.

It wasn't so bad. That was what I kept thinking. It wasn't so bad.

"Crane," said Magister Vadra.

Lysander Crane went down to the circle like a man walking onto a stage. He'd done something to his golden hair to make it fall just right across his forehead, and he smiled at the theatre as if we'd all come to see him, and I suppose some of us had. Bry walked behind him with her jaw set. In the honey light from the dome I could see the marks on her wrist where his fingers must have been the night before: four small bruises, blue, in a row.

"The feather, Mr. Crane," said Magister Vadra.

"Of course." Lysander picked it up, turned it in his fingers, admired it. Then he took Bry's wrist. Not the way Fitch Ostrander had taken Joss's. He held it the way you'd hold the neck of a bottle.

"Still it," said Vadra. "In the air. For a breath."

Lysander tossed the feather up. And pulled.

The gold line that came out of Bry's arm was not a thread. It was a rope. It came out of her so hard and so fast that the whole theatre heard her gasp, a raw, startled sound, as if somebody had kicked her in the stomach. The feather stopped in the air. And the gold kept coming. A breath. Two. Three. I watched the shine at the tip of Bry's count go out like a pinched candle, and then, below it, one of the little month notches across her wrist bone browned at the edges and went dark.

A month.

For a feather.

Bry's knees went. She went down sideways onto the stone floor of the circle, all of her, the whole strong solid weight of her, and her head hit the floor with a sound I can still hear, and she lay there with her eyes half open, not moving.

Lysander let go of her wrist. The feather fell. He caught it, neatly, between two fingers.

"Mr. Crane," said Magister Vadra. Her voice had gone very quiet. "A feather costs an hour."

"Does it?" Lysander looked at the feather, and then up at her with an expression of great innocence. "I was being thorough. I'll take less next time, Magister. I'm still learning to measure. That's what we're here for, isn't it?"

There was a long silence.

I knew, and every tithe in that theatre knew, what Magister Vadra was weighing. She was a Wick girl who'd married a Vey. He was the nephew of a Crane and the cousin of a Thane. Whatever she wanted to say to him would come back on her, and on her House, and on every tithe she ever taught.

"Indeed it is," she said at last. "Take her up. Gently. Sit her down. Mr. Penhallow."

Lysander walked back up the stairs. He didn't take Bry up. He left her for Pell, who scooped her off the floor like she weighed nothing and carried her to the bench at the end of the tithes' row and sat her down with her back to the wall and stood over her like a mountain, glaring. Bry's eyes were open now, but they weren't looking at anything. Her hands were shaking in her lap.

I'd started forward without deciding to. I was halfway to her when the heartbeat in my wrist kicked.

Not a pull. Nothing left me. It was a jolt, sudden and hot, the second pulse under mine jumping hard and fast like a hammer missing its nail, and I stopped where I was and looked up, and Severin was still sitting on his bench in the top row, perfectly still, looking down at the circle with no expression at all.

His heart was going like he'd run up four hundred stairs.

I'd thought he hadn't felt anything. I'd thought nobody in that room had felt anything except the tithes. And I stood there in the aisle with my hand on my wrist and felt the Thane heir's fury coming down the thread like heat off a stove, and I didn't understand it at all.

"Miss Fenn," said Magister Vadra. "Your place."

I went back to it.

---

Hollis Penhallow went down to the circle with Pell behind him and stood there with his hands in his pockets, looking at the table.

"The candle, Mr. Penhallow," said Magister Vadra.

"Ah," said Hollis. "Yes. About that." He took one hand out of his pocket and scratched the back of his neck. "I find I'd rather not."

A ripple went around the benches.

"Rather not draw?"

"Rather not draw." He said it with the air of a man declining a second helping of pudding. "Nothing personal, Magister. Lovely candle. Very well made. Pell's a splendid fellow, aren't you, Pell? I just don't think I'd care to, today."

"Then you'll cast on your own count, Mr. Penhallow."

"I'd rather not do that either, if it's all the same. I'm saving my time for something important." He considered. "Breakfast, probably. Or lunch."

Somebody up in the benches snorted. Even a couple of the tithes laughed, nervously, and then looked frightened at having laughed.

Magister Vadra's long face did not change. But I thought, though I couldn't have sworn to it, that something at the corner of her mouth was being held very still. "Then you fail the practical."

"Put it with the others," said Hollis cheerfully. "I'm collecting them."

"Penhallows," drawled Lysander Crane from the second row, not quite loud enough to be addressing the room and not quite quiet enough not to be, "can't afford to cast, can't afford to draw, and can't afford to live. It's rather efficient, when you think about it. Like a moth."

Hollis turned around and looked up at him.

"And yet," he said, "here I am. Being alive at you. Right in front of your face." He spread his hands. "Must be dreadful."

And he walked back up the stairs, with Pell behind him beaming like the sun had come out, and sat down in his place and crossed his legs and examined his fingernails, and I decided, then and there, that I liked Hollis Penhallow better than anyone I had met on the far side of the Hours Bridge.

---

"And now," said Magister Vadra, "the final-years will remind us what all of this is for. Mr. Thane."

My stomach dropped through the floor.

He got up without a word. I followed him down the tiers, one step behind, and with every step I could feel my own pulse rising in my throat and his staying exactly where it was, slow and even, as if we were walking down to breakfast.

The circle was very bright under the honey dome. The candle was still burning where Fitch Ostrander had lit it, and the feather lay on the table where Lysander had dropped it, and the water stood in its bowl, and beside it the glass jug.

"The jug, Mr. Thane," said Vadra. "As you like."

He picked it up. He held out his left hand to me without looking, palm up, the way you'd hold out your hand for a coin. I put my wrist in it.

His glove was cool. His fingers closed around my wrist, light and firm, above the silver line of the thread, and the second heartbeat stopped being something in another room and was suddenly everywhere, under my skin, in my teeth.

"Breathe," he said, so quietly that nobody else could have heard it. "You're drawn on. It hurts. Act like it."

Then he tipped the jug, and the water poured out over the edge in a long clear ribbon, and he stilled it.

I didn't see much of the working. I was busy. But I saw enough to understand why people paid for this. The water stopped. All of it. A twisted ribbon of glass hanging from the lip of the jug to an inch above the table, with the light from the dome caught in it, and every drop along its length frozen in the middle of falling. There was a sound like a fingertip run around the rim of a glass, high and sweet and endless. A thin gold light ran along the ribbon from end to end like something alive.

Every eye in the Lantern Theatre was on the water.

Almost every eye.

I had my right hand at my left wrist, where his glove was, the way a tithe clutches at a draw that aches. It's the most natural thing in the world. I'd watched eight tithes do it that morning. And under the cover of that hand, with the twist of ash in my palm and a lick of spit on my thumb, I rubbed the tip of my count down.

Slowly. One breath. Two. Not too fast; the shine doesn't drop in one go when you're being drawn on, it fades along with the working. Three. Four. Feather the edge with the nail, the dark crescent of ink under it to grey the very tip. Five.

I felt Lysander Crane look at my arm.

I don't know how I felt it. I just did, the way you feel somebody behind you on a dark street. I lifted my eyes and he was leaning forward on the second bench with his chin in his hand, watching my wrist, and smiling.

Six. Seven.

Across the circle, by the lower door, Magister Vadra was not looking at the water either. She was looking at my hand.

Eight. I made myself sway. Just a little. Just enough. I let my breath catch the way Joss's had and Bry's had, the little missed-stair gasp, and I put a crease between my brows and closed my eyes for a moment as if it hurt, and I thought about Bry's head hitting the floor, and it was not hard to look sick.

Nine.

And under all of it, under my own racing heart, I felt *something*.

It came through the thread. It wasn't a pull. I knew what a pull felt like now; I'd seen eight of them and felt a binder's minute go out of me in the Tithe Court. This was the opposite. This was warmth moving, a steady trickle, flowing past my wrist like a current past a stone, going somewhere, and not one drop of it was coming out of me.

Ten.

He released the working. The ribbon of water dropped all at once with a splash that soaked the table and the floor and the front of his black coat, and the whole theatre let out its breath.

I took my hand off my wrist.

The tip of my count was dimmer. A day and six hours dimmer, near enough; you'd have needed a binder's eye and a binder's needle to say it was wrong. The ash was gone, rubbed into nothing. My thumb was grey. I curled it into my fist.

Severin let go of my wrist. He set the empty jug back on the table, exactly where it had been, its handle parallel to the edge.

"Thank you, Mr. Thane," said Magister Vadra. "That is what all of this is for."

He inclined his head. He didn't look at me. But as he turned to go back up the stairs, he gave the smallest nod I have ever seen, a quarter inch, no more. Then he climbed back to his place in the top row and sat down in his wet coat as if nothing had happened at all, and his heartbeat went slow and even and calm, and mine took the rest of the morning to come back down.

---

Magister Vadra caught me at the door.

The others were filing out, wrights first and tithes behind, Pell half carrying Bry, Joss chattering at Anni, who was humming. I was almost through when a long green sleeve came down across the doorway like a bar.

"Miss Fenn."

I stopped.

She looked down at my right hand, closed in a fist at my side. Then she looked at my face.

"Your thumb is dirty," said Magister Vadra.

My heart, which had only just started to slow down, kicked straight back up. "I clean the grates in the Hall, Magister. In the mornings."

"Of course you do."

She didn't move her arm. Up close, she smelled of lavender water and something under it, something sharp and smoky that I knew better than my own name, and for one terrible moment I couldn't think where from.

Then she lifted her sleeve out of my way.

"Mind you wash it," she said, "before the Steward sees."

I went down the stairs so fast I nearly fell.

It wasn't until I was halfway across the court, with the stopped clock looming over me and the river wind in my face, that I placed the smell.

Tallow ink.

---

Joss had a good day. That's the thing I keep coming back to. He had a good day.

His wright, Fitch Ostrander, sent him on errands all afternoon and gave him an apple and told him to keep it. Joss came back to the Hall at supper with the apple in one hand and a book for Fitch in the other and his whole face shining, and he told everyone who would listen that the Ostranders were the kindest House in the Gilt, and that Fitch had asked him what Hetty was like, and *listened* when Joss told him.

"I said she's got a laugh like a gull," Joss said. "He wrote it down. He said he might put it in a poem."

"About Hetty?" said Anni, round-eyed.

"About gulls, probably," said Joss, and laughed, and lay back on his bed with his boots up on the rail. "Nine years for her mam. And a poem. Burn me, Tam. I think it's going to be all right."

Bry slept through supper. She woke when Steward Rook came through with the snuffer, and she didn't speak to anyone, and she turned on her side to face the wall. I lay in the dark and listened to her breathing and wanted to break Lysander Crane's beautiful face with my own two hands.

At the eleventh bell, Rook locked the iron door.

I fell asleep at some point. I know I did, because I dreamed of water. Black water, rising, and a sound in it like a slow heart, and my mother's crooked finger tracing circles on the surface, round and round, as if she were winding a clock.

The bell woke me.

Not the hour bell. This one was deeper, a huge bronze note that went on and on and on, rolling over the island and down through the stone and into the Tithe Hall until the iron bed frames hummed with it. I sat bolt upright. All down the Hall, embers of count-light were rising under blankets as people sat up in the dark.

The iron door slammed open. Lamplight. Steward Rook, half dressed, with her keys in her fist and her milky eye wide.

"Count off," she snapped. "Now. Every one of you. Name and House."

We counted off. Kettle, Crane. Fenn, Thane. Rusk, Lisle. Darrow, Penhallow. One after another down the row, in the swinging light, while the alarm bell went on and on overhead.

Somebody didn't answer.

Steward Rook held up her lamp.

Across the aisle from me, the blanket on Joss Mallory's bed was thrown back. The sheet was cold. Fitch Ostrander's apple sat on the pillow, one bite out of it.

And under the bed, side by side, neatly, the way he'd left them when he went to sleep, were Joss's boots.
