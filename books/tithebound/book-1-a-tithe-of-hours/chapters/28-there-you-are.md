# Chapter Twenty-Eight

## There You Are

"No," said Severin.

He didn't raise his voice. He'd never needed to. It went out across the white marble and up the stone tiers and into the gallery under the black silk, and the whole Dial heard it, the way the whole Tithe Court had heard *thirty*.

Lady Aurelia's smile didn't move. "I beg your pardon, darling?"

"No," said Severin again. "I won't draw on her."

"It's the Reckoning. It's your great working. Every Thane heir for three hundred years has—"

"I know what every Thane heir has done." He reached across with his right hand and flicked open the clasp of the silver cuff on his left arm.

It fell. It hit the marble with a heavy clink and rolled in a half circle and lay still at his grandmother's feet. And he held up his bare left arm, in front of the dais and the gallery and the tiers and the whole of the Meridian, in the cold blue morning light.

All that grey. From the elbow down, rung after rung of it, cold and flat and burned out. And at the wrist, by the silver thread, seven lines of gold. Two notches.

The sound the Dial made then wasn't a roar. It was the opposite. It was six hundred people drawing breath at once.

"I've never drawn on her," said Severin. "Not a minute. Not once since Tithe Day. Every working you've seen me cast this term, every one, in the Lantern Theatre and on this Dial and in your ballroom on the Long Night, I cast with my own time." He held his arm higher. "I haven't drawn on anyone in five years, Grandmother. Not since the day after my sixteenth birthday. Not since you stood behind me in the long gallery and put your hand over mine on Liesl Varga's thread."

Up in the gallery, someone made a sound like a sob. I didn't see who.

Lady Aurelia looked at his arm. At the seven gold lines. For a long moment, her round sweet face showed nothing at all.

"Then you're no Thane," she said softly.

"Then I'm not," said Severin.

---

I stepped forward.

I hadn't known I was going to do it until I did. It was the plan; we'd laid it out at the velvet table over lemon cake. But in my head it had always been calm, and orderly, and in my head my legs hadn't been shaking like this.

"Keeper," I said.

My voice came out of me thin and cracked, a Wick voice, flat vowels and all, in the middle of all that white marble. Every head on the Dial turned.

"Before anyone draws anything," I said, "the Dial should see something."

I didn't look back. I didn't need to. I heard the footsteps behind me, heavy and fast on the marble, the only person on the island who ran like that, like a cart horse breaking into a gallop. Pell came across the Dial from the rim with his jacket held closed over his chest, and stopped beside me, and opened it, and put the blanket-wrapped lens in my hands. Then he stood behind me like a wall.

"*What is this?*" Clerk Penn's voice cracked. "My lady, this is *highly* irregular. A tithe cannot address the Dial. A tithe cannot—"

"The right of Echo."

Magister Vadra had risen from her place at the edge of the magisters' tier. She came down the stone steps to the rim of the Dial, tall and straight in her green gown, and stepped out onto the marble.

"Statute of the Ledger, the ninety-first," she said, in the voice she used in the Lantern Theatre, the voice that made wrights put their hands down. "Any matter touching the Ledger, or the conduct of its Keeper, may be shown on the Dial by Echo, before the Keeper and the Houses, by any magister of the Meridian. It's been on the books since the Year 40 After." She looked at Clerk Penn. "I don't believe it's ever been used. But it's never been struck out."

Clerk Penn opened his mouth. He looked at the Ledger in his arms. He closed his mouth.

"*Let it be shown.*"

It came from the six. From the far side of the Dial, opposite the dais. Captain Hesketh Marr had taken a step out onto the marble in his worn grey coat, and his long jaw was set, and his washed-out eyes were on Lady Aurelia Thane.

"Statute's the statute, my lady," said Captain Marr. "I'd like to see it. I imagine the Houses would too."

Up in the gallery, the Long-Lived were murmuring. Some of them were nodding.

Lady Aurelia looked at Captain Marr. Then at Magister Vadra. Then at me, standing in front of her with the wrapped lens in my shaking hands. And then, quite suddenly, she laughed. Light and silvery, the way she'd laughed in the Refectory over *seventy-one, forgive me*.

"Oh, by all means," she said, and sat back down in her tall carved chair, and opened the silver box at her elbow. "Show me."

---

I unwrapped the lens and gave it to Vadra.

She held it up above her head in both hands, to the sky, at arm's length. She pushed back the sleeve of her green gown, and I saw her scars, all those fine white rings and the cobweb lattice above them, and her thirty-one years of gold underneath. And she cast.

Hollis had cast it small, in a little square room under the cistern, at the cost of six weeks. Vadra cast it large. I felt the working go up out of her into the glass like a gust of wind, and the air over the whole Dial began to ring. The lens blazed. And the Echo came up out of it into the open air, enormous, the size of the Dial itself, gold smoke rising over the white marble and filling the cold blue sky.

The little square room. The iron rings in the walls. The stone bench.

Two figures in the entrance. A tall golden one, and a stumbling one in grey, being dragged by the wrist.

Silent. Every one of them silent. But six hundred people on the Dial watched them, twenty feet tall in gold smoke, and nobody needed sound.

They watched Lysander Crane drag Bryony Kettle across the room and force her down against the wall. They watched him lift her wrists to the ring and put on the cuffs and drive the pins through. They watched him laugh, his golden head thrown back. They watched him kneel with the long black broker's needle and thread her, rough and fast, and tie off the knot. They watched him take her wrist and draw, and the gold rope come out of her, and her count go out, rung after rung, from the elbow down.

And they watched the third figure come out of the dark.

Small. Slight. In black. With a cloud of pale hair pinned up loosely, and a little box in her left hand, and her right hand lifting from the box to her lips. They watched her cross the room, unhurried, eating, and stand beside Lysander and watch. They watched her laugh. They watched her bend down beside the drained girl on the floor and reach out one small hand and touch her face, the way you'd touch a kitten.

And they watched her turn her face to the torchlight.

Twenty feet tall, in gold smoke, over the Dial. Round and sweet, with two dimples. A silver clock hand on a chain at her throat.

The Echo faded. The lens in Vadra's hands went on glowing faintly, and inside it, small, the whole thing had already begun again from the beginning.

Nobody on the Dial made a sound.

On the marble near the rim, where he'd been standing since his working, Lysander Crane had gone the color of the snow.

---

Severin stepped up beside me.

He had the black folder in his hand. He opened it, and held it up, and turned so that the gallery could see the pages, and he began to read. Names. In his clear, level voice that carried to the top of the tiers.

"Corin Ashe. Sixty-five years. Leaffall, ten years ago. Sold in full to Broker Seven. Drowned."

The gallery stirred.

"Teodor Brack. Sixty-two years. Sold in full to Broker Seven. Drowned. Saskia Venn. Seventy years. Broker Seven. Fever. Hen Pallister. Sixty-four years. Fell from the Hours Bridge." He turned a page. "Joseph Mallory. Sixty-six. Broker Seven. Annika Rusk. Sixty-three. Broker Seven." He looked up at the gallery. "Ten years. Eleven tithes. Every one a long count. Every one tested and drained in the first term, and sold to the same broker, whose name is withheld under the Brokers' Privilege." He closed the folder. "There is no Broker Seven on the Row. Broker Seven is the number the Ledger uses in its own books when the buyer is the Ledger itself."

He turned and looked at his grandmother.

"When the buyer," said Severin, "is the Keeper."

And Magister Vadra, standing beside him with the glowing lens in her hands, said four words, not loudly, in her buried Wick voice that every Long-Lived soul in that gallery had been taught to fear since before they could walk.

"*Never again the well.*"

It went up the tiers like fire up a dry curtain.

*The well.* I heard it start in the gallery, among the Long-Lived, under the black silk. A woman's voice, high and frightened. Then a man's. *She's been hunting a well. Ten years. For herself.* Then the wrights in the upper tiers, and the magisters, and the servants packed along the top. *The Keeper. A well. Never again the well.* The man with the ear trumpet was on his feet again, shaking it at the dais like a fist. Someone was shouting for the Ledger to be closed. Someone else was shouting for the Houses to convene.

And down on the marble, Captain Marr began to walk across the Dial toward Lysander Crane, with four Greycoats falling in behind him.

---

Lady Aurelia Thane let it go on for a long time.

She sat in her tall chair and ate a violet and watched the whole Dial rise up shouting against her, and she didn't move, and she didn't stop smiling. She let it go until the shouting was so loud I couldn't hear my own heart, until Marr was halfway across the marble, until the Long-Lived in the gallery were standing, all of them, every last one, and turning toward her with their smooth faces twisted.

Then she stood up, and raised one small hand.

And the Dial went quiet. I don't know how. She didn't cast anything. She just stood there with her hand raised, small and slight in black lace on her dais, with her count blazing through her sleeves, and one by one, they stopped.

"*Yes,*" said Lady Aurelia Thane.

Silence.

"Yes," she said again, warmly, sorrowfully, into it. "I have been hunting a well."

Nobody breathed.

"For ten years," she said. "As every Keeper of the Ledger has sworn to, since the Founders stood on the Hours Bridge. To find any well that rises in Lanthorn, and to end it, as the law commands. *Never again the well.*" She laid her hand over her heart. "I have kept that oath for two hundred years. I kept it ten years ago, when I learned that a well had risen in this city again. I have kept it every day since, searching, so that none of you would ever have to know how close we came."

The gallery was very still.

"And I trusted the wrong man to help me," said Lady Aurelia.

She turned her head and looked at Lysander.

He'd backed away from Marr, toward the rim. He'd stopped. He was standing in the middle of the white marble in his honey-colored coat with his ruby cuff on his arm, and his golden face was grey, and he was staring at her.

"My sister's great-great-grandson," said Lady Aurelia gently. "I let him help me search. And he found the hunt too sweet. He killed in my name. He drained children for his own count. He meant to find the well for himself and hide it from me." She shook her head, and the silver clock hands in her hair caught the light. "I gave him every chance to confess."

"*Grandmother.*" Lysander's voice cracked in half. "Grandmother, you *said*—you were *there*, you *told* me—"

"Lysander," said Lady Aurelia. "Darling. Come here."

He came.

I don't know why. I've thought about it so many times. He could have run. Marr was a dozen paces away; the rim was closer. But he came across the marble to her dais as if she'd pulled him on a string, slowly, white to the lips, and stopped in front of her, and she reached down from her step and took his left wrist in her small bare hand. Kindly. The way she'd taken mine in the Refectory.

"Justice," she said.

And drew.

It took one breath. Less. I felt it go past me in the air like a cold wind, a single huge pull, and Lysander Crane's whole count went out. Every line. From the elbow to the wrist, all at once, all together, the way I'd seen it on Joss's arm under the clock on the second morning of the term.

Like a row of candles blown out by a single breath.

He didn't make a sound. He looked at her. He looked surprised. And then he folded up onto the white marble at the foot of her dais and lay there in his honey-colored coat with his golden head on the stone and his ruby cuff gleaming on his blank grey arm, and didn't move again.

Lady Aurelia let go of his wrist. She took a lace handkerchief from her sleeve and wiped her fingers.

"Justice is done," she said.

---

Nobody moved.

Not Marr, standing frozen halfway across the Dial. Not the Greycoats behind him. Not the Long-Lived in the gallery, standing in their furs with their mouths open. At the rim, I heard Bry say, very quietly, to nobody, "I was going to break his nose." And then nothing else at all.

Lady Aurelia put away her handkerchief. She took a violet from the silver box and put it in her mouth and chewed it, slowly, thoughtfully, looking down at Lysander's body at her feet.

Then she lifted her eyes and looked at me.

"And now," she said, "the well."

The gallery stirred.

"Tamsin Fenn," said Lady Aurelia Thane, to the whole Dial, warm and clear. "Of Tallow Lane. Seventy-one years and four months, today. Seventy-one years and four months on Tithe Day. And seventy-one years and four months, I'd wager, every day for ten years before that." She smiled. "Not one day less. Not one hour. You heard the Steward's book read out every Seventhday, a little less each week. That book was a lie. This girl has painted her arm with tallow ink every morning of this term to hide what she is from you." She tilted her head. "And on the Long Night, in my ballroom, in front of many of you, I watched her pour three years of her life into my heir, and lose nothing."

The sound that came out of the gallery then was not a roar. It was worse. It was a hiss.

"*The well,*" someone said. And then someone else. "*Never again the well.*" And it started up the tiers again, the same words, the same fire up the same dry curtain, but it was going the other way now. It was coming toward me. I could feel it. Six hundred faces, turning. The Long-Lived, the wrights, the servants on the top tier. The magisters. The binders. Looking at my bare arm, blazing gold in the cold blue light. *Never again the well. Never again the well.*

The words carved over the Ledger House in gold. The one law they actually feared. And she'd taken it out of our hands and turned it around and pointed it at me.

"Captain Marr," said Lady Aurelia pleasantly. "The Founders' law. You know it, I think. The Keeper of the Ledger takes custody of any well found in Lanthorn, to be dealt with as the Founders dealt with them."

Captain Marr stood in the middle of the Dial with his long jaw set. He looked at me. He looked at Lysander's body on the marble. He looked up at the gallery, at six hundred people chanting, at the black silk awning and the Long-Lived under it, who owned every silver button on every grey coat in the city.

He didn't move.

Severin moved. He stepped in front of me, close, his bare arm with its seven gold lines held out at his side like a bar across a door. Behind me, Pell. At the rim, I saw Hollis start forward and two Greycoats catch him by the arms. I saw Bry go for one of them, and three more Greycoats, and Bry go down under them, fighting.

Lady Aurelia came down off her dais.

She stepped over Lysander's body as if it were a fold in a rug. She walked up to us across the white marble, small and slight in black lace, with her count blazing and the silver clock hands in her hair, and she stopped in front of Severin and looked past his outstretched arm at me.

Her face was very close. Her warm brown eyes were flecked with gold. There was a fine powdering of sugar on her lower lip.

"There you are," she said softly.
