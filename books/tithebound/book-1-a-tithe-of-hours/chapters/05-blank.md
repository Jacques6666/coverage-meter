# Chapter Five

## Blank

They didn't let us out until dawn.

The alarm bell stopped sometime after two. In the silence it left, we could hear them searching: boots on the stairs above us, voices calling across the court, the clank of the iron door at the far end of the Hall being tried and found barred, and once, terribly close, a man laughing at something, a short ordinary laugh, as though this were any other night's work. Steward Rook sat in a chair by the main door with her keys in her lap and her lamp at her feet and didn't answer any of our questions. After a while we stopped asking.

Nobody went back to sleep. Anni hummed. Pell sat on the end of her bed with one huge hand over both of hers, not saying anything. Bry lay on her side facing Joss's empty bed with her eyes open, and when I reached across the gap between us in the dark and found her fingers, she held on so hard my knuckles cracked.

I kept looking at the boots.

He'd set them side by side under the bed, toes to the wall, the way his mother must have taught him. Nobody leaves the Tithe Hall barefoot in autumn. The stone floors would take the skin off your soles, and the court outside is cold as a grave by midnight. Nobody walks out of a locked room, either, unless somebody opens the door.

I thought about Severin Thane, sitting at his velvet table in his room full of clocks, saying *don't go near the cisterns at night* without even looking up.

I thought about that for a long time.

---

At first light, Rook stood up.

"Boots on," she said. "Jackets. All of you. You're wanted in the court."

"Have they found him?" said Anni, in a voice like a thread about to snap.

Rook looked at her for a moment with her good eye. Then she turned and unlocked the door, and that was all the answer we got.

The sky over the Meridian was the color of dishwater, and the fog was coming in off the river in long low banks that curled around the base of the colonnade like cats. The whole college seemed to be in the Tithe Court. Wrights in black coats thrown on over nightshirts, huddled under the arches with their House sashes forgotten. Servants. Binders in Ledger grey. And Greycoats, more than I'd ever seen in one place outside the Wick on a riot night, standing in a loose ring around something on the flagstones at the foot of the tower.

Under the clock. Of course they'd put him under the clock.

He was covered with somebody's coat. One of the Greycoats' own, by the silver buttons. It wasn't long enough. His bare feet stuck out at the bottom, and they were white and wrinkled from the water, and the sight of them hit me harder than anything else that morning. Those were the feet that should have been in the boots.

Fitch Ostrander was on his knees beside the body. He was still in his nightshirt, with his black coat hanging off one shoulder, and he was crying the way small children cry, without any shame about it, with his mouth open.

"I gave him an apple," he kept saying, to nobody. "I only gave him an apple."

"Tithes in a line," said Rook. "Here. Where you can see."

We lined up along the edge of the ring of Greycoats. I understood, then, why we'd been brought. Not to mourn him. To look at him. Somebody had decided that the Wick ought to see what happened to a tithe who did whatever it was the Meridian had decided Joss had done, and we were the Wick, and here we were.

A tall man in a long grey coat stepped into the ring and crouched beside the body.

He was older than most of the Greycoats, perhaps forty-five, with grey stubble on a long jaw and the deep, permanent creases of a man who frowned for a living. There was nothing grand about him. His coat was worn shiny at the elbows. But the other Greycoats moved when he moved, the way water moves around a rock, and I knew who he was before anybody said his name. Every child in the Wick knows the name of the Captain of the Greycoats. It's the name you hear when somebody's door gets kicked in.

"Captain Marr," said Clerk Penn, from the edge of the ring, in a voice like a man trying not to be sick. "If you'd be so good as to conclude this quickly. The Chancellor has been informed."

"The Chancellor," said Captain Hesketh Marr, without looking up, "will be informed again when I know something." He reached out and folded the coat back from the body.

Somebody behind me made a small sound. I think it was Anni.

Joss looked asleep. That was the worst of it. His eyes were closed and his face was calm, and his big jug ears were pink with cold, and there was a strand of green weed caught in his hair like a ribbon. If it hadn't been for the color of his lips, you'd have thought he'd only lain down for a moment in the wrong place.

Then Marr lifted his left arm out from under the coat, and turned it palm up, and I saw his count.

I'd seen dead people's counts before. You can't grow up in the Wick and not see them. When the old die, there's nearly always a line or two still lit at the end, a little stub of gold near the wrist, because the body gives out before the time does. When the sick die, it's the same. Even when somebody's sold themselves down to nothing, which happens more often than the Gilt would like to believe, you usually see the last notches still glowing faintly at the wrist, the final few days or hours they couldn't bring themselves to sell.

Joss's arm was blank.

Not one line. Not one notch. Not the faintest breath of shine at the tip. Sixty-six years of gold, and every rung of it gone the flat dull grey of a cold grate, from his elbow all the way down to his wrist bone. I had never seen an arm like that on somebody who'd been nineteen. I had never seen an arm like that at all.

"Blank," somebody whispered down the line. "Burn me, he's *blank*."

"Sold out," said one of the Greycoats behind Marr, a heavy man with a red face, and he said it with a certain grim satisfaction, as though it was what he'd been expecting all along. "Look at that. Sold every hour."

"There's this, Captain." Another Greycoat crouched and held something out: a leather purse, soaked black with river water, heavy. She tipped it into her palm, and silver spilled out, bright against her grey glove. "Took it off him when we pulled him out. Forty crowns, give or take."

A murmur ran around the court like wind through grass.

"There it is," said the red-faced Greycoat. "Broker on the island. Black thread, needle, and a purse. Boy sold everything he had for his girl back home. Then he went in the water." He shook his head. "They do it. I've seen it a dozen times in the Sump. Can't face it, once it's done. Sell it all and walk into the river."

"Where was he found?" said Marr.

"The sluice grate. Downstream side, where the cisterns empty out into the Sallow. Caught on the bars like a leaf."

The cisterns. My skin went cold all over.

"Clerk Penn," said Marr, and his voice was very flat. "Does the Ledger have a record of a sale?"

Penn had a book open in his hands. He'd had it open, I realized, since before Marr arrived. "The Ledger records a sale," he said. "In the night. Sixty-six years. To a licensed broker, name withheld under the Brokers' Privilege. Forty crowns."

"How convenient," said Marr.

"Captain."

"The Ledger records whatever it's told, Clerk Penn. That's its great virtue."

Penn's face went a blotchy pink. "Captain, I'd remind you that—"

I didn't hear what Penn would remind him. I wasn't listening. I was looking at Joss's arm, and something cold and practical had come up in me, the part of me that had stood behind Mother Gannet's counter for seven years and looked at ten thousand counts, and it was telling me things.

Forty crowns.

At Wick rates, the best broker on the Row pays three crowns a year, if you're young and healthy and your count's clean. The worst pays one. Sixty-six years, sold all at once, would fetch somewhere close to two hundred crowns, and no broker on the Row ever carried two hundred crowns in his coat, and if one did, he wasn't going to row it across to the Meridian in the middle of the night. Nobody sells sixty-six years for forty crowns. Not even a boy who wants to die. You'd have to be robbed.

Or you'd have to be dead first, and somebody would have to put the purse in your pocket afterward.

And then the lines.

I made myself look at them properly, the way Gannet taught me. When you sell time, even to a broker with the best needle on the Row, it goes out of you from the top. It gutters. You can always see it on an arm: the highest lines go darkest first, and the ones underneath are browner at the ends where they went out more recently. A count that's been sold down looks like a row of candles that burned out one after another, each a little shorter than the last.

Joss's count didn't look like that. Every line was the same flat grey. Top to bottom. No browning at the ends, no shading from dark to darker. As if every one of those sixty-six years had gone out at the same moment.

Like a row of candles blown out by a single breath.

A broker can't do that. A broker uses a needle and an hour and a lot of patience. To pull sixty-six years out of somebody in one go you'd need a working. A big one. The kind that rings in the air like a struck glass.

The kind they teach on the far side of the Hours Bridge.

And then I saw his wrist.

Above the silver line of Fitch Ostrander's thread, maybe a finger's width higher, there was another mark. A thin red welt circling the wrist bone, fresh and angry, the way your skin looks for a day after a binder threads you. A second tether. Cut, by the look of the raw end. And under the skin, at the inside of the wrist where the thread had been tied off, there was a tiny raised bump. A knot.

Wick black-thread binders all use the same knot. It's called a broker's twist, and it's ugly and quick: you wrap the thread three times and pinch it, and it leaves a lump under the skin like a grain of rice. I'd seen a thousand of them.

This wasn't a broker's twist. It was small and neat and flat, a double loop with the tail tucked under, so tidy you'd have to be looking for it to see it.

I'd seen that knot exactly once before in my life. Yesterday morning, in this court, when a Ledger binder said a word I didn't know and tied it in the air between my wrist and Severin Thane's.

I didn't decide to step forward. I just did. Out of the line of tithes, past the edge of the Greycoats' ring, until I was close enough to touch Captain Marr's sleeve.

"He didn't sell," I said.

The whole ring turned to look at me.

Steward Rook's hand closed on my shoulder like a trap. "Fenn. *Back in line.*"

But Marr had raised one hand, and Rook's fingers stayed where they were without pulling. The captain looked up at me from where he crouched by the body. His eyes were a washed-out blue, red at the rims, and very tired. They didn't miss anything.

"Say that again," he said.

"He didn't sell." My mouth was dry. I said it anyway, quietly, so it was only for him. "Look at the lines. They're all the same grey, top to bottom. If he'd sold to a broker, the top ones would be darker. It went out in one pull. All of it. A broker can't do that."

Marr looked down at Joss's arm. Then back up at me. "And you'd know that how?"

"I worked a counter on Broker's Row for seven years. For Mother Gannet." I swallowed. "And sixty-six years is two hundred crowns at Wick rates, not forty. And there's a second thread on his wrist, cut, and it's not tied with a broker's twist. It's a Gilt knot. A Ledger knot. Like the one they tied on us yesterday."

There was a short silence.

"And he left his boots," I said. "Under his bed. Side by side. Nobody goes to sell their life barefoot."

Marr didn't say anything for a moment. Then, very slowly, he reached out and turned Joss's wrist to the grey light, and looked at the small, neat, flat lump under the skin. I watched his long jaw tighten.

"What's your name, girl?"

"Fenn. Tamsin Fenn."

"Whose?"

"Thane's," I said.

Something moved behind his eyes. I didn't know what it was. It wasn't surprise.

"Noted," said Captain Marr.

He stood up, knees cracking, and turned to Clerk Penn. "I'll want the cistern searched," he said. "The whole length of it. And the sluice. And I'll want the name of the broker."

"The broker's name is withheld under the Brokers' Privilege, Captain," said Clerk Penn, very stiffly. "As you know."

"Then I'll want it un-withheld."

"That would require an order from the Keeper of the Ledger." Penn's pink face had set like a pudding. "And the Keeper has already reviewed the record. The Ledger records a sale. The boy was of age. A sale is a lawful transaction. What a tithe chooses to do with his remaining count on his own time is his own affair." He closed his book with a soft clap. "The finding is that Joseph Mallory, tithe to House Ostrander, sold his count in full and subsequently drowned. The House will send the body home with a coat and a blessing. The Ledger considers the matter closed."

Captain Marr looked at him for a long, long moment.

Then he looked at the red-faced Greycoat, and the one holding the purse, and the ring of grey coats with their silver buttons, every one of which, I suddenly understood, had been paid for by the same Houses whose sons and daughters were standing under the colonnade in their nightshirts.

"The Ledger," said Captain Marr, "considers the matter closed."

He said it with no expression at all. Then he pulled his worn grey coat straight, and turned his back on Clerk Penn, and on me, and on Joss, and walked away across the court into the fog.

Rook's hand pulled me back into line. She didn't let go of my shoulder after that.

"You little fool," she said into my ear, so quietly I barely heard it. "You little Wick fool. Do you want to be next?"

---

I don't know how long we stood there. Long enough for the fog to start burning off. Long enough for two binders to come with a long wicker basket and lift Joss into it, and fold his blank arm across his chest, and cover him over. Long enough for Fitch Ostrander to stop crying and just sit on the cold flagstones with his arms around his knees, staring at nothing, until some older Ostrander cousin came and pulled him up by the elbow and walked him away.

They'd send him home in a box. Joss's mam would ask whether she could open it and they'd tell her it wasn't advisable. And somewhere on Tallow Lane, or in Saltmarket, or wherever it was, a girl with a laugh like a gull would get a coat and a blessing and forty silver crowns, and nine more years for her mother, and nothing else for the rest of her life.

*I'm going to call it Ostrander. Boy or girl.*

Beside me, Bry had gone very quiet in a way I'd never seen her go quiet before. Anni wasn't humming anymore. Pell had both arms around her.

I stood with Rook's hand on my shoulder and counted the things I knew, the way I'd count change.

Joss had sixty-six years. The longest count in the Hall. The longest anybody knew about.

Somebody had come for him in the night and walked him out of a locked room with no boots on.

Somebody had threaded him with a Gilt knot and pulled out every minute he had in one breath, with a working, which meant a wright.

Somebody had put forty crowns in his pocket and dropped him in the cisterns.

And somebody had made sure the Ledger had a sale written down before Captain Marr ever got out of bed.

My count was seventy-one years. Under the ink, it was the longest in the Hall. The longest anybody didn't know about.

*Don't go near the cisterns at night.*

The binders carried the basket away. And as the court began to empty, somebody came out of the tower door at the foot of the Horologe and stopped at the top of the steps.

Severin.

He didn't have his coat. He was in a black shirt, open at the collar, with the sleeves rolled to the forearm and the silver cuff bright against them, as though he'd come down in a hurry. His hair was wet. The hems of his trousers were dark and heavy with water to the knee.

He looked at the place where Joss had been lying. Then at the wicker basket going away between the binders. And I watched the color leave his face. Not slowly. All at once, the way a count goes out when the working takes it all, as though someone had pulled every drop of blood out of him in a single breath.

He looked at me.

I don't know what was in my face. Whatever it was, he saw it. Something flinched in his, quickly, and then closed.

He turned and went back up the steps.

As he turned, he put one hand on the door frame to steady himself, and I saw it. On the palm of his right glove, across the fingers, the black leather had gone darker in a long wet stain, almost too dark to see against the black.

Then the light caught it as he went through the door.

It was red.
