# Chapter Twenty-Two

## Below

Steward Rook was asleep in her chair.

She'd pulled it up against the inside of the iron door every night since Anni, and sat in it with her lamp at her feet and her keys in her lap and her good eye open, all night, every night, for three weeks. Tonight, for the first time, the eye had closed. Her head had fallen sideways against the doorframe. Her mouth was a little open. In the light of the lamp her face looked older than I'd ever seen it, and very tired.

The keys were in her lap, under her folded hands.

"I can't," whispered Pell, behind me. "Tam, I can't, she'll—"

"You don't have to."

I'd learned to lift a purse on Broker's Row when I was eight. Every child in the Wick does; you don't have to be good at it, just quick, and quiet, and willing. I knelt down beside Rook's chair on the cold stone. I put two fingers under the great iron ring where it lay against her skirt, and lifted it, slowly, a quarter inch at a time, the way you'd lift a sleeping cat's paw. Her hands slid off it onto her knees. She sighed. She didn't wake.

The keys didn't jingle. I'd wrapped them in the corner of my blanket before I lifted them.

The big key turned in the lock with a sound like a bone breaking. We both froze. Rook muttered something and shifted in her chair. Then her breathing went long and slow again, and I eased the iron door open a foot, and we went out and up the stairs into the dark.

---

Hollis opened his door on the third knock in a plum velvet dressing gown with a nightcap on his head, actually a nightcap, with a tassel, and a candle in his hand.

"It's two in the morning," he said. "If this is a social call, I'm flattered, but—" He saw our faces. The candle went still. "What's happened?"

"Bry," I said. "Lysander sent for her at the tenth bell. She hasn't come back."

He didn't ask anything else. He pulled the nightcap off and threw it behind him into the room, where I heard it hit Lord Featherstonehaugh's glass case, and pulled the dressing gown tighter, and came out onto the landing in his bare feet.

"His rooms are on the fifth," he said. "Two above mine. Come on."

Lysander's door was unlocked. Hollis pushed it open with one finger and we stood on the threshold, the three of us, and looked in.

It was a beautiful room. Much bigger than Hollis's, and much warmer, and full of beautiful things: rugs from the south, a carved bed with golden curtains, a wall of books with gilt spines that looked as if nobody had ever opened them. A fire was dying in the grate. The lamps were out.

Nobody was there.

On the table by the fire there was a single sheet of paper, folded once, with one word written on it in a round, careless hand. *Wait.* Exactly what Bry had told us, the night after Joss. Beside it, on the back of a chair, hung Bry's grey jacket.

"She'd never leave that," said Pell. His voice was shaking. "It's cold. She'd never."

The chair beside it was knocked over on its side.

And on the floor between the table and the door, on the pale southern rug, there was a smear. Grey. Gritty. I knelt and touched it, and rubbed it between my fingers, and smelled it, and I knew it the way I knew the smell of tallow.

River silt.

"He's been in the water," I said. "And come back. And gone again." I stood up. "And taken her with him."

"The sluice," said Hollis. "But we'd have heard—"

"Not from the Hall. Not if you were asleep." I looked at the smear on the rug. Not the sluice. Not yet. The sluice was the end of it, the grate, where they found you in the morning caught like a leaf. This was before.

*Don't go near the cisterns at night.*

*Do not go below the Hall.*

"There's a door," I said. "At the end of the Tithe Hall. Barred. Chained. Rook told us the first night: don't go below." I looked at Hollis. "What's below the Tithe Hall?"

"The cisterns," said Hollis slowly. "They run under the whole island. They feed the kitchens, and the baths, and the fountains in the court. They're old. Older than the Meridian, some people say." He swallowed. "The sluice is at the bottom of them. Where they empty out into the Sallow."

---

Rook was still asleep when we came back down. I didn't know whether that was luck or something else. I didn't stop to think about it.

We went down the aisle between the beds in the dark, past the sleeping tithes in their faint gold glow, past Joss's boots and Anni's folded blanket and Bry's empty bed with the dent in the pillow, to the far end of the Hall. To the smaller iron door, with the rusted lock the color of dried blood, and the bar across it, and the chain through the bar.

The chain was hanging loose.

Somebody had cut it. Not sawn through, not forced; *cut*, a single clean gap in one heavy link, the two ends bright and new against the rust, as if the iron had been as soft as thread. The bar had been lifted out of its brackets and leaned against the wall. And the door stood open a finger's width.

Cold came through the gap. Cold, and the smell of deep water, and something else, something under the water smell, that made the hair stand up on my arms.

"A quickening," said Hollis, very quietly, looking at the cut link. "Aged it. Years and years in a breath, right there in the one link, till it was rust and nothing. That's—" He swallowed. "That's not cheap."

*Saving up,* Bry had said.

I put my hand on the iron door.

"Tam," said Hollis. "Wait. We should get Severin."

"No."

"*Tam.*"

"No." I didn't look at him. I couldn't. "I'm not waiting for him to decide whether I can bear this."

Hollis was quiet for a moment. Then he said, very gently, "It's not about what you can bear. It's about what Lysander can do. He just aged an iron chain to dust. He's got two years of stilling saved up off Bry, at least. He's got the Keeper behind him. And you've got—" He looked at me. "What have you got, Tam?"

I thought about it. Seventy-one years and four months, and a pot of ink with nothing in it.

"More than he thinks," I said.

Hollis looked at me for a long moment in the faint gold light from under the blankets. I don't know what he saw. Whatever it was, he didn't argue with it.

"Then I'll get Severin," he said. "You go. Pell—"

"I'm going with Tam," said Pell.

"No," I said. "Pell. Stay here. At this door. If I'm not back by the fourth bell, wake Rook. Wake everybody. Shout the Hall down." I took his huge hand in both of mine. It was trembling. "Somebody has to be here when Bry comes up. Somebody she trusts. Please."

He looked down at me with his gentle face working. Then he nodded, once, and planted himself beside the iron door like a tree that had decided to grow there.

Hollis put his hand on my shoulder, just for a moment. Then he turned and ran, barefoot, back down the Hall in his plum dressing gown, toward the stairs and the tower and the room full of clocks.

I opened the door the rest of the way and went down.

---

The stairs were stone, steep and narrow and wet, going down into the dark in a straight line under the Hall. I counted them. Forty. Fifty. The air got colder and colder until it burned my lungs, and the walls ran with water, and somewhere below I could hear it: not dripping, not running. Lapping. The slow, patient lap of a great deal of still water against stone.

At seventy-three, the stairs ended in a ledge, and the dark opened up in front of me, and I stopped.

I couldn't see. I had no lamp. I'd left it all, the lamp and the candle and Rook's keys, with Pell at the top of the stairs, because I'd known I'd need my hands. But I had something better than a lamp. I'd always had it.

I pushed my left sleeve up to the shoulder.

My count lit the cistern.

Not all of it. It couldn't. The ink was over the top ten years, the last thin smear I'd stretched out of the thimble pot at Thane House, and the ink dulled them to almost nothing. But the rest of it, sixty years of gold from wrist to elbow, blazed out into that dark like a lantern with the shutters thrown open, and the cistern came up out of the black around me.

It was enormous. I'd known it would be big. I hadn't known it would be like this. A forest of pillars, stone pillars as thick as oak trees, going away in rows in every direction into the dark, holding up a vaulted ceiling I could barely see. And between the pillars, water. Black water, flat as glass, stretching away and away, with my gold light lying on it in a long trembling path. The ledge I stood on ran along the wall to my left, narrow, slick with green slime, and then disappeared into the water a dozen paces on.

Somewhere far off, a drip. A long time later, another.

And under it, under everything, so low that I felt it in my feet before I heard it, a beat.

Slow. Deep. The same beat I'd felt through the soles of my boots in the tunnel under the river, at the iron gate, when Severin said *not that way*. A long, deep throb coming up through the stone, and then nothing, nothing, nothing, and then again. Like a drum struck very softly a long way off. Like something enormous, asleep, breathing.

The thread at my wrist went warm.

Not hot, the way it went for Severin. Warm. Slowly spreading. Like a hand closing gently around it. And it wasn't pulling me back up the stairs toward the tower. It was pulling me *forward*. Out. Over the water. Toward the beat.

I took a breath of the burning cold air, and I walked along the ledge to where it ended, and I stepped down into the water.

---

It came up to my knees, and then my thighs, and then my waist, so cold that it didn't feel cold at all; it felt like being cut. I gasped. I couldn't help it. The sound went away from me across the water and came back off the pillars a dozen times, smaller and smaller, *ah, ah, ah, ah*. I held my left arm up high over my head, out of the water, so the light would carry, and I waded forward.

Between the pillars. Through the black. The floor under my boots was smooth stone, and then it was silt, soft and sucking, and then it was stone again. Every few steps I stopped, and listened, and felt.

The beat.

And the thread at my wrist, warm, pulling. Left. Between those two pillars. Straight on.

I don't know how long I waded. A long time. Long enough that I couldn't feel my legs anymore, only the stone under my boots, very far away, as if it belonged to someone else. Long enough that my arm, held up high, began to ache and then to burn and then to shake. Long enough that I started counting pillars, because counting is what I do, and lost count at a hundred and forty.

Then the pillars ended.

There was a wall in front of me. Not the smooth dressed stone of the cistern; something older, rougher, black rock with the marks of tools still in it. The water lapped against it, waist-deep. And set into it, half under the surface, with the black water lapping at its middle, there was a door.

It was iron. Arched. Old. Not like the doors in the Meridian, or the gate in the tunnel; older than either. Its surface was covered all over in a pattern, worked into the metal, that I didn't understand at first, and then did: clock faces. Dozens of them, small and large, overlapping, every one with a single hand. And every hand pointed at eleven.

It was standing open. Just a crack. A finger's width, like the door at the top of the stairs.

And through the crack, light.

Not gold. Not count-light. Yellow and flickering. A lamp, or a torch, somewhere on the other side, in whatever was behind that door, in whatever was under the Meridian that nobody went to and nobody spoke about and nobody had ever written on any map but my brother's.

The beat came up through the water and through the stone and through my numb legs, slow, enormous, so close now I could feel it in my teeth.

And the thread at my wrist pulled, warm, toward the door.

There was no door here, on any plan of the Meridian. There was no door here in any story I'd ever heard, except one, told in the dark at the end of my bed by a woman with a crooked finger, tracing circles in the air.

*Way down in the dark.*

I lowered my aching arm, and held it out in front of me with the light streaming off it across the black water, and I waded toward the door that shouldn't exist.
