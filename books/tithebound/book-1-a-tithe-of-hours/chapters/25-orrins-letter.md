# Chapter Twenty-Five

## Orrin's Letter

I went up that night.

I didn't decide to. I lay in the dark after the eleventh bell with Rook's words going round and round in my head, *every arm on that floor, before and after*, and my hand over my wrist, and his heartbeat under my fingers, waiting. And at some point I was sitting up and pulling on my boots, and Bry, in the next bed, without opening her eyes, said, "About *time*," and rolled over.

Rook was awake in her chair by the iron door. She looked at me. She looked at my boots. She got up, and unlocked the door, and stood aside.

"I'll leave it on the latch," she said. "Mind you're back before the weighing."

---

The black door opened before I reached the top step.

He was at the velvet table. Not working. Sitting with his hands flat on the cloth, the way he'd sat the night after Joss, and between his hands, on the velvet, lay Liesl's watch with its broken crystal and its hands stopped at twenty past four. He looked up when I came in. He didn't stand. He didn't say anything. He just looked at me as if I were something he'd been told he'd never see again.

I sat down in my chair beside him. Mine. The one he'd pulled out for me the first night of lessons.

For a long time, neither of us spoke. The clocks ticked. The great dead gears hung in the dark overhead.

"You stood in the water all night," I said at last.

"Yes."

"At the gate."

"I could feel you. Going into the cold. Further and further." He was looking at the watch. "And then something hurt you. In your wrist. Like a needle. And then you started to pour. Years. Out of you. Down a thread that wasn't mine." He swallowed. "I thought you were dying. I thought someone had you on a black thread and was draining you, and I was standing on the other side of an iron gate with a chain on it, and I couldn't get to you." His jaw worked. "I tried everything. Workings. My hands. I aged the lock to rust and the chain wouldn't go. I don't know what it's made of. I stood there till the sun came up."

I looked at his hands on the velvet. The gloves were off. His knuckles were raw, skinned to the flesh, scabbed over.

"It was Bry," I said. "On the thread. I threaded myself to her with Lysander's needle. I gave her twenty."

"I know. Penhallow told me. The next morning." He finally looked at me. "He came up here and hit me."

"He *hit* you?"

"In the face. Quite hard, for a Penhallow. And then he apologized for twenty minutes and asked if I had any ice." The corner of his mouth moved. "And then he told me what you'd done, and what Bry had heard in that room, and what my grandmother said. *Not yet. At the Reckoning. In front of everyone.*" The corner of his mouth stopped moving. "And then he told me that if I ever made you climb those stairs to find a door shut again, he'd come back and finish it."

---

He reached into the inside of his coat and took out a letter.

It was folded small and soft, the way Kit's map was folded, the paper gone furry at the creases from being opened and closed too many times. He put it on the velvet in front of me, beside Liesl's watch.

"I should have shown you this a long time ago," he said. "When you asked about Orrin. When you put your finger on his line on Wat Sorrel's list and said *he doesn't fit*." He paused. "I should have shown you a great many things."

I unfolded it.

It was written in pencil, in big careful letters, the handwriting of someone who'd learned late and still pressed too hard.

*To the man in the tower.*

*The baby came on Harvest-eve. A girl. Marta says if I name her after you she'll leave me and take the cow, so she's called Wren. She has a count of sixty-eight and a voice like a gull with a grudge. I hold her every morning at the window and show her the sea.*

*My count is going down like anybody's. A day at a time. I watch it go and I've never been so glad of anything in my life. Every morning I think about the four years you spent making me look dead, and I don't waste them. I promise you. Not one.*

*Tell Mother G. the herring she packed was terrible and I ate all of it.*

*If you ever need a place by the sea, there's a bed. Marta says you're to bring your own blanket. She's not running a charity.*

*O.F.*

I read it twice. Then I put it down on the velvet very carefully, the way he put things down.

"Four years," I said.

"I stilled his heart." He said it to the watch. "For a day and a half. The Steward found him cold in his bed in the morning, and a binder came and looked at his arm and pronounced him, and the Ledger wrote *fever*. Rook packed him into a box with her own hands." His mouth twisted. "She's never forgiven me for that. She thinks I killed him. She's thought it for a year."

"And the box—"

"Went out by the Thane passage, through the tunnel, to the Wick end. The cellar with the crates. Gannet's people were waiting. They opened the box, and I broke the stilling, and Orrin Fisk sat up in it and was sick on the floor." He paused. "And then they put him on a herring barge to Calder Bay with a new name, and he married the first girl who laughed at him, and now there's a baby called Wren."

I couldn't say anything.

"My grandmother thinks I bribed a binder," said Severin. "She found out where he was in the spring. She decided he wasn't worth the trouble of bringing back." He looked at his raw knuckles. "That's what she said. *Nothing*. He's nothing. A Wick boy with fifty-three years and a count that falls."

"You spent four years of your life on a Wick boy with fifty-three years and a count that falls."

"Yes."

"Why?"

He was quiet for a long moment.

"Because I got there too late for Liesl," he said. "And too late for Corin Ashe, and Teodor Brack, and Saskia Venn, and all the others on those lists. And too late for Joss. And too late for Anni." His voice didn't change at all. "Orrin's the only one I ever got to in time."

---

I looked at him. At his raw hands on the velvet. At his face in the lamplight, pale and tired and shut, the way it always was, the way it had been the first night when he sat at this table with a loupe in his eye and told me to sit down.

He'd stood in the middle of the Dial and let himself be frozen like a fly in honey. He'd stood in the black water at an iron gate all night. He'd stilled a boy's heart for a day and a half and spent four years of his own life doing it, and let the whole Meridian believe he'd sent his tithe home in a box. And he'd never said a word. To anyone. Not once.

Because he always decided alone.

"You're going to stop," I said.

He looked up.

"Deciding. For me." I didn't raise my voice. "All of it. What I can know, what I can carry, who gets bought and who gets left, whether I'm told before or after. You're going to stop. Starting now."

"I know," said Severin. "I've known since you walked out of here with the folder. I'm trying." He held my eyes. "Tell me what you want me to do."

"Tell me everything first. The Reckoning. Your plan. All of it. Every piece, even the ones you haven't fixed."

He told me.

It didn't take long. That was the worst of it. He laid it out on the velvet the way he laid out a watch, every piece in its place, and it was very simple, and very clean, and it was going to kill him.

At the Reckoning, he would cast his great working: a stilling over the entire Dial, every soul on the marble, held for a count of a hundred. Nobody had done one that size on the Dial in fifty years. It would cost five years, near enough, maybe a little more. He would take my wrist in front of the whole Gilt, and draw on me, visibly, so that everyone saw the gold rope come out of my arm. And it would be a lie. The rope would be his own time, pulled up out of his own wrist and through the thread and out again, made to look like it was coming from me. A reversed draw. Vadra had told him it could be done; she'd done it once, thirty years ago, for Aurel Vey. And I'd ink my arm to fall by five years as he cast, the way I'd done at every class since Tithe Day.

"And you'd have two years," I said.

"Two years and two months."

"And she'll be standing ten feet away reading my arm before and after. With her own eyes. Close enough to touch." I heard my voice start to shake. "She brushed my ink with her thumb in the Refectory, Severin. She *knows*. Ink won't fool her. Not up close. Not now."

"I know," he said quietly. "That's the piece I haven't fixed."

---

"No," I said.

"Tamsin—"

"*No.* I won't let you burn five years on a trick that won't even work. I won't stand there and watch you go down to two years in front of her for nothing. I won't."

"It's not nothing. If she sees your count fall—"

"She won't *see* it fall! She'll see ink! And then she'll smile at you with her dimples and say *bravo, darling* and take me anyway, and you'll have two years left and nothing to show for them." I was on my feet. I didn't remember standing up. "You'd be dead in two years for *nothing*."

He was on his feet too. "I'd rather have two years with you alive than seventy without you."

"That's not your decision!"

"Then whose is it?"

"*Ours!*"

It rang off the clocks. It rang off the great dead gears overhead. And then it was gone, and there was only the ticking, hundreds of little clocks all disagreeing, and the two of us standing on either side of the velvet table, breathing hard.

"Ours," I said again, quieter. "It's ours. It's not yours to carry by yourself in a tower with a watch that won't go. It never was."

He looked at me for a long time.

"I love you," said Severin Thane. "I didn't decide that either. I'd like that noted."

The clocks ticked.

I came around the end of the table. I put my hand flat on his chest, over his heart, over the place where I'd been feeling it for three months through a silver thread in my wrist like a drum in another room.

"I know," I said. "Your heart's very loud."

And then, because he looked at me as if I'd hit him, and because I'd been carrying it up six hundred and twelve stairs every night for three months without ever letting myself put it down: "I love you too. You stupid, stupid boy."

---

Somebody knocked on the door.

It wasn't a polite knock. It was a fist, three times, and then a voice on the other side, high and carrying and slightly out of breath.

"I *know* you're in there. I can hear you shouting. The whole stairwell can hear you shouting. Open this door before I have to climb another stair, because I won't survive it."

Severin closed his eyes.

"Penhallow," he said.

The door swung open.

Hollis was on the landing. So was Bry, leaning on the frame with her arms folded and twenty years bright on the bare arm she'd pushed up to show it. So was Pell, enormous, behind them both, carrying a covered basket that smelled of lemon.

And between them, tall and straight-backed in a dark green gown with her sleeves down over her knuckles, stood Magister Ines Vadra.

"Good evening, Mr. Thane," she said. "Miss Fenn. I gather you've been having a conversation. I'm afraid it's been audible from the third landing." She stepped past Hollis into the room without waiting to be invited, and looked around at the clocks with an expression of mild interest, and then up at the dead gears. "I've been wanting to see this room for thirty years."

"We brought cake," said Pell, holding up the basket.

"We brought Vadra," said Bry. "Penhallow went to her this afternoon. Told her everything. Don't look at me like that, Tam. He was going to do it anyway. I just made sure he didn't faint on the way."

"I didn't *faint*—"

"You went very grey on the second landing."

"I was *thinking*."

Severin looked at me. I looked at him. Then, very slowly, he pulled out every chair in the room, all three of them, and Pell sat on the floor, and we sat around the velvet table in the light of the lamp under the stopped heart of the Horologe, six of us, with lemon cake.

---

"You're both trying to hide," said Magister Vadra.

She'd listened to all of it. Severin's plan. My objections. The lists in the folder, which Hollis had brought up from the library and laid on the table. Wat's copies of the weighing books. Bry's account of the room under the cistern and the small woman in black eating violets. She'd listened with her long severe face perfectly still and her hands folded on the velvet, and she hadn't said a word until we'd finished.

"That's the flaw," she said. "You're both trying to hide the girl. He wants to hide her behind his own count. She wants to hide behind her ink. And the Keeper of the Ledger has been looking for her for ten years and has, by every account in this room, already found her." She looked at me. "You can't hide something from someone who's already looking straight at it, Miss Fenn. You can only make them look somewhere else."

"Where?" said Hollis.

"At themselves."

Nobody said anything.

"Lady Thane wants a spectacle," said Vadra. "She said so. *In front of everyone. I want them to see.* So give her one." Her eyes moved around the table. "Not of the girl's count. Of hers."

"The lists," said Severin slowly.

"The lists. Ten years of long counts in her own records, in your hand, each one tested and drained and sold to Broker Seven, which is the Ledger. Wat Sorrel's copies of the weighing books, matching every death. And the one thing you don't have." She looked at Bry. "A witness. Not to the murders. The Gilt won't care about dead Wick children; they never have. But to what she's been looking for."

"*Never again the well*," said Hollis, very softly.

Vadra inclined her head. "It's carved over the door of the Ledger House in letters three feet high. It's the one law the Long-Lived actually fear, because they know exactly what happens to everybody else when one person holds a well. If the Gilt believes the Keeper of the Ledger has been hunting a wellspring, secretly, for ten years, for herself—" She paused. "They'll tear her apart. Not for the tithes. For the well."

"Then they'll know there's a well," I said. "And they'll look at me."

"They'll all be looking at you anyway," said Bry quietly. "In three days. On the Dial. Before and after."

"Yes," said Vadra. "But this way, they'll also be looking at her." She turned to Severin. "You can't prove what she's been doing with your copies alone. A good advocate would say you forged them. You need something nobody can argue with. Something everybody on that Dial can see with their own eyes."

"An Echo," said Hollis.

Everybody turned to him.

He had gone a little pale under his freckles. But his chin was up.

"An Echo of that room," he said. "Under the cistern. The night Lysander took Bry down there. If someone cast it, you'd see it all. Him. Her. The thread. The violets." He swallowed. "And you can catch an Echo in a lens, can't you, Magister? Like the ones in the library. And play it again. Anywhere. On the Dial, if you liked. In front of the whole Gilt."

"You can," said Vadra. "If you have a blank lens." She reached into the folds of her green gown and took out a disc of thick clear glass in a plain brass frame, about the size of a dinner plate, and laid it on the velvet among the lemon cake. "I took it from the library this afternoon. The librarian won't miss it till spring."

We all looked at it.

"An Echo two weeks back," said Severin slowly. "Through stone, under water. It would cost—"

"A month and a half," said Hollis. "Maybe two. I've done the sum." He looked at me. "And before you say anything, Tam, it's my time, and I'm *deciding*. Isn't that what you just shouted at him? For the whole stairwell to hear?"

I opened my mouth. I shut it again.

Vadra was watching me. So was Severin. So were they all. The lamp hissed. Overhead, the great stopped engine of the Horologe hung in the dark like a held breath, the way it had for three hundred years.

I thought about my ink. About three months of thin coats and feathered ends and ash and spit and a thumb. About hiding. About Kit's map, and the circle with the heart in it, and the iron gate in the tunnel, and the beat in the stone, and a woman under a pendulum in an engraving with my mother's crooked finger. About Joss's boots, side by side, under a bed.

"Kit," I said. "If we do this, she'll go for Kit."

"Gannet's people will take him out of Tallow Lane on the night of the Reckoning," said Vadra. "Before the first working. My word on it. With Mr. Thane's man to walk him there."

Severin nodded once. "My word too."

I looked around the table. At Hollis, pale and stubborn, with his six years under a tin cuff. At Bry, with twenty years she hadn't had a month ago. At Pell, with lemon cake on his fingers and his gentle face set. At Vadra, with her scars under her sleeve. At Severin, beside me, with his raw knuckles and his seven years and his heart going loud enough that I could feel it without even touching my wrist.

Three months, I'd been hiding. Ten years, if you counted properly.

"No more ink," I said.

They looked at me.

"No more hiding. No more reversed draws, no more burning anyone's years for a trick. She wants them to see?" I put my hand flat on the black folder on the velvet. "Then we'll make them see. All of it." I looked at Severin. "Together. Not you alone in a tower. Not me alone in the Hall. All of us."

He looked at me for a long time. Then he put his raw hand over mine on the folder.

"Then we do it your way," he said.
