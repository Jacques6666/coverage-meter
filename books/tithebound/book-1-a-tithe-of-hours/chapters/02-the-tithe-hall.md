# Chapter Two

## The Tithe Hall

He didn't say anything else.

He straightened up, as if he'd only leaned down to check the binder's knot, and turned, and walked away across the black flagstones with his gloved hands behind his back. He went up the tower steps and in through the arch, and the shadows took him, and that was all. The heir of House Thane had bought me for thirty years, told me he knew the one thing about me that could get me killed, and left me standing in the Tithe Court with my sleeve rolled up like a fool.

The second heartbeat went with him. It got fainter as he climbed, but it didn't stop. I could feel it in my wrist, steady as a clock in another room.

*I watched it go up.*

There are two things you do with a secret in the Wick. You sell it, or you use it. Nobody pays thirty years for something they mean to sell.

"Tithes." A woman's voice cracked across the court like a dropped plank. "With me. Now. Your wrights don't need you until tomorrow, and I don't want you dripping on my flagstones all day."

---

Her name was Agathe Rook, and she was the Steward of the Tithe Hall. She was short and square and grey all over, grey dress, grey hair scraped back hard enough to hurt, a ring of iron keys at her belt as big as a dinner plate. One of her eyes was brown and sharp. The other was milky white, and it seemed to look at you more than the good one did.

She marched the twelve of us across the court and through a low door in the base of the tower, and down. The steps were black stone, worn into dips in the middle by three hundred years of feet, and with every turn the air got colder and wetter, until I could taste the river in it.

"I was a tithe in this Hall for one term," Steward Rook said, without turning around. Her voice bounced off the walls. "I was bought by House Lisle with fifty-two years on my arm. I went home at the Reckoning with nine. That was considered a great success. Old Lord Lisle sent my mother a ham."

Somebody laughed nervously. It died fast.

"That was thirty-one years ago," said Steward Rook. "You may do the arithmetic yourselves."

I did. Behind me, I heard Joss Mallory do it too, in a whisper: "Nine years, thirty-one years ago—"

"The Meridian pays its Steward in years," she said. "Every term I keep you in line, I get to live through another one. Remember that before you mistake me for your friend."

The stairs ended at an iron door. She unlocked it with the largest key on the ring, and swung it open, and we went into the Tithe Hall.

It was long, longer than our whole building on Tallow Lane, and vaulted like a church, with a double row of narrow iron beds marching down it under the arches. The walls were black stone sweating with damp. There were windows, but they were up near the ceiling, long thin slots, and through them I saw something that took me a moment to understand: water. The surface of the Sallow, green-brown and moving, sliding past the glass at the height of my head. We were below the river. The light that came through the slots was green and wavering, and it made everyone look drowned.

"Beds are marked," said Steward Rook. "Uniforms are on them. You'll be in grey by supper and you'll stay in grey until you leave. Now listen, because I say this once a year and I'm tired of saying it."

We stood at the ends of our beds. She walked down the aisle between us with her hands behind her back, exactly as the Thane heir had walked away. I wondered whether it was something they taught here.

"One. You belong to your House until your term ends at the Reckoning. Your wright may draw on you whenever and for whatever they please. Refusing a draw is theft, and theft from a House is a hanging matter. I have seen it done. Don't make me see it again."

Nobody moved.

"Two. You will not touch a wright unless a wright touches you first. You will not speak to one unless spoken to. Those of you who think you're going to make a wright fall in love with you and live in the Gilt forever, get it out of your heads. I've seen that tried too. The Gilt doesn't marry its batteries."

Bry, beside me, made a small noise of disgust.

"Three. Curfew is the eleventh bell. And yes, I know what the clock says. The clock says eleven in the morning and eleven at night and eleven at your funeral. The *bells* keep real time. The clock keeps the Gilt's. Be in this Hall by the eleventh bell or be locked out until morning, and the river gets cold at night.

"Four. Every Seventhday I weigh you. You'll line up and show me your arm and I'll write your count in this book." She touched a fat ledger hanging from a chain at her belt, beside the keys. "If your count falls below ten years, you go home. Short. If you can walk. The House sends you home with a coat and a blessing and the satisfaction of knowing you served."

She had reached the far end of the Hall, where a second iron door, smaller than the first, was set into the wall. Its lock was rusted the color of dried blood, and there was a bar across it, and a chain through the bar.

"Five," said Steward Rook, and laid her palm flat on the iron. "Do not go below the Hall."

A thin boy from the Sump, one of Vey's, put up his hand like we were in a schoolroom. "What's below the Hall, Steward?"

The milky eye turned on him.

"Water," she said. "And nothing that wants you."

---

We changed into grey. The uniforms were wool, scratchy, cut for nobody in particular: a long tunic, trousers, a thick jacket with the Meridian clock stitched over the heart in black. The sleeves were short. Of course they were. Wrights need to see what they're drawing.

I took the bed at the end of the row, against the wall, so that I'd have only one neighbor watching me. Bry took that bed before anybody else could. On my other side, against the stone, there was nothing but damp and a crack the shape of a lightning bolt.

The others drifted together the way people do, sitting on the ends of beds, trading names and counts and Houses.

Joss Mallory had taken the bed across the aisle from mine, and he had already decided we were friends. He was nineteen, with ears that stuck out like the handles on a jug, and he talked the way some people whistle, without needing a reason.

"Sixty-six," he said, showing me his arm, as though I hadn't heard Clerk Penn say it. "And *nine* for Hetty's mam. Nine! Ostrander's a good House. My cousin was Ostrander's three years ago and he says they hardly draw at all, they mostly just make you carry books. Nine years. Her mam's got a cough like a cart going over cobbles, and now she'll see Hetty married, and she'll see the baby." He blushed to the tips of his ears. "When there's a baby. Not that there's a baby. I mean there's going to be. After."

"Congratulations," I said. "On the future baby."

"Thanks," said Joss earnestly. "I'm going to call it Ostrander. Boy or girl."

A small girl perched on the bed next to his, with a cloud of pale hair and a pointed, anxious face, laughed despite herself and then looked frightened at having laughed. Her name was Anni Rusk. She was from the Sump, where the spring floods come into the ground floors and the children grow up thin and good at climbing. Sixty-three years, House Lisle, seven of Grace for her grandmother. She hummed under her breath when she was nervous, which was all the time, and she seemed to know only hymns.

And then there was Pell.

Pell Darrow was the largest person I have ever seen. He had to duck under the arches. His uniform jacket wouldn't button and his trousers stopped halfway down his shins, and he sat on the end of his bed so carefully, as if he expected it to break. He had forty-nine years and the gentlest face in Lanthorn.

"Penhallow," he said, when Joss asked. He said it shyly, as if he were confessing to something. "Ten."

"The floor?" said Joss. "Ah, that's rough, mate."

"He said he was sorry," said Pell. "My wright. He said it a lot. It's all his House had, he said. And then he asked if I liked lemon cake." He considered this, with his big hands folded in his lap. "I do, as it happens. I bake."

"Hollis Penhallow," I said. "Freckles? Talks a lot?"

Pell's face lit up. "Yes. Do you know him?"

"He apologized to me in the Tithe Court."

"He apologized to *everyone* in the Tithe Court," said Bry from the bed beside mine. She was lying on her back with her boots still on, staring at the vaulted ceiling. "He apologized to the binder. He apologized to a pigeon."

"He's six years," I said, and Pell nodded slowly, his face falling.

"I know," he said. "He told me. He said I shouldn't worry, because he wasn't planning on drawing anyway, and then he'd fail out and go home and die in peace in his own bed, which is how a Penhallow ought to." Pell looked down at his folded hands. "I don't think he was joking. I think he says things like they're jokes so nobody has to be sad about them."

For a moment nobody said anything.

Then Joss leaned across the aisle toward me with the air of someone who'd been holding something in for an hour and couldn't hold it any longer.

"Thirty," he said. "*Thirty*, Tam. From *Thane*."

"I was there."

"Do you know about him? About his tithes?" He didn't wait. Joss never waited. "Orrin Fisk. From the Sump, like Anni. Went in last autumn with fifty-odd years. Thane bought him for the floor. In February they sent him home in a box." He lowered his voice. "They didn't open the box. His mam asked and they said it wasn't *advisable*."

Anni had stopped humming.

"There's one before that, too," Joss said. "Not a tithe. A girl in the Thane house, a Wick girl, a maid or something. They say he drank her dry when he was sixteen years old. Didn't even need a working. Just did it to see what it felt like."

"Who says?" said Bry, still looking at the ceiling.

"Everyone."

"Everyone said Hen Pallister fell off the bridge drunk," I said, and I don't know why I said it, except that I'd said the same thing to Kit that morning and it had tasted wrong then, too.

Joss shrugged. "Maybe it's all rubbish. But nobody pays thirty, Tam. Not for a tithe. My cousin says the most he ever heard of was Marchbank paying twelve, and that was for a girl with seventy-something years, and they drew her down to ten before winter."

"Maybe he likes my face," I said.

Bry turned her head on the pillow and looked at me. "Nobody likes your face."

Joss laughed so hard he fell off the end of his bed, and Pell had to pick him up, and for a little while the Tithe Hall felt like any other room full of nervous people trying to be brave. But when the others had turned away, Bry kept looking at me. She didn't laugh.

"Crane paid eight for me," she said, low enough that only I could hear. "He looked at me like I was a cake in a shop window. That's what eight buys." She turned onto her side, facing me across the gap between our beds. "Nobody pays thirty for the same thing. What does he want with you, Tam?"

Bry had known me since I was five. She'd known my mother. She had been the first person through our door the morning after the Greycoats came, with half a loaf and a bottle of milk she'd almost certainly stolen.

I could have told her. I almost did.

"I don't know," I said.

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she rolled onto her back again.

"If he hurts you," she said to the ceiling, "I'll kill him."

"Which one? Thane, or your golden boy?"

"Either. Both." She closed her eyes. "I'm not fussy."

---

They brought us supper on trays, bread and a stew that was mostly turnip, and at the eleventh bell Steward Rook came through with a long brass snuffer and put out the lamps one by one, and locked the iron door behind her. The Hall went dark except for the green slots of the windows, and then even those went black as the night came down over the river.

But the Tithe Hall is never entirely dark. Not with twelve long counts in it.

Under every blanket was a faint warm glow, gold and soft, where someone's arm lay against the wool. I'd never slept in a room with so many long counts. In the Wick, the short ones outnumber the long, and a dark room is a dark room. Here it looked like a field of embers, banked for the night.

I pulled my blanket over my head and turned my arm to the light of my own count.

The threading had made a mess of the ink. Where the binder had held my wrist, my skin had sweated, and two of the buried lines near the elbow had begun to show through, gold glinting under grey like sun through dirty glass. If Steward Rook saw that, she'd see seventy-one, or close enough to wonder.

I got the pot out of the inside pocket I'd sewn into my old coat, and the brush, and I fixed it, working by count-light under the blanket the way I'd done a thousand times on Tallow Lane. Thin coats. Feathered ends.

Then I tilted the pot to the light and looked at what was left.

Not much. A smear around the bottom, a crust at the rim. If I was careful, if I only touched up and never repainted, two weeks. Maybe less. The first weighing was on Seventhday, five days away. The one after that, twelve. After that I would be standing in front of Steward Rook's milky eye with my sleeve rolled up and nothing between my secret and her book but sweat.

Mother Gannet's shop was on Broker's Row, at the Wick end of the Hours Bridge. A quarter of a mile. Past twenty-four lamps, and the bridge wardens, and the eleventh bell.

I'd worry about that tomorrow. I put the pot away and checked the tip of my count. The minute the binder had taken in the Tithe Court was back, of course. Of course it was.

I lay in the dark with my wrist against my chest and tried to sleep.

I couldn't, because of the heartbeat.

It was still there. Under mine, slow and even. All evening it had been so faint I could almost forget it, but in the quiet it filled my whole arm. It was the strangest feeling in the world, like lying in bed and hearing someone breathing in the next room, someone you didn't know, someone who knew you.

Somewhere around midnight, it changed.

It didn't speed up, exactly. It *sharpened*. The way your own heartbeat sharpens when you've been lying awake thinking about something and you finally decide. The silver line around my wrist went warm, and then I felt something I didn't have a word for. It was not a pull; nothing left me. It was more like a hand on a rope, testing the weight at the other end.

He was awake. Up there somewhere in the tower, the heir of House Thane was awake and thinking about me.

I lay very still with my heart going hard against the mattress. Beneath the floor, under the stone, water moved in a long slow surge, as if something very large had turned over in its sleep.

Then light.

A lamp, swinging in the doorway at the end of the Hall. Keys. The iron door scraped open, and Steward Rook stood there in a grey wrapper with her hair down her back like a grey rope, and the lamp in her hand turned her milky eye to a coin.

Twelve heads came up off twelve pillows. Somebody gasped. Anni Rusk started to hum, very quietly, a hymn about the lamb.

"Fenn," said Steward Rook.

I sat up.

Across the aisle, Joss's eyes were enormous in the gloom. Beside me I felt Bry go rigid under her blanket, the way she used to go rigid in the ring before somebody threw the first punch.

"Up," said the Steward. "Boots on. The Thane heir wants his tithe."

Nobody breathed.

"*Now.*"
