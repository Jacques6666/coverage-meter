# Chapter Thirty

## Eleven O'Clock

I don't remember kneeling. I remember being on my knees on the black stone in the pool of light that ran off her, and the light soaking through my grey wool, warm as bathwater, and my hands on her face.

Her skin was thin and dry, like paper that's been folded and unfolded too many times. Her cheekbones were sharp under my palms. Up close, the grey in her hair was everywhere, threaded all through the dark. She'd been thirty-one the night she walked out into the fog. She looked fifty. She looked a hundred.

She couldn't hold me back. Her arms were held out and up by the silver threads, rigid, like a puppet's, and she couldn't bring them down. So I held her. I put my arms around her, around her thin shoulders and the threads and the light and all of it, and I put my face in her neck, and I held on.

She smelled of stone, and cold, and underneath that, so faint I thought I was imagining it, of tallow.

"Go on, dear," said Lady Aurelia Thane, somewhere behind me. "Take your time. I'm not a monster."

I didn't look round.

"Tamsin." My mother's mouth was against my ear. Her voice was a thread, cracked and dry, as if she hadn't used it in a long time. "Listen to me. Quickly. While she's being kind."

"Mama—"

"Listen." Her breath was shaking. "The night they came for me. The night I kissed you and walked out. I knew they were coming. I'd known for a week; I'd sold where I shouldn't have and somebody saw. So before I went, I tied you to me. Sitting on the end of your bed in the dark, while you were asleep. An old binding. My mother taught it to me, and hers taught her. A mother's tether." She swallowed. "Whatever they took from me, half would go to you. Always. So they'd never have all of me. And so you'd never, ever run out."

The night my wrist burned. Hot and then cold, like a hand closing around it in the dark.

"You've been feeding me," I said into her neck. "Ten years. You've been *feeding* me."

"I've been keeping you," said my mother.

I couldn't speak.

"I felt you," she whispered. "Every day. Through it. Every time you sold, every time you gave. I felt you climb that tower. I felt the boy with the clocks." Something like a laugh moved in her chest. "His heart, under yours, all winter. So loud."

"*Mama.*"

"Kit." Her whole body went rigid against me. "Tamsin. Kit. Is he—"

"He's alive." I made myself pull back far enough to see her face. "He's alive. He's ten. He's got thirty-one years, Mama. He draws maps. He draws places he's never seen, from the stories. Your stories. I told him all of them." My voice broke. "He drew this room."

Her face crumpled. All at once. The way Bry's had in the little square room under the cistern.

"I was coming back for him," said my mother. "That night. I was going to tie him too. I only had time for one of you before they came, and you were the eldest, and I thought—I thought I'd get back. I was *coming back.*" Her voice cracked in half. "I never got back."

---

"Well," said Lady Aurelia brightly. "That's quite enough of that, I think."

I turned my head.

She was standing at the edge of the pool of light, in her black lace, with the silver clock hands in her hair and the silver box in her hand. The two Thane guards stood by the parted silver door. The binder in grey stood beside her with his velvet cushion and his silver needle. She was smiling at us, with both dimples, the way she'd smiled at Anni when she touched her cheek.

"Here is how this will go," she said. "And then we can all have some tea."

I didn't let go of my mother.

"You'll be threaded to me," said Lady Aurelia. "Directly. Right here, now, by my binder, with the Ledger's own needle. You refill from her, endlessly. So you'll be my well, dear, and she'll be yours, and nothing will ever leak away again. Everything she has will come to me, through you, all of it. Not half." She ate a violet. "It's very elegant, really. I'm surprised the Founders never thought of it."

"And if I say no."

"Oh, darling. You won't." She tilted her head. "But since you ask. If you say no, I'll have my binder cut your mother's tether, the little Wick one she tied on you in the dark. And you'll be an ordinary girl with an ordinary count, seventy-one years, going down a day at a time like everybody else's. And I'll keep her until she wears out, the way I kept the last one." She smiled. "And then I'll go and find your brother. Christopher, isn't it? Thirty-one years, such a generous Grace. In Mother Gannet's cellars at the end of the Row." Her eyes were warm and gold-flecked and delighted. "Gannet's cellars aren't nearly as secret as Gannet believes. They never have been. I'll take his years back, one at a time, every Long Night, for the fountain. And I'll see whether wells really do run in families."

I stopped breathing.

"And if you say yes," said Lady Aurelia, "then your brother lives his thirty-one years. Every one. Unwatched, untouched, wherever Gannet's put him. My foolish heir lives his four, if he's careful with them, which I doubt. The Penhallow boy and the boxer and that great gentle lump who bakes go home at the end of the term, whole. You have my word. In front of my binder." She nodded at the man in grey. "Which makes it law."

Under my arms, I felt my mother shaking.

I looked at her face. At her dark eyes, too big, so like Kit's. At the silver threads running up from her wrists into the dark, and out across the floor into the walls. Ten years. Ten years kneeling here in the light, with her arms held out, feeding the clock and the Ledger and the Long-Lived and a small sweet woman in black lace who ate violets. Ten years of half of everything she had running up a thread she'd tied in the dark to a girl asleep in a bed on Tallow Lane.

Keeping me.

*Let me carry this one,* I'd said to Severin, on the Dial, with my hand on his heart.

"Yes," I said.

---

I stood up.

I didn't want to let go of her. I did anyway. I stood up out of the pool of light, with my grey wool soaked gold to the knees, and I turned, and I walked across the black stone to Lady Aurelia Thane, and I held out my left arm.

She took my wrist.

Her fingers were cool and dry and very light. Like being held by a moth. She turned my arm over in the light from my mother, and looked at my count, seventy-one years and four months, bare and gold, and her face went soft with something that on anybody else I'd have called love.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, *look* at you."

The binder came forward with his cushion. He picked up the silver needle. It was fine as a hair, finer than the one that had threaded me to Severin in the Tithe Court on the first day of the term. He took a spool of thread from his sleeve, and it was silver too, so fine it looked like a line of light.

"It will hurt," he said. To me. Not to her.

It did.

Not like the broker's needle under the cistern. Not like the threading in the Tithe Court. Worse. It went in under my skin, just above Severin's silver line and the raw red welt of Bry's black one, and around, and I felt it go, cold, and then burning, and then cold again, a cold that went down into the bone. The binder drew the thread across the space between my wrist and hers. He threaded her. She didn't flinch either. She closed her eyes, and smiled.

And as he did it, something spoke inside me.

Not in my ear. Not in the air. Inside. Down the other thread, the old one, the one I'd never seen and had felt every day for ten years as heat in the dark. My mother's voice. Not cracked and dry like it had been in the room. Clear. Close. The voice I remembered from the end of my bed, tracing circles in the air.

*Not yet,* said my mother.

I didn't move. I didn't look at her.

*Not yet, Tamsin. Let her. Let her have it. Wait.*

*Wait for what?* I didn't say it out loud. I didn't know if I could ask it at all. But I asked it, somewhere, in the part of me that knows my own count.

And she answered.

*For him. The boy with the clocks. He's coming. I can feel him in you.* A pause. *Wait for him.*

The binder said a word I didn't know, and tied a knot in the air between my wrist and Lady Aurelia's. Small. Neat. Flat. A double loop with the tail tucked under.

The thread sank into my skin and vanished.

---

And I felt her.

I'd felt Severin's heartbeat for three months, a drum in another room, slow and even, a heart trained into steadiness. I'd felt Bry's for a quarter of an hour, a bird in a box. I'd felt my mother's through the silver door three nights ago, slow and deep and then quickening to meet mine.

Lady Aurelia Thane's heartbeat was not like any of them.

It was slow. Slower than Severin's. Slower than anything alive. And cold. Not cold like the cistern, a cold you could feel on your skin. Cold the way a room is cold when nobody has lived in it for a very long time. It beat once, and then there was nothing, a long empty silence like a corridor in a shut house, and then it beat again. Like a clock in a cellar that nobody had wound in a hundred years, still going, somehow, by itself.

And it was *hungry*.

I felt the hunger come down the new thread toward me like a mouth opening.

She opened her eyes. They were shining.

"Oh," breathed Lady Aurelia. "*Oh*, yes."

And above us, in the dark, something moved.

---

I heard it first in my bones.

A sound, from very far above. From the top of the dark, past the end of the pendulum shaft, up where the silver threads from my mother's wrists ran away into nothing. A groan. Deep and grinding and enormous, like the sluice opening under the island but bigger, much bigger, as if the whole tower above us were a door on old hinges and somebody had put their shoulder to it for the first time in three hundred years.

The floor shook. Dust came down out of the dark overhead in a long grey sifting curtain, and the lamps in the passage behind us flickered.

The silver threads running up from my mother's wrists began to sing. Not the high sweet note of the door. A deeper sound, a thrumming, like the strings of a harp being plucked one after another, all of them, all the way up.

The binder dropped his cushion. The two Thane guards at the silver door had their swords half drawn and their faces turned up into the dark.

Lady Aurelia Thane was looking up.

For the first time since I'd known her, she wasn't smiling.

Above my mother's bowed head, the pendulum moved.

The great black iron disc on its shaft, as wide as a cartwheel, that had hung perfectly still in the dark for three hundred years, that I'd seen through the grate in Severin's ceiling every night for a whole term, stirred. Shivered. And then, slowly, slowly, with a sound like a ship's keel grinding over stone, it began to swing. Out, over our heads, in a long low arc through the dark. Away. And back.

Once.

And somewhere far above us, in the works, I heard it. I knew what it was. Severin had shown it to me through a loupe at a velvet table with his breath against my ear. *The escapement. It catches the wheel. Lets it go one tooth. Catches it again.*

A tooth went forward. And caught.

The sound of it came down through the whole Horologe, through the rock, through the floor, through my feet and my bones and the three threads on my wrist. A single enormous *tick*. Like a door closing on the world.

Lady Aurelia's cold slow heartbeat stuttered in my wrist.

Under the swinging pendulum, in her pool of light, my mother lifted her head. She looked straight at me. And in her face, for just a moment, there was something I'd never seen there, not once, not even at the end of my bed in the dark when I was small.

She was smiling.

Somewhere above us, for the first time in three hundred years, the clock began to count.
