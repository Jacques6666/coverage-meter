# Chapter Eleven

## Lessons

He sent for me three days after Wat's list.

Not at midnight this time. At the ninth bell, when the Hall was still lit and everyone was sitting on their beds mending stockings or playing knucklebones on the floor, Steward Rook unlocked the iron door and looked down the room and said, "Fenn. The tower." And then, as I was pulling on my boots, she added, with her mouth twisted as though the words tasted of vinegar: "He says you're to come every night this week. *Lessons.*"

The whole Hall went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with fear.

Somebody at the far end whistled, low. Bry, on the next bed, didn't look up from the stocking she was darning, but the corner of her mouth went up and stayed there.

"Shut it," I said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

"I was going to say *wear a ribbon*," said Bry. "That's all. Thane black, something tasteful."

I threw my pillow at her. She caught it without looking and stuffed it behind her own head.

---

The door at the top of the tower opened by itself, like always, and the clocks were ticking, like always, and the great stopped gears hung in the dark overhead. But the room had changed.

He'd cleared the velvet table. All the little tools were gone, packed away somewhere, and the watches with them. In their place there was a slate, propped against a stack of books, covered in neat chalk columns. Beside it lay a fat volume bound in grey cloth, and a stick of chalk, and a cloth. There were two chairs drawn up to the table, side by side, not across from each other.

He was standing at the window with his back to me.

"Sit down," said Severin.

I didn't. I stood just inside the door with my arms folded, the way I'd stood the first night.

"You threw it," I said.

He didn't turn around.

"The Dial. You threw it. You stopped in the middle and let him freeze you. Because of my arm." I'd had three weeks to work out how I'd say it, and it still came out wrong, too loud and too fast. "Why?"

"I lost," said Severin. "That's all that happened. My cousin was faster and I lost."

"Your cousin couldn't still a cup of water without spilling it on his own boots."

That made him turn around. Not much. Just his head, so that the lamplight caught one side of his face. Something moved at the corner of his mouth, and didn't stay.

"Your arm is my arm, Fenn. For the term. If you're caught, I'm caught. If Lysander had seen what was under your hand, he'd have been at my grandmother's door before dinner." He walked to the table and pulled out one of the chairs. "Sit down. That's why you're here. You guessed, on the Dial. You were guessing what I spent, and you guessed wrong, and you panicked, and you sweated through your ink. Guessing gets people killed."

"So you're going to teach me."

"I'm going to teach you."

"Teach me what?"

He tapped the grey book on the table. "The Table of Hours. What every working costs, for every caster, at every distance. Every first-year wright learns it by heart before the Long Night. You're going to learn it by Seventhday." He looked at me. "If you're going to lie about magic, learn it."

I looked at the book. I looked at the slate, with its rows of tiny chalk numbers. I looked at him, standing there by the chair he'd pulled out for me, like a footman in a Gilt drawing room.

I sat down.

---

He was a good teacher. I hadn't expected that.

I'd expected him to lecture, the way the magisters did in the Lantern Theatre, standing in the circle with their hands behind their backs. Instead he sat beside me at the velvet table and opened the grey book flat between us and went through it page by page, one finger running down the columns, and asked me questions.

"A hastening. Ten paces. A man of my height and weight."

"A day and a half."

"Wrong. A day and seven hours. Weight matters. Why?"

"Because you're moving more of yourself."

"Good. Fifteen paces."

"Two days. And—" I looked at the column. "Eleven hours."

"A stilling. A sword in flight, three feet from the caster."

"Six hours."

"At ten feet?"

"Nine." I was doing it in my head now, faster than I could find it on the page. "Every foot past three costs half an hour more, until twenty, and then it's an hour a foot, because the working has to reach."

He stopped with his finger on the column. He looked at me sideways.

"Who taught you that?"

"You did. Ten minutes ago."

"I told you the rule. I didn't tell you why." He was still looking at me. "You worked out the why."

"I kept Mother Gannet's books for seven years," I said. "Brokers cheat on distance all the time. A man comes in to buy two years, he wants them threaded across the room, the broker says it costs extra to reach. Same rule. Half a crown a foot past the counter." I shrugged. "Nobody likes to pay for the reach."

He looked at me for a moment longer. Then he made a sound I hadn't heard him make before, short and low in his throat, that I realized after a second was a laugh.

"You'd have made a good wright," he said.

"I'd have made a terrible one."

"Why?"

"I can't bear to spend anything." I said it lightly, and it came out heavier than I meant. "You can't be a wright if every working feels like somebody's taking money out of your pocket."

He didn't say anything to that. He turned the page.

---

We worked until the eleventh bell, and then he walked me down the four hundred and twelve steps to Rook's landing and the two hundred more to the bottom, saying nothing, with a lamp, and left me at the iron door of the Hall. The next night we did it again. And the next.

By the third night I had the first half of the Table of Hours by heart. By the third night I'd also stopped standing by the door when I came in. I'd come in and sit down in my chair, and he'd already have the book open to the page we'd stopped at, and the lamp turned up, and sometimes, without either of us mentioning it, a cup of tea at my elbow going cold.

On the third night I said, without looking up from the page: "Orrin Fisk had fifty-three years."

His finger stopped on the column.

"At the Market," I said. "Fifty-three. And he died on the fourteenth of Deepwinter. After midwinter."

The clocks ticked. A little brass one on the windowsill chimed the half hour, very sweetly, and nobody looked at it.

"You've been reading the weighing books," said Severin.

"Somebody has."

"Who?"

"Nobody."

He knew that was a lie. I knew he knew. He looked at me for a long moment, and his face didn't do anything at all.

"Leave Orrin alone," he said quietly.

"I just think it's interesting. That he doesn't fit."

"Leave him *alone*, Fenn."

It wasn't a threat. That was the strange thing. It sounded more like asking.

I left him alone. For that night.

---

On the fourth night he didn't open the book.

"You know the numbers," he said. "Now you need to know what they feel like. So you'll know, without looking, what I'm spending." He pulled his chair around so that he was facing me, knee to knee, almost, with the corner of the velvet table between us. "Give me your wrist."

I hesitated. Then I held out my left arm, palm up, and laid it on the corner of the table.

He didn't take it. He laid his own left hand on the velvet beside it, gloved, palm up too, so that our wrists lay side by side an inch apart with the two silver threads gleaming in the lamplight.

"Close your eyes," he said.

"Why?"

"Because you'll feel it better. And because you'll stop watching my face to see if I'm lying." A pause. "I'm not, tonight."

I closed my eyes.

"The thread," he said. His voice was close and low. "Find it."

I didn't have to look. I'd been feeling it for five weeks. The second heartbeat, slow and even, under my own. In the dark behind my eyelids it was louder, and closer, a drum heard through a floor.

"I can feel your pulse."

"Good. Now don't listen to the pulse. Listen to the line. The thread itself. It's like a rope between us, with a little give in it. Can you feel the give?"

I tried. It took a while. And then, all at once, I could: not a sound but a kind of tension, a faint pull in the silver at my wrist, the way you can feel a kite string tugging even when you can't see the kite. A line running out of me, across the inch of velvet, into him.

"Yes."

"I'm going to light a match. A quickening. Seven minutes. Tell me what you feel."

I heard the scrape of something on the table. Then, very faintly, a sound like a fingertip on the rim of a glass.

And I felt it.

Warmth. Moving. The same thing I'd felt in the Lantern Theatre and on the Dial, a current of something gold and alive flowing along the line, but slower now, a trickle instead of a flood, so that I could follow it. I followed it. It wasn't coming from me. It didn't come past me, either, like I'd thought, like water past a stone. It didn't come from anywhere I could find. It started in him. Right there, an inch away, in his wrist. It welled up out of him and ran down into his fingers and out into the match, and then it stopped.

I smelled sulfur. I opened my eyes. The match was burning between his gloved finger and thumb, a small clean flame.

"Well?" he said.

"It didn't come from me."

"No."

"I mean—" I stared at his wrist, at the silver cuff above it. "It didn't come *through* the thread at all. When Fitch lit the candle, I saw it. It came out of Joss's arm and went up the thread into Fitch and down into the wick. Like water through a pipe. This didn't do that. This just started in you. Like you were—" I stopped.

He shook the match out. He set it down on the velvet, very neatly, parallel to the edge of the slate.

"Everything a wright casts comes through the wright," he said.

"That's not what I mean and you know it."

"Seven minutes," he said. "You felt seven minutes. That's good. Most first-years can't feel anything under an hour."

"Severin."

It was the first time I'd said his name. It came out of my mouth before I knew it was there. He went still.

"Where does it come from?" I said. "Where does *your* time come from, if it doesn't come from me?"

The clocks ticked. Up in the dark above the grate, the great stopped engine of the Horologe hung over us both.

"That," said Severin Thane, "still isn't part of the arrangement."

---

But it was different, after that. I don't know how else to say it. He didn't push his chair back around to the side of the table. He opened a drawer instead, and took out a watch.

It was a plain silver one, not the little gold lady's watch with the *L*. He opened the back of it with his thumbnail and set it on the velvet between us, and pulled the lamp closer, and handed me his loupe.

"Look," he said. "Here."

I screwed the loupe into my eye the way I'd seen him do it, awkwardly. The inside of the watch leapt up at me, enormous: a tiny city of brass and steel, wheels within wheels, all turning, all ticking. And at the heart of it, rocking back and forth, back and forth, a little golden wheel with a hair-fine coil of spring at its center, and beside it a small forked lever that caught and released, caught and released, a toothed wheel beside it.

"That's the escapement," said Severin. His voice was right by my ear. "The fork and the wheel. That's the most important part of any clock."

"Why?"

"Because the spring wants to unwind all at once. That's all a spring is: a lot of stored time, trying to get out. If you let it go, it would spin every wheel in that watch to nothing in a second, and the watch would be useless." He leaned in, very slightly, and pointed with the tip of a tweezer, careful not to touch anything. "The escapement doesn't let it. It catches the wheel. Lets it go one tooth. Catches it again. One tick at a time. That's all a clock is, really. Something that stops time from running out all at once."

I watched the little fork catch and release, catch and release.

*That's what my ink is,* I thought. *My escapement. A thing that lets my time out one day at a time, so nobody sees the spring.*

I didn't say it. I took the loupe out of my eye and gave it back to him, and our fingers didn't touch, and the place where they didn't touch felt warm anyway.

"It's beautiful," I said.

He looked at the watch for a moment. Then he looked at me. "Yes," he said.

He looked away first.

---

It was late. Past the eleventh bell. I should have gone, and I didn't, and he didn't tell me to. He'd started taking the silver watch apart, the way some people bite their nails, his hands busy and his face gone quiet and absorbed in the lamplight. I sat beside him with my chin on my fist and watched.

His gloves were too clumsy for the hairspring. I watched him try twice, with the tweezers, to lift the tiny coil out of its seat, and both times the leather fingertip brushed the balance wheel and sent it rocking. On the third try, without seeming to notice he was doing it, he pulled off his right glove with his teeth and dropped it on the table.

The bandage was gone from his palm. There was a long pink scar across it, healing.

He tried again, bare-fingered. Better. But the silver cuff on his left arm kept catching on the edge of the velvet as he leaned in, the heavy edge of it dragging at the cloth and jolting his hand. Once. Twice. He made a small irritated sound in his throat.

And then he did something I'd never seen him do.

He reached across with his bare right hand, still looking at the watch, still frowning at the hairspring, and flicked the clasp of the cuff open with one fingernail. He slid it off his left forearm the way you'd push up a sleeve that was in the way. He set it down on the velvet beside the slate with a soft heavy clink.

He didn't look at me. He didn't seem to remember I was there. He just bent back over the watch.

I looked at his arm.

It was pale, paler than his hands, the skin of someone who hadn't seen sunlight on his forearm since he was seven. Lean, corded with fine muscle. And running up the inside of it, from the wrist bone to the crease of the elbow, a ladder of lines. A long one. Longer than mine. A Thane count, a Gilt count, a count you'd be born with if you were born on the white terraces under the right roof.

Almost every line of it was dark.

Grey. Flat, cold grey, from the elbow down, rung after rung after rung, the way a count looks when it's been spent. Not sold; you can tell, when you've worked a counter. Sold time goes brown at the edges. This was *burned*, line after line of it, burned and gone out, so many of them that I lost count trying to count them.

And at the bottom, at the wrist, near the silver thread, a little row of gold.

Nine lines. Nine.

I counted them three times.

He must have felt it. My heart, through the thread. It must have gone through him like a dropped stone. His hands stopped on the watch. He went very, very still, the hairspring held in the tweezers above the balance wheel, and I watched him understand, slowly, all at once, what he'd done.

He didn't reach for the cuff. He didn't move at all.

"You've got nine years," I said.

My voice came out of me like somebody else's. Small, and very far away.

He set the tweezers down. Parallel to the edge of the slate.

"Severin," I said. "You're *dying*."
