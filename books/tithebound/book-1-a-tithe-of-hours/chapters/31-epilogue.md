# Epilogue

## Severin

The whole island felt it.

He was lying in a narrow bed in the Meridian's infirmary with his left arm on top of the blanket and his eyes on the ceiling, counting his own heartbeat because it was the only thing in the room he could still count on, when the tick came down through the stone.

It went through everything. The bed frame. The floor. The glass in the long window, which rang like a struck bell. Down the row of beds, a Lisle tithe who'd been asleep sat bolt upright and screamed. A physic dropped a tray of bottles. And Severin Thane lay very still with his hand flat on the blanket and felt it go through his bones, through his teeth, through the silver thread at his wrist, one single enormous *tick*, like a door closing on the world.

Then the bells began.

Not the Meridian's. All of them. Every bell in Lanthorn, from the bronze alarm in the Horologe over his head to the cracked tin bell of the chapel in the Sump, ringing and ringing, out of time with each other, the way his clocks did. Out in the court, people were shouting. Running feet. A woman's voice, very high: *Look! Look at it! Look at the hand!*

He got up.

His legs didn't want to. He made them. He went to the long window with one hand on the wall, the way he'd seen Agathe Rook go up the tower stairs on bad days, and looked out across the court and up.

The face of the Horologe hung over the island in the grey afternoon light, pale and pitted, wide as a pond, with its Gilt numerals of beaten gold. And its single iron hand, longer than a house, which had pointed at eleven for three hundred years, every day of his life and his father's life and his grandmother's grandmother's life, pointed at one minute past.

He stood at the window and looked at it until his eyes burned.

---

His count read four years, two months.

He'd looked at it as soon as they put him in the bed, the way you'd look at a wound. He looked at it again now, in the window light, with the bells still going. Four gold lines at the wrist. Two notches. And above them, to the elbow, all that grey.

Four years. He'd had nine on Tithe Day. He'd never minded the nine. He minded the four. He minded them so much he could hardly breathe.

Because she was in them.

He could feel her. Through the thread. Far away and far below, under the stone, under the tower, under all of it, the way he'd felt her in the cistern three weeks ago, going into the cold. Her heartbeat, fast and steady and alive. He'd been holding on to it since the Dial like a man holding a rope over a drop.

But it wasn't alone anymore.

Underneath it, coming up the line toward him through her, there was something else. Something slow. Slower than anything alive. Cold, the way a shut house is cold. It beat once, and then there was nothing, a long empty silence like a corridor, and then it beat again.

He knew that heartbeat. He'd felt it once before in his life, at sixteen, in the long gallery at Thane House, through a small cool hand laid over his own on Liesl Varga's thread.

His grandmother was on the other end of Tamsin's wrist.

He put his forehead against the cold glass and shut his eyes.

---

When he opened them, he put his hand in the pocket of the coat they'd hung on the hook by his bed. He didn't know why. To have something to hold, maybe. His fingers found what he always carried, what he'd carried onto the Dial that morning against his chest like a stone.

He took it out.

Liesl's watch. Small and gold, cheap gold plate, the crystal smashed into a star, an *L* on the case worn almost smooth by a thumb. The watch he'd taken apart and put back together a hundred times in five years. The only one he couldn't fix. Its hands had stopped at twenty past four on the afternoon after his sixteenth birthday, and they had never moved since.

It was ticking.

He held it in his palm. He held it up to his ear, like Kit had held the tin one on the bare boards of a room on Tallow Lane. Faint. Tiny. Steady. The smallest voice in the world.

Under the broken crystal, the minute hand had moved off twenty past four.

---

"Thane."

He turned. Captain Hesketh Marr was standing in the infirmary doorway in his worn grey coat, with his hat in his hand and his long jaw set and his washed-out eyes very tired.

"Captain."

Marr looked at him. At the watch in his hand. At his bare arm with its four gold lines. Then he walked down the row of beds, past the screaming tithe and the physic sweeping up glass, and took Severin's black coat down off its hook, and held it out.

"I've had a long look at that Echo," said Marr. "And a long look at those lists. And a longer one at what happened to the Crane boy on the Dial." He paused. "The Houses are in the Ledger House, shouting at each other about the clock. The Keeper's not come up from wherever she went. Nobody's watching this room." He pushed the coat into Severin's hands. "I never saw you leave it."

Severin looked at him.

"Mother Gannet's," said Captain Marr quietly. "The Wick end of your tunnel. Some people are meeting there tonight who'd be very interested to hear what you've got to say. The Vey woman's one of them." He put his hat on. "And I'd be quick, son. When the Keeper comes up out of the ground, she'll want to know where everybody is."

He went out.

---

The Thane passage was dark and cold and wet, and Severin went down it without a lamp. He didn't need one. He'd walked it a hundred times, carrying Orrin Fisk in a box, carrying lists, carrying himself back from the sluice too late. He knew every step.

At the fork, he stopped.

The iron gate across the old dark arch on the left. The steps going down beyond it, into the black. The chain wrapped three times through the bars, which he'd aged to rust with every working he had and which hadn't so much as flaked.

He put his bare hand on the bars.

They were warm.

And through them, up from the dark, up through the stone, the beat. Slow. Deep. Patient. And under it, faint and fast and far away, a heartbeat he'd have known anywhere, that he'd been feeling under his own for a whole winter. Tamsin's.

He stood there for a long moment with his hand on the warm iron.

"I'm coming," he said to the dark. Out loud. He didn't care how it sounded. "I'm coming. Wait for me."

The thread at his wrist went warm, just for a moment. As if something, very far below, had heard.

---

Mother Gannet's back room was full.

He came up through the trapdoor in the cellar of crates and through the derelict chandlery and along the alley in the fog and in at the back door of the shop with no sign, and stopped in the doorway, because it was full of people, and every one of them had turned to look at him.

Gannet herself, enormous in her dozen shawls in her chair by the stove, with rings on every finger and her small clever eyes. Magister Vadra, standing by the curtained window in her dark green gown, with the echo-lens in its brass frame on the table in front of her, still glowing faintly. A handful of faces he didn't know: Wick faces, men and women in worn coats, with old white thread-scars on their wrists, who looked at him the way you'd look at a dog that might bite.

And at the end of the long table, in a row, as if they'd been waiting for him: Hollis Penhallow, white as paper, with dried tears on his freckles and his tin cuff pushed back; Bryony Kettle, with a split lip and a black eye and her arms folded; and Pell Darrow, with a crushed primrose in his huge hand.

"We came on the laundry cart," said Bry, before anyone else could say anything. "Don't ask."

"I was under the nightshirts," said Hollis faintly. "It was very undignified. I may never recover."

Severin stood in the doorway. He didn't know what to do with his face. So he didn't do anything with it.

"Well," said Mother Gannet, in a wheeze like a bellows. "Look what the river spat out. Sit down, Thane, before you fall down. You look like a heron that's been hit by a cart."

He didn't sit down.

He walked to the table, and put his left hand on it, flat, with the cuff off and his four years bare to the lamplight and to all of them. He looked at Vadra. Then at Gannet. Then at the faces he didn't know, with their old white scars.

"I need to learn," said Severin, "how to cut a Ledger thread."

Nobody said anything. The stove ticked.

Then Magister Vadra came away from the window, and stood across the table from him, tall and straight, with her sleeves down over her knuckles.

"And what is it," she said, very quietly, "that you want to steal from your grandmother, Thane?"

He thought about it. He thought about a velvet table and a slate full of chalk sums. About a room full of clocks that all disagreed. About a Wick girl in a dead girl's dress on a frozen balcony, and her forehead against his, and their breath in one cloud. About a woman under a pendulum. About a small cool hand laid over his on a thread.

"Everything she ever took," said Severin Thane.

---

Behind Gannet's chair, the curtain to the cellar moved.

Everyone turned.

A small boy stood in the gap. Ten years old, in a coat much too big for him, with the sleeves rolled up four times, and thin legs, and dark eyes too big for his face. Behind him, in the shadow, loomed the man from the room over the pie shop, with his hand on the boy's shoulder.

In one fist, the boy was holding a cheap tin watch, ticking. In the other, a sheaf of papers.

"Kit," said Bry, in a voice Severin had never heard her use.

Kit Fenn didn't look at her. He looked straight down the table at Severin. And then he came forward, past Gannet's chair, past Vadra, past the people with the white scars on their wrists, and stopped beside him, and laid his papers on the table, and spread them out with both hands.

It was the Meridian. From above, the way a gull would see it. The island and the bridge, and the twenty-four tiny careful lamps. The court. The Tithe Hall under the river, and the cisterns under that, black and wavy. The tower.

And beneath the tower, deep in the rock, the little circle with the heart in it.

Under it, in fresh pencil, in big careful letters that pressed too hard, Kit had written a word. Severin looked at it for a long time.

*MAMA.*

"That's where Tam is," said Kit. His voice was very steady. He'd been crying; you could see it. He wasn't crying now. "Isn't it? With Mama. Under the clock." He looked up. "I heard it. The tick. Everybody did. And my watch—" He held up the tin watch. "My watch stopped. Right then. At the tick. For one whole minute. And then it started again."

Severin reached into his own pocket and took out Liesl's watch, ticking, and laid it on the map beside Kit's. Two cheap watches, side by side, on the circle with the heart in it. Both going.

Kit looked at it. Then at him.

"I know how to get there," said Kit Fenn. He put one thin finger on the heart. "I drew it."

Severin looked down at the map. At the heart under the clock. At the word in pencil. At the two little watches, ticking, out of time with each other, keeping time perfectly.

Four years, he thought. Four years and two months.

It would have to be enough.

He went down on one knee beside the boy, the way he had on the bare boards of a room on Tallow Lane, so that their eyes were level.

"Show me," said Severin Thane.
