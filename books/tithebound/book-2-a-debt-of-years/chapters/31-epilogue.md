# Epilogue

## The Keeper

::: {.pov}
Aurelia
:::

There were four violets left in the silver box.

The Keeper of the Ledger counted them by candlelight, the way she had counted them every night since she walked out through her silver door in white with gold smoking off her arm: with the tip of one small finger, not quite touching, the way one counts anything precious. Four. Sugared and dark and faintly translucent at the edges where the candle shone through, each in its own nest of white paper. A confectioner on the third terrace had made them for her, and only for her, for forty years, and his father before him, and his grandfather before that. She didn't suppose he'd be making any more. Ismay said he'd locked his shop on the second morning and gone down the Long Way to the river flats, to stand in the street with the rest of them and look at his arm.

She closed the box without taking one.

She had never once in her life done that.

---

The room under Lisle House had been built for seven.

It was Founders' work, very old: a low round vault of narrow brick, and seven chairs of black stone set round a black stone table, with the name of a House cut into the back of each. THANE. CRANE. PENHALLOW. VEY. OSTRANDER. MARCHBANK. LISLE. The Founders had met here in the first years after the Drowning, while the Gilt was still building its white terraces over their heads, because there were things the Seven needed to say to one another that could not be said anywhere a servant might stand behind a door. Her mother had sat in that chair. Aurelia could remember being carried down here once, very small, asleep on someone's shoulder, and waking to candlelight and low voices and seven tall shadows on the curved brick.

Three hundred years of Lisles had kept the room swept and told nobody. It was exactly like a Lisle, Aurelia thought, to keep a secret for three hundred years purely for the pleasure of having one.

A low door in the far wall opened onto the old Lisle tunnel, which ran from here under the terraces and the flats and the river, all the way to the round room under the clock. Captain Marr's men had found the other end of it on the second day and bricked it up. Aurelia had found that very funny, for nearly a minute.

Two of the chairs were occupied.

She sat in THANE, in a borrowed gown of Lisle white that did nothing for her and that, a week ago, she would not have been seen dead in. Across the table, in LISLE, wrapped in white fur against the cold, with her silver hair cropped short as a boy's and her long clever face sharp in the candlelight, sat Ismay. Between them the other five chairs stood empty round the table, like guests who had been very much delayed.

"You've been looking at that box for an hour," said Ismay Lisle.

"Have I?"

"You have." Ismay's fox's eyes went to the box and back. "Eat the thing or don't. I'm not sitting down here all night watching you be sentimental about confectionery."

"You're sitting down here all night," said Aurelia pleasantly, "because if you go up, Captain Marr will hang you beside Kell Durrow. Which I imagine would be very sentimental indeed."

Ismay didn't answer that. She drew her fur closer round her shoulders and looked at the candle.

---

Aurelia pushed back her sleeve.

She'd been doing it all evening, every few minutes, the way a girl with a new ring keeps looking at her hand. She despised herself for it a little and did it anyway. The long gold blaze from wrist to shoulder, the count that had been the wonder of the Gilt for two hundred years, that people had come to her salons simply to see.

Smoking.

There was no better word for it. A thin, shining, ceaseless smoke rising off it like breath on a winter morning, drifting up into the candlelight and away through the brick to wherever it went. Home. The first night, in the dark of the tunnel, she had tried to stop it with everything she had. She'd held her arm tight against her body and put her other hand over it and tried, absurdly, the way a child cups a hand round a candle in a draft, to draw it back into herself.

It came out between her fingers.

A sixtieth part, every day. She could do the arithmetic. She had been doing arithmetic for two hundred and forty-seven years.

Three days ago the blaze had run up over her shoulder and along her collarbone like a necklace; she'd been able to see the end of it in a glass. Tonight it stopped at the shoulder.

Across the table, Ismay's arm was smoking too, under the white fur. Aurelia could see the faint shine of it rising past her cheek. Ismay was pretending not to notice. Ismay had always been better at pretending than she was given credit for.

Aurelia pulled her sleeve down and laid her hand on the table, palm up, and looked at the inside of her own wrist.

There was nothing on it now. The Ledger thread was gone; her great-great-great-grandson had cut it in the round room, in the slack of a blink, with a little laundress's knife, and she had felt it part. For six weeks before that, she had carried the girl's heartbeat at her wrist, morning and night, quick and angry and young, beating against her skin like a bird in a pair of cupped hands. She'd grown used to it. She had fallen asleep to it.

She missed it. Absurdly. The way one misses a clock one has stopped hearing.

---

Somewhere far above them, faint through the earth and the brick and the wine cellars of Lisle House, a bell began to ring.

Then another. Then all of them: the Gilt chapels, and the Watch House, and the iron bell of the Candleworks down on the flats. The ordinary bells, keeping ordinary time for people who could afford to care what time it was. Midnight. The small kind.

She counted them, the way she had counted them every night for a month.

Twelve.

"Fifty-four days, Ismay," said the Keeper.

"I can count."

"I know you can, dear. I'm saying it aloud so that one of us will."

Ismay's mouth went thin. "Corwin went up at dusk," she said, "to see who's left. Half the Inner Ledger's shut up in their houses with the curtains drawn, weeping into their pillows. The other half are packing."

"Packing."

"For Calder Bay. For the islands. For anywhere."

"As if one could outrun a clock," said Aurelia. "Let them pack. Let them sit in a cold inn in Calder Bay with the sea outside the window and watch their arms for a fortnight. Then they'll come home. They'll come down that stair, every one of them, and kneel on these bricks, and kiss our hands, and give us anything we ask for. Their silver. Their men. Their grandchildren's years." She smiled. "Fear is very clarifying, dear. I've found that."

"And then?" Ismay leaned forward into the candlelight. "You can't tie it again, Aurelia. You haven't got a well. Your Wick woman's finished. You said so yourself, in the tunnel. I heard you."

"Maren Fenn is spent," said the Keeper.

She said it gently. Almost with regret.

"Ten years under the clock," she said, "and then the Stay cut out of her in the slack. I felt her go quiet when it parted. Like a river going under the ice." She turned the silver box a quarter turn on the stone with one finger. "Oh, the well's still in her. A little. A pond, where there was a river. My mother told me it would happen, at the end. The one before Maren went the same way, in her last year, after a hundred and forty. Quiet. Restful. Then gone." She looked up. "You can't tie a Stay to a pond."

"Then you've nothing."

"I didn't say that." Her dimples came. "Maren Fenn is spent. But the girl..."

Ismay sat back. "The girl," she said, "would rather watch every arm in the Gilt go grey than lift one finger for you."

"Oh, she would. Gladly. She'd stand on the river wall and cheer." Aurelia folded her small hands on the table. "She'd let you and me go home tomorrow, Ismay, and the whole Inner Ledger with us, and dance in the street with her Wick while we went. She'll go on wanting it, too." A pause. "Right up until she looks at her brother's arm."

Ismay Lisle went still.

"The given unto the giver," said Aurelia softly. "You've read it. We've all read it; every Keeper reads it on the day she takes the stair, and every Lisle who ever tried to steal my wells has read it too. Thirty-one years on that child's arm, Ismay. Thirty of them were *given*. On a Tithe Day, by my grandson, out of my own reserve, through a Ledger needle, all perfectly proper." She tilted her head. "Do you know what the boy was born with?"

Ismay didn't answer.

"Eleven years," said the Keeper. "He's ten. At midnight, he'll have what's his." She let that lie on the table between them for a moment, in the candlelight, among the five empty chairs. "And my grandson. Every year the girl ever poured into him goes back to her. Every one. And all the years of his own that he burned to spite me go nowhere at all. *The spent unto none.*" She smiled. "He's been burning since he was a boy. I doubt what's left would fill a thimble."

"She may not know," said Ismay slowly. "Not yet. The page—"

"She doesn't need the page. She was a *counter*, Ismay. She sat at a broker's counter on the Row for seven years and read the arm of every poor fool who came through the door. In a day or two that boy will push up his sleeve the way Wick children do, and she'll look at it, and she'll see it going the wrong way." Aurelia's warm brown eyes were very bright. "And then she'll do the arithmetic. The way they do, down there. On their fingers."

The candle guttered and steadied. Somewhere above them, the last of the bells stopped ringing.

"And then?" said Ismay.

"And then she'll come down my stair on her own two feet," said the Keeper. "In whatever she happens to be wearing. And kneel by the well, and hold out her arms." She smiled across the table. "*Willing.* The Charter's very particular, I'm told."

Ismay recognized her own words, spoken on a barge behind a fold of white awning, out of anyone's earshot. Aurelia watched her recognize them, and wonder how, and enjoyed it very much.

"And when she's kneeling there," said Ismay at last, "and the knot's to be tied. Whose hand ties it, Aurelia? Yours, or mine?"

"We'll discuss it, dear."

"We will."

"You're very sure of her," Ismay said, after a while. "Of a Wick girl you've known for two months."

"I've met the family."

Aurelia looked at the candle.

"Ten years ago," she said, "I stood under a lamp at the bottom of a lane in the Wick, in the fog, and waited for a woman to come out of her door. And she came. And she saw me." She was quiet for a moment. "She thought about running. I watched her think about it: the alleys of the Sump, and the river, and the dark. You could have driven a carriage through the gaps between my men. And then she looked back over her shoulder at a window above a chandler's shop, and I watched her think about the stairs." A small smile. "I thought it was the baby she was looking back at. I know better now."

The smoke rose off her arm, steady, shining, toward the brick.

"She walked to me," said Aurelia Thane. "All the way down that lane, in the fog, with her chin up. She never ran." She lifted her eyes to Ismay's. "Neither will the girl. She'll come down those stairs on her own two feet, for the little one with the cough. For the boy with the clocks." The dimples came out in her round sweet face. "They always come for family."

She opened the silver box, then, and took out one of the four violets, and put it on her tongue.
