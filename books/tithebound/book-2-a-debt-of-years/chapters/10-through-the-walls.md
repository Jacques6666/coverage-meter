# Chapter Ten

## Through the Walls

::: {.pov}
Severin
:::

The way in was under the old ice house.

He'd forgotten that until he was standing in front of it. Fifteen years since he'd last been down here, a boy of six with a candle and his father's hand on his shoulder, and he'd remembered the tunnels, the long dark brick throats under the Gilt that the Founders had dug before they'd built a single house on top of them, and he'd remembered the cellars of Thane House. But he'd forgotten how you got from one to the other. And then he came round the last bend of the Founders' tunnel with Kit's map in his pocket and a candle stub in his hand and saw it: a round brick chamber, domed, with a drain in the floor and an iron ladder going up the wall into the dark, and he smelled cold stone and old straw and remembered.

*The ice house, Sev. They used to cut ice off the Sallow in winter and pack it down here in straw, so the Thanes could have cold wine in August. Nobody's used it in a hundred years. Your great-grandmother doesn't like cold wine.*

He climbed the ladder.

At the top there was a trapdoor, and above the trapdoor there was the ice house itself, a low stone hut half buried in the snow at the bottom of the Thane gardens, and on the far wall of the ice house, behind a rotting stack of straw bales, there was a door that didn't look like a door. He found it by the draft. Cold air, breathing out of the stone where there was no gap.

*You can always find them by the draft.*

He put his hand flat on the stone and pushed, and it moved.

---

The passages between the walls of Thane House were exactly as he remembered and much smaller. He'd been six. He'd walked them upright, holding his father's hand. Now he had to go sideways, with his shoulders scraping both walls and his head bent under beams that had once been far above him, and the candle flame bending in the drafts, and dust in his mouth.

He knew the way. That surprised him. His feet knew it, the way your feet know the stairs of a house you grew up in, even in the dark. Up the narrow stone steps behind the kitchens. Left at the place where you could hear the ovens through the wall. Up again, a ladder this time, iron rungs set into brick. Along a passage so narrow it was really just a gap between two walls, with the backs of portraits on one side and lath on the other, and voices, faint, a footman somewhere laughing.

He passed the long gallery. He knew it by the cold. The passage ran behind the west wall of it for forty feet, and through the backs of the portraits he could feel the room on the other side, huge and empty and dark, and he didn't stop.

*Every Thane needs a way out of his own house, Sev. Don't tell your great-grandmother.*

He'd never told her. He'd never used them, either, not once, not since his father. Not even the night of the long gallery, when he'd stood in his boy's room at the top of the east wing with Liesl's watch in his hand and thought about it. Where would he have gone? There was nowhere a Thane could go that his grandmother couldn't follow.

There was somewhere now.

---

He knew which room. He'd known since Hollis came down the ladder after the salon and told him: *the Violet Rooms, in the west wing, the old ones, at the end*. They'd been his great-great-grandfather's sister's, a hundred years ago; she'd died in them. His father had shown him the panel. *If you ever need to go somewhere nobody's looking, Sev, nobody ever looks in the Violet Rooms. They're haunted. Ask anyone.*

He stopped behind the wall. The panel was in front of him, a narrow section of wood with a brass latch on this side, black with age. Through it he could feel the room. Warm. A fire. And empty.

She wasn't there.

He stood very still in the dark with his hand on the latch and felt for her down the thread. There. Close. Closer than she'd been in a month. Moving. Somewhere in the house, along a corridor, slowly, the way you walk when you're walking to think. He felt her stop. He felt her heart go fast, and then slow, and then, for a long time, very strange, as though she were listening to something she didn't want to hear.

He waited.

He waited a long time. The candle burned down to a stub and he pinched it out and waited in the dark. He'd waited in the dark for her before, on a bridge, in a tunnel, in the black water at an iron gate all night. He could wait behind a wall.

Then she was coming. Down the corridor. Toward the room. He felt her get closer through the thread, each step, and his heart was going so hard he was afraid she'd hear it through the panel.

The door of the room opened and closed. A man's voice: *good night, miss*.

He lit the candle stub again with hands that weren't steady. He lifted the latch.

---

She was standing just inside the door.

In a plain grey wrapper, of all things, the only plain thing he'd ever seen in the Violet Rooms. Her hair down. Her face turned toward the wall where the panel was opening, and pale, and very still. And on her left arm, under the grey sleeve, her count glowing gold in the dim red light of the dying fire, seventy-one years and four months of it, lighting the room the way it had lit his boy's room at the top of the east wing on the night of the Long Night.

He stepped out of the wall and held out his hand.

He didn't know why. He hadn't planned it. It was the way he'd held out his hand to her at the First Draw, in the Lantern Theatre, palm up, for a wrist. *You're drawn on. It hurts. Act like it.* It was the only way he knew how to ask for anything.

She looked at his hand. Then at his face.

And then she crossed the room so fast he barely saw her move, and he dropped the candle.

---

He'd thought about it. Every night for a month. On the cot in Gannet's cellar, with his hand over his wrist, he'd thought about what he'd say.

He didn't say any of it.

He held her. That was all, for a long time. He stood in the middle of the Violet Rooms in the dark with the candle stub out on the carpet somewhere at his feet and held her so hard he was afraid he'd hurt her, and she held him harder, with her face in his coat and her hands fisted in the back of it, and neither of them said anything at all. He could feel her heart. Not through the thread. Through her ribs, against his. Fast, and real, and *here*.

Then she pulled back far enough to look up at him, and took his face in both her hands, and kissed him.

He'd forgotten. Not forgotten; you don't forget. But he'd made it into something else, over a month of lying in the dark, something careful and rationed, an hour a word. It wasn't careful. It was the frozen balcony at Thane House on the Long Night and his old room in the east wing afterward and the tower and the clocks, all of it at once, and her hands in his hair and her mouth on his, and the thread at his wrist ringing like a struck bell.

When they stopped, they didn't stop far. Her forehead against his. Their breath in one cloud, the way it had been on the balcony.

"You came through the *wall*," she whispered.

"I did say," he said. His voice wasn't working properly. "In the note. *Learn the house.*"

"That was Pell's note."

"Pell wrote it down. I told him what to write. He spelled *house* with a *w* the first time."

She laughed. It came out wet and helpless and much too loud, and she clapped her own hand over her mouth, and they both froze, listening. Outside the door, in the corridor, nothing. Corran, his grandmother's guard, shifting his feet.

She took her hand off her mouth.

"You stupid boy," she whispered. "You spent a *year* on a shed full of washerwomen."

"You moved the *clock*."

"You'd have been dead by Midsummer."

"You'd have been *caught*—"

"I *was* caught. She came into my room in her nightgown." Her eyes were bright and furious and wet. "I lied to her face."

"Did she believe you?"

"No."

He put his forehead back against hers and closed his eyes.

---

Then he saw her wrist.

She'd lifted her hand to his face again, and the grey sleeve fell back, and there in the gold light of her own count were the threads. Four of them, now. His, old and silver, from the Tithe Court. A raw red welt, black underneath: Bry's, from the cistern. And another silver one. New. Above the others, closest to the hand, tied with a small, neat, flat knot, a double loop with the tail tucked under.

He'd felt it. Every morning at the eighth bell, a cold heart at the other end of her line, drinking. He'd known it was there. He hadn't seen it.

He took her wrist in his hand. Gently. He turned it to the light. He laid his thumb on the Ledger thread, on the knot, and under his thumb, faint and slow and far away, he felt it. The heart like a clock in a cellar. One beat. A long nothing. Another.

His grandmother. Asleep. On his girl's wrist.

He didn't know his face had changed until she put her free hand over his.

"I know," she said quietly. "I know. I feel it every morning."

"I'm going to cut it."

"I know."

"I've been learning. In the Sump. From—" He stopped. He didn't know how to say it. "From Liesl's mother."

She went very still. He watched her understand: her face going from one thing to another, the way it always did, too fast for most people to follow, too fast to hide. Then she put her hand flat on his chest, over his heart.

"Lotte," she said. "Bry told me. Down the black thread. She said there was an old woman shouting at you on a wall." A pause. "She said she was teaching you."

"Every night. And every night she tells me something about Liesl." He swallowed. "It's her price."

"Is it terrible?"

"Yes," he said. "No. I don't know." He looked down at her hand on his chest. "Liesl saw the sea when she was fourteen. On the herring boats out of Calder Bay. She came home and told her mother it was green."

Her eyes filled.

"Green," she said.

"Like the bottom of a bottle."

"Kit," she said. "Kit always—"

"I know." He reached into his coat with his free hand. "He sent you something."

---

It was a drawing, folded small, the way Kit folded everything. She unfolded it in the light from her own arm, kneeling on the carpet in front of the dead fire, with him kneeling beside her.

It was the sea.

He'd watched Kit draw it at the long table at Gannet's, the night before, with his tongue between his teeth, for three hours. A great wide sweep of water to the edge of the paper, with little boats on it and gulls over it and a line of hills along the bottom where the land was. Every wave was drawn separately. He'd colored it with a stub of green chalk he'd begged off Gannet, carefully, inside the lines, every wave.

Green.

And at the bottom, in big careful letters that pressed too hard: *TAM. THE SEA IS GREEN. I CHECKED. (HE SAYS SOMEONE ELSE SAID SO TOO.) KIT.*

She held it in both hands and didn't say anything for a long time.

"He checked," she said at last, unsteadily.

"He asked Orrin. Orrin's from Calder Bay now. Orrin said it depends on the weather, and Kit said that was cheating, and Orrin said all right, green."

She laughed again, and this time she remembered to do it quietly, into his shoulder.

---

She told him about his mother.

He hadn't expected that. He'd come through the walls with a dozen things to say and the Charter and the Renewal and the passages and Durrow, and she sat on the carpet in front of the dead fire with Kit's green sea on her knee and her hand in his and said, "Severin. Your mother's alive."

He didn't move.

"I met her tonight. In the music room at the end of the west wing. She plays the harpsichord. Three notes. Over and over. The beginning of a song your father used to play." Her hand tightened on his. "She doesn't remember the rest of it."

He knew the song. He hadn't thought of it in fourteen years. He thought of it now, all at once, his father at the harpsichord in the music room with the cherubs on the ceiling, and a boy of five sitting under the instrument on the floor, feeling the notes come down through the wood into his back.

"She gave you to your grandmother," said Tamsin, very gently. "After your father. She thought, if you were *hers*, she wouldn't do to you what she did to him. She thought it would keep you alive." A pause. "She's never told you. She didn't know how."

He couldn't say anything. He sat on the carpet in the Violet Rooms in the dark with his hand in hers and his whole chest locked shut, and felt it, and couldn't say anything at all.

"She doesn't drink," said Tamsin. "The years. She's never taken one. She's forty-six and she looks sixty, and she sits in that room playing three notes of your father's song so your grandmother won't see her." Her thumb moved on the back of his hand. "She asked how many you had. I told her eight. She cried."

He bent his head until his forehead was against her shoulder, and stayed there.

She didn't say anything else. She didn't need to. She put her free arm around his head and held it, the way he'd seen her hold Kit, and they sat like that on the floor of the Violet Rooms in front of the dead fire for a long time.

---

Later, much later, she held out her wrist.

"Take some," she said.

He lifted his head.

"Not five. Not like the other night. I'm not moving the clock again; she'll find you." She held his eyes. "Slowly. A little at a time. The way a broker's needle does it, not a working. My mother says the clock only feels it when it's a flood." She turned her wrist over, beside his, the two silver threads gleaming side by side. "A trickle, Severin. All night. Let me."

He looked at her wrist. At his own. At the thread between them that he'd held so hard all winter, and tried so hard never to pull on, not once, not a minute.

*She loves you, you fool, and you're making her sneak.*

*Pride's a fine thing on a duchess. On a Wick girl, it's a cough.*

"Ours," she said quietly.

He put his wrist against hers. Skin to skin, thread to thread.

"Ours," he said.

---

It wasn't like the ballroom. It wasn't like waking up full. It was slow, so slow he could barely feel it at first: a warmth coming into him down the silver thread, a thread of warmth, thin as a hair, steady, like the first light coming into a room before dawn. Hours. A day. He felt each one arrive. He felt it go out of her and felt it come back up into her from below, from the round room under the clock, gentle, unhurried, a tide coming in rather than a wave.

The windows didn't rattle. The house didn't shake. Across the river, the clock didn't move.

At some point he stopped feeling it at all, because she'd put her other hand on his face, and he'd turned his head and kissed her palm, and then her wrist, and then her mouth, and there wasn't room left in him to notice anything else.

What happened after that is theirs.

---

He woke in the dark with her asleep against his chest and the fire long dead and the gold of her count the only light in the room.

He didn't know what woke him. He lay very still. Her breathing, slow and even. The tick of water in a pipe in the wall. The wind moving along the corridor outside like breath.

And footsteps.

Light. Quick. Bare feet on a carpet runner, coming down the corridor toward the door. And in the silver Ledger thread on Tamsin's wrist, lying against his chest, he felt it: the cold heart, awake. Not asleep anymore. Close. Coming closer.

She felt it too. She came awake all at once against him, rigid, the way she'd come awake in the Tithe Hall the night Joss went.

A knock on the door. Light. Two taps.

"Tamsin, dear?" A warm voice, sleepy, musical. "Are you awake? I couldn't sleep. I thought we might have chocolate."

He was off the bed before the second tap. Coat. Boots. In one hand. No time. The panel was ten steps away, by the dead fireplace, still standing open a crack, a black line in the violet silk.

Behind him he heard her say, in a voice thick with sleep that he'd have sworn was real: "Mm? Lady Aurelia? One moment—"

The door handle turned.

He was through the panel. He pulled it to behind him. It didn't close; it caught, a finger's width from the frame, on something, a fold of the carpet. He stood in the dark between the walls with his boots in his hand and his coat in the other and his heart going like a hammer, and through the gap he saw the door of the Violet Rooms swing open, and candlelight come in, and his grandmother.

In a long grey wrapper, her hair in a plait down her back, her feet bare. With a little silver tray in her hands, and on it two cups, steaming.

She stopped just inside the door. She stood there in the candlelight, small and sweet and sleepy, looking at Tamsin sitting up in the enormous bed with the blanket to her chin.

Then she lifted her face, very slightly, and breathed in through her nose.

"How strange," said Lady Aurelia Thane. "Do I smell clock oil?"
