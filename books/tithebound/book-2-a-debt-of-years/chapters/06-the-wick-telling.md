# Chapter Six

## The Wick Telling

::: {.pov}
Severin
:::

"*Tied with her,*" said Hollis.

He was sitting on Gannet's stove-side stool with his grandfather's ruined coat over his knees and the crystal vial from the salon in his hand, turning it in the lamplight. It glowed. Gold, warm, a month of somebody's life corked up in a thing the size of his little finger.

"That's what she said. In front of all of them. *This year it will be tied with her.* And they clapped." He swallowed. "Lady Marchbank clapped with my vial still in her other hand. She'd had two. She asked me afterward if I knew a good dressmaker for a Renewal; she said she'd never been to one and didn't know what one *wore*."

Nobody laughed.

Severin was standing at the long table with his hands flat on Kit's map of the Undercroft. He'd been standing like that since Hollis came down the ladder, and he hadn't moved.

"Nineteen days," said Vadra quietly, at the window.

"Eighteen," said Kit, from under the table, where he was supposed to be asleep. "It's after midnight."

---

That night the thread at his wrist warmed, and her voice came down it, small and far and steady.

*Nineteen days.*

He closed his eyes.

*I know,* he sent back.

An hour. He felt it go. It was the cheapest hour he'd ever spent.

---

Lotte didn't let him stop.

He'd thought she might, after the salon. He'd thought, a little, that she might take pity on him. She didn't. He came down to the steam-house every night at the tenth bell, and she was waiting on her basket by the last vat with the watch ticking in her apron pocket, and she gave him a thread to cut.

Not practice thread anymore. Real ones. Lotte had been cutting black threads off Wick arms for twenty years, quietly, whenever she felt like it, and the Sump knew, and now the Sump came. One at a time, after dark, in through the river door at the back of the shed. A girl of fifteen whose father had sold her years to a broker in Saltmarket before she was old enough to say no. A grandfather with a broker's twist on his wrist from a debt forty years old, who'd paid it off three times and was still paying. A woman with two threads on her, one on each wrist, sold twice by the same man.

He cut them. All of them. He felt for the slack between the two hearts, the one under his fingers and the far one up the line in some broker's bed, and when it came he cut, and the black thread parted like wet paper, and they looked down at their arms and then up at him, and some of them cried and some of them didn't, and none of them knew who he was.

He didn't make another mistake after the eleventh. Lotte noticed. She didn't say anything about it.

Every night, at the end, she paid him.

*Liesl at seven, stealing a whole mutton pie off the cooling rack at the Candleworks canteen and sharing it with a dog under the Hours Bridge, one bite for her and one for him, till it was gone.*

*Liesl at nine, in the Sump fever, burning up for three days in the bed by the window, and on the fourth morning sitting up and asking for an onion. Just an onion. She ate it like an apple.*

*Liesl at ten, learning cards from a sailor on the Gilt landing, and coming home and taking every copper in the house off her mother in a single afternoon, and giving it all back except one, which she kept, she said, as a fee.*

He listened. Every night. He sat on the wet brick floor of the steam-house and listened, and didn't say anything, and when Lotte had finished, he'd say *thank you*, and she'd say *don't*, and he'd go home.

He knew Liesl better every night. He hadn't known there was so much of her to know.

---

On the sixth night after the salon, Lotte told him about the clock.

She didn't announce it. She was sitting on her basket with a heap of linen in her lap, mending a torn sheet with big stitches, while the last of the night's Wick folk went out through the river door with their wrists bare and their arms held up to the lamp. When the door had closed behind them, she said, without looking up from her needle:

"You've read the Founders, I suppose. At the Meridian. The proper history."

"Yes."

"And what did it tell you?"

"That the wells were a plague. That their counts refilled, and the city went mad fighting over them, and the Founders drowned them all in one night to save Lanthorn." He'd heard Penhallow give the same speech in the Library of Echoes, months ago, to Tamsin. "And stopped the clock at the hour it was done. *Never again the well.*"

"Mm." Lotte bit off a thread. "That's the Gilt telling."

"Is there another?"

"There's the Wick telling." She held the sheet up to the lamp and looked at her stitches. "Laundresses have been telling it over the vats for three hundred years. My grandmother told it to me. I told it to Liesl." A pause. "Sit down, Thane. Not there. On the basket. You've earned a basket."

He sat on the basket.

"The clock," said Lotte Varga, "was made by a well."

He didn't move.

"A Marrow woman, the story says. The first one. Or the first we know of. Back before the Seven were anything but seven greedy families with a bit of land on the river. She had more time than she knew what to do with, the way wells do, and she looked around at the Wick, at the children dying at five and ten and twelve, at the women going grey at thirty, and she thought: *that's wrong*. So she built a clock." Lotte threaded her needle again. "Not to tell the time. To *give* it. A great engine that would draw on her well, gently, a little at a time, and give it out to every child born in Lanthorn. A share for everyone. That's what the Allotment was, Thane. That's what your count is, the one you were born with. Not God's gift. Hers."

The steam-house dripped. Somewhere outside, on the river, a barge horn sounded, long and low.

"And it worked," said Lotte. "For a while. Children got their share. The Wick got its share. And the clock went round and round, and every night at midnight, everything settled. Like accounts at the end of a day. What you'd borrowed, you paid back. What you'd sold went home. What was taken was returned. And the next day started fair." She looked up at him. "That's what midnight was for, on that clock. Settling. Every hour going home where it came from."

"And the Seven," said Severin slowly.

"And the Seven looked at that clock," said Lotte, "and saw a well that never ran dry. And they thought: why share it? Why let it settle? If we could stop the clock just *before* midnight, just before the settling, then everything we took would stay took. Every year we bought would stay bought. Every year we stole would stay stole. Nothing would ever go home again." She tied off her thread. "So they did. They took her, and they chained her under it, and they stopped the hand at eleven. One hour short. And they called it the Drowning, and they carved *never again the well* over their door so nobody would ever go looking for one again."

She held the mended sheet up to the lamp. Then she folded it, very neatly, and laid it on the pile.

"You'll have heard what they say in the Wick about the clock," she said. "Every child in the Wick knows it."

He knew it. Tamsin had said it to him once, on the Hours Bridge, in the fog, with her collar up.

"*The rich stopped the clock,*" said Severin, "*so they'd never have to go home.*"

"It's not a joke, Thane," said Lotte Varga. "It never was. It's a *recipe*."

---

He sat on the basket for a long time without speaking.

"How," he said at last. "How did they stop it? What's holding it?"

"There's a cord, the story says. A Stay. Made of the well's own thread, up from her to the works. Tied with a knot nobody but the Keeper knows." Lotte shook her head. "That's where the Wick telling stops. Laundresses don't know knots. The Gilt never tells us anything it doesn't have to."

"Then who knows?"

"The Founders," said Lotte. "They wrote it all down. They always do; that's the Gilt for you. They can't steal so much as a teaspoon without making a note of it in a ledger." She picked up the next sheet from the pile. "There's a charter. The Founders' Charter. My grandmother said there was. Everything they agreed, everything they did, signed by all seven. How they took her. How they tied her. How it's held." She snapped the sheet out straight. "It'll be in the Ledger House. Where else?"

The Ledger House. The great grey building on the third terrace of the Gilt where the Council of Houses sat, and the Ledger itself was kept, and every year bought and sold in Lanthorn was written down in a hand that never stopped. He'd been inside it a hundred times as a boy, trailing behind his grandmother through its halls. He'd stood in front of the great doors at the end of its central hall, under the three-foot gold letters carved into the stone above them.

*Never again the well.*

"There's a vault," he said slowly. "Under the Council chamber. I've seen the door. Nobody goes in but the Keeper. Once a year, on Council day, she opens it, and the Houses—"

He stopped.

Because the river door at the back of the shed had just opened, and Bry Kettle had come through it fast, and shut it behind her, and put her back against it.

"Greycoats," she said. "On the river path. Twenty of them. Coming this way."

---

He'd stopped arguing with Bry about coming with him on the fourth night. She'd simply followed him down the courts of the Sump in the fog and sat on the step of the shed with her taped fists between her knees, and when he'd told her to go home, she'd said, "You've got four years and a face everyone in Lanthorn knows. Somebody's got to watch the door," and he hadn't been able to think of an answer.

She'd been watching the door.

"Who told them?" said Lotte. She was on her feet. She didn't sound frightened. She sounded the way she sounded when somebody spilled lye.

"Pruett," said Bry. "I'd put money on it. You cut his porter loose last week. Abel. Nine years Pruett had a claim on, and now he hasn't." Her jaw was tight. "There's a big red one at the front. Sergeant. I've seen him before."

So had Severin. In the Tithe Court, on the morning they found Joss, behind Captain Marr, saying *sold out* with a grim satisfaction. In the Reckoning's crowd. In Kit's careful voice, telling the story of a knock on a door on Tallow Lane. *Red-faced. Big. He smelled of beer and talked too loud.*

"Durrow," said Lotte. "Kell Durrow. He's been taking thread-cutters off the river flats for ten years." She looked at Severin. "Out the front. Now. Both of you."

"There are people still here," said Bry.

There were. Not many. Three women who'd stayed late at the vats with work that wouldn't wait, and a girl of fifteen, the one whose father had sold her years, who'd come back tonight to bring Lotte a loaf of bread to say thank you, and was standing by the mangles with it held in both arms, frozen, staring at the river door.

The front doors of the shed burst open.

---

They came in like a flood. Grey coats and silver buttons and lanterns held high, pouring through the big front doors into the dark shed, too many of them to count. Some of them had truncheons. Some of them had broker's needles, long and black, and coils of black thread over their shoulders. *Taking thread-cutters off the river flats.* Severin understood all at once what that meant. You didn't arrest a thread-cutter. You threaded one. To a broker, or to the Ledger, and drew on them until they stopped.

At the front of them was a big man with a red face.

"Varga," said Sergeant Kell Durrow, cheerfully, over the heads of his men. "Lotte Varga. There you are. Been wanting a word with you for years, love." His eyes moved past her. And stopped. And went wide, and then narrow, and then very pleased.

"Well, now," said Durrow. "*Well*, now. Look what we've got here."

He'd seen Severin. Severin saw him see.

"Get them," said Durrow. "All of them. Her first. And him." He grinned. "Keeper'll give me a medal."

They came down the aisle between the cold vats in a grey wave.

He didn't think about it. That was the thing he'd tell Tamsin later, when she asked him, furious, down the thread at three in the morning, *why*. He didn't think. He didn't do the sum. He didn't count what he had left. He saw the girl by the mangles with the loaf of bread in her arms, and Lotte with her chin up and her raw red fists at her sides, and the three women by the vats, and Bry already moving, already going for the first grey coat with her taped fists up, and he reached down into himself, past the thread, past Tamsin, past everything, down to the place where his own life sat in a dwindling row of gold at his wrist, and pulled.

And stilled the room.

The air rang. Like a hundred glasses struck at once. Gold light rolled out from him across the steam-house, over the wet brick and the cold vats and the sleeping mangles, through the grey wave of Greycoats, and they stopped. All of them. Mid-stride, mid-shout, truncheons raised and lanterns swinging and frozen in the swing, black needles held out like knives. Durrow at the front with his red face split in a grin and one finger pointing, a man made of glass.

Bry stopped too, with her fist an inch from a Greycoat's jaw.

"*Out,*" said Severin. His voice came through his teeth. "The river door. *Go.*"

Lotte was already moving. She'd been standing apart from the grey wave, by the vats, and he'd held her clear of the working, her and the girl and the three women, the way a caster can hold clear whatever he can see standing apart. Bry, in the thick of the coats, he couldn't. She grabbed the girl with the bread by the arm and shoved her toward the back of the shed. The three women by the vats ran. Lotte went last. She stopped beside him, for one breath, and looked at his face.

He didn't know what was in it. He could feel what was going out of him. A stilling over a shed this size, over this many people, held this long. Every second past the first costs more than the last. He'd taught Tamsin that at a velvet table, a lifetime ago, with chalk.

A day. A week. A month.

"Go," he said.

She went.

He held it. He held it until he heard the river door slam at the back of the shed, and then a breath longer, for the river path, for the fog, for the alleys of the Sump. He held it until the gold in front of his eyes went dark at the edges and his legs started to shake. Then he walked, holding it, the length of the frozen shed, past Durrow's red grinning face, to where Bry stood with her fist an inch from a Greycoat's jaw, and he took her by the arm and let the stilling go from her and only her.

She finished the punch.

The Greycoat, still frozen, didn't fall. He just stood there with his head snapped sideways at an angle no neck should have, held up by the working, like a broken doll.

"*Burn* me," said Bry, staring at him.

"Out," said Severin.

They went out through the river door into the fog, and he let the stilling go behind them, and heard the shed come crashing back into motion through the brick, two dozen men shouting at once, and Durrow's voice over all of them, roaring.

---

He made it as far as the river wall at the bottom of the next court before his legs went.

He sat down on the wet cobbles with his back against the stones and the fog all around him and the river slapping somewhere below. Bry crouched beside him. Lotte was there too; she'd waited, at the corner, with the girl with the bread. The girl had gone. Lotte hadn't.

She knelt in front of him on the cobbles, in the dark, and took his left arm in her big raw hands, and pushed the sleeve of his Wick coat up to the elbow, and turned it to the faint grey light that came off the river.

All that grey. And at the wrist, by the silver thread, a row of gold.

Three lines. Two notches. A little less.

Lotte Varga looked at it for a long time. Her face, in the fog, was a face he'd never seen on her. Not angry. Something worse. Something that looked, if he hadn't known better, almost like grief.

"Three years," she said quietly. "For a room full of washerwomen."

He didn't answer.

She was silent for a long moment. Then she let go of his arm, and pulled his sleeve back down over it, carefully, the way you'd pull a sheet up over somebody sleeping.

"You stupid boy," said Lotte Varga.
