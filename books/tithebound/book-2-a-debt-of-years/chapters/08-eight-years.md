# Chapter Eight

## Eight Years

::: {.pov}
Severin
:::

He knew before he opened his eyes.

He'd gone to sleep on the cot in Gannet's cellar emptied out, scraped hollow, with his legs still shaking from the stilling and Lotte's *you stupid boy* still in his ears and the faint, constant, low ache that had lived under his breastbone since the Dial grown a little worse. He'd gone to sleep counting. Three lines. Two notches. A little less.

He woke up full.

That was the only word for it. Not well; not rested. *Full*, the way a cup is full, the way the cistern under the Tithe Hall was full, heavy and brimming and almost too much to hold. He lay on his back in the dark cellar with his eyes still closed and felt it, all through him, in his chest and his arms and the backs of his eyes, warm. As if somebody had poured something into him while he slept.

He opened his eyes and held up his left arm in the grey light from the grate.

Eight lines. Two notches.

He looked at it for a long time.

He knew. Of course he knew. There was only one person in Lanthorn who could pour five years into a sleeping man from the other side of a city without losing a minute, and only one who'd want to, and only one who'd be stubborn enough to do it at the exact moment she knew he couldn't argue.

He sat up on the cot with his arm held out in front of him and laughed. Out loud, in the dark cellar, helplessly, the way he hadn't laughed since he was a boy on the back stairs at Thane House with a Wick girl teaching him cards.

At the top of the ladder, the trapdoor opened, and Kit's head appeared, upside down, in a square of grey light.

"The clock moved," said Kit.

Severin stopped laughing.

---

The Wick was in the street.

He'd never seen anything like it. Broker's Row at dawn should have been empty, a few early carts, a few shutters going up, Gannet's cat on the step. Instead it was full. People in shawls and nightshirts and coats thrown over nothing, standing in the middle of the cobbles in the cold with their heads tipped back, looking north across the roofs toward the river. More of them on the roofs. Children on their fathers' shoulders. An old woman in a doorway with a candle still burning in her hand, as though she'd come straight out of bed with it and forgotten.

All of them looking at the clock.

You could see it from the bottom of the Row, over the warehouses, the great pale face hanging above the island in the dawn fog. The single iron hand. And it wasn't at one minute past anymore.

Two minutes past eleven.

"It ticked," Kit was saying, at his elbow, in his too-big coat. "In the night. Everyone heard it. It was like the whole sky went *tock*. Mrs. Achterberg's windows cracked. All the dogs started barking at once." He was holding the tin watch in one fist, the way he did when he was frightened and didn't want anyone to know. "And everyone came out. And they've been out ever since."

At the corner by the bridge, a man was standing on an upturned crate in a coat with no buttons, shouting. A preacher, or a madman, or both. Severin had seen him before on the Row, selling tracts nobody read. Nobody was ignoring him now.

"*It's coming home!*" the man was shouting, his arms flung up toward the clock. "*Three hundred years, and it's coming home! Didn't they tell you? Didn't your grandmothers tell you? The rich stopped the clock so they'd never have to go home!*" His voice cracked. "*Well, it's going! It's going now! Midnight's coming, and every hour goes home!*"

Some of the crowd laughed. Most didn't.

Severin stood in the doorway of Gannet's shop, in the cold, with eight years on his arm, and looked at the hand. Two minutes past.

*It's the blood,* he thought. He didn't know where the thought came from; it arrived whole. *She pulled five years up out of her mother and through herself and into me, all at once, and the clock felt it, because the clock was made by one of them.*

*She moved the clock. To give me five years.*

He put his hand over his wrist.

---

He sent her one word.

He'd thought about it on the way back down the ladder, and while Gannet made the tar she called tea, and while Kit sat at the long table drawing the clock face with the hand at two minutes past and very carefully writing *TICK* under it in letters that pressed too hard. He'd thought about all the words he could send. *Thank you.* *You shouldn't have.* *The clock moved.* *Are you safe.* *What did she do.* *I love you.*

He was going to be careful. He'd decided that. Eight years was more than three; it was still not much. He sent one.

*Don't.*

An hour. He felt it go.

The answer came almost at once. Not one word. Not ten. It came down the silver thread in a rush, breath after breath of time, word after word, as though she'd been sitting with it ready, waiting for him to say exactly that.

*You don't get to decide that. You spent a year on a shed full of washerwomen last night without asking anybody, and I felt every minute of it go, and I spent five on you without asking anybody, so we're even. It moved the clock, which I didn't mean to do, and your grandmother came into my room at dawn in her nightgown with no violets and I lied to her face and she knew I was lying and she said if I do it again she'll find you and drink you. So I won't do it again unless you do something stupid again. So don't do anything stupid again. Bry says you were being shouted at on a wall by an old woman. Good. Tell Kit I said wind the watch.*

He was sitting on the bottom rung of the cellar ladder when it arrived, with his tea in his hand. He put the tea down very carefully on the floor.

Then he put his head in his hands and laughed until he couldn't breathe, until his ribs hurt, until Gannet put her head through the trapdoor and asked if he'd finally gone mad, and Kit came and stood at the bottom of the ladder and looked at him with deep concern.

"What did she say?"

He couldn't answer. He held up one hand. Wait.

"Is it bad?"

He shook his head. He wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand.

"She says," he managed at last, "wind the watch."

Kit looked at the tin watch in his fist. Then back at him.

"I always wind the watch," said Kit, offended.

---

"She loves you, you fool," said Lotte Varga, that night, "and you're making her sneak."

She was sitting on her basket by the last vat with the mending in her lap. He'd come down to the steam-house at the tenth bell the way he always did, as if nothing had happened, and she'd looked up at him, and her eyes had gone straight to his left sleeve, and she'd put down her needle and held out her hand. He'd given her his arm. She'd pushed up the sleeve and turned it to the lamp and looked at it for a long time.

Eight lines. Two notches.

She'd let out a breath through her nose, very slowly, as though she'd been holding it since the river wall. Then she'd let go of his arm and picked her needle back up and not said anything at all for a long while.

"I'm not making her do anything," he said.

"You're making her pour years into you in your sleep, because you're too proud to take them awake." She jabbed the needle through the sheet. "Same thing."

"It's not pride."

"No? What is it, then?"

He didn't answer.

"My girl," said Lotte, "was proud. Did I tell you that? Proud as a duchess. Wouldn't take a copper off anybody she hadn't won it from fair at cards." She pulled the thread through. "Wouldn't take my coat, the winter she went up to the big house, because it was mine and I'd be cold. Went up the Gilt Road in her own thin shawl in the snow. Came down with a cough that lasted till spring." Her jaw set. "Pride's a fine thing on a duchess. On a Wick girl, it's a cough."

The watch ticked in her apron pocket.

"Tonight's," she said. "She was fourteen, the first time she saw the sea. Her uncle took her out of Calder Bay on the herring boats for a week in summer. My brother, Tam. Dead now. She came home brown as a nut with her hair full of salt, and sat at my table, and told me about it for three hours without stopping." Lotte's needle paused. "She said it was green. Not blue, like in pictures. *Green*, Mam. Like the bottom of a bottle. Like a cat's eye."

He didn't move.

There was a boy in a cellar on Broker's Row who drew maps of places he'd never been and had decided, on no evidence at all, that the sea was green.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't," said Lotte.

---

The knock came at Gannet's back door at the eleventh bell the next night.

Not the front. Nobody came to the front anymore. Durrow's men had been up and down the Row twice since the steam-house, with lanterns and dogs, and Gannet had bolted the front and hung a sign in the window saying CLOSED FOR ILLNESS, which made the whole Row laugh, because Gannet hadn't been ill in forty years. The back door opened onto an alley so narrow you had to go sideways, and only people who'd been told about it ever used it.

Hob Marsh opened it with a cudgel in his other hand.

"Easy," said a voice out of the dark. "Easy, mate. I'm expected. Well. I'm not. But I ought to be."

A young man stepped sideways out of the alley into the lamplight of Gannet's back room. Twenty, maybe. Sunburnt to the color of a brick, with salt-white eyebrows and hair cropped short, in a heavy blue sailor's coat stiff with brine. Over one shoulder he carried a coil of rope as thick as his own arm, tarred, and he had the rolling, careful walk of somebody who'd spent the last year on boats.

He looked around the room. At Gannet in her shawls. At Vadra by the window. At Bry, who'd come up off her stool with her taped fists rising. At Kit, at the long table, staring. And at Severin, standing by the stove with a cup of tar in his hand.

His sunburnt face split in a grin.

"Heard in Calder Bay," said Orrin Fisk, "the Thane boy's in trouble."

Severin set down the cup.

"I owe you four years," said Orrin. He let the coil of rope drop off his shoulder onto Gannet's floor with a thump. "I'm here to pay."

---

He crossed the room in three strides and threw both arms around him.

Severin didn't know what to do. He stood there by the stove with his arms out at his sides and a brick-red fisherman hugging him so hard his ribs creaked, and he didn't know where to put his hands. Nobody had held him like that since he was seven. Since his father. He stood there stiff as a post while Orrin pounded him on the back, twice, hard, the way you'd knock a fish off a hook.

Then, slowly, awkwardly, he put one hand on Orrin's shoulder.

"You're supposed to be dead," he said.

"Am I?" Orrin pulled back and looked at him with his salt-white eyebrows raised. "I'm a terrible disappointment, then. Marta says so daily." He grinned again. "Wren's got a tooth. She bit me with it. I've brought you a herring. Mother G., don't let me forget the herring; it's in my coat." He looked down at Severin's left arm, at the sleeve, and his grin went away. "Heard about the Dial. All of it. Heard about the old woman taking three off you, and the girl." He paused. "How many?"

Severin pushed up his sleeve.

Orrin looked at it. Eight lines. He let out a breath.

"Better than I'd heard," he said. "In Calder Bay they had you at two."

"Someone gave me five," said Severin. "Three nights ago."

Orrin looked at his face. Whatever was in it, he read it, the way people who've been on boats read weather.

"Ah," said Orrin Fisk, very gently. "*Her.*"

"Her."

"Then I'd better help you get her back, hadn't I?" He bent and picked up the coil of rope from the floor and slung it over his shoulder again as though it weighed nothing. "Marta says you're to bring your own blanket. When you come. She's not running a charity." He looked round at the room. "So. What are we stealing?"

---

They planned it at the long table that night, all of them, in the lamplight, around Kit's maps.

The Founders' Charter. In the vault under the Ledger House, behind the great doors with *never again the well* carved over them in gold. Opened once a month, on Council day, by the Keeper herself, so that the delegates of the Seven Houses could witness the Ledger's seals. The next Council day was in three days. And this month, with the Renewal coming and the clock slipping, Hollis said, every House would send its delegate, and every delegate would bring a train of servants, and the Ledger House would hire extra hands for the day to carry trays and fetch chairs and mop up after the Long-Lived, who were apparently spilling things now, their hands had started to shake.

"Penhallow has a seat," said Hollis. He'd come down on the coal barge again, in a different ruined coat. "We always have. We just never use it. Nobody ever listens to a Penhallow, so we stopped going." He swallowed. "I'll go. Mother'll faint. It'll be the third time this month; she's starting to enjoy it."

"Wat's been in the Ledger House archive twice," said Bry. "Copying. He says it's all underground, under the Council chamber. Stacks and stacks of the old books, and the vault at the bottom, under the stairs." She cracked her knuckles. "And they hire Wick for Council day. Carrying. Mopping. Pell and me could carry a tray."

"I can't carry a tray," said Pell, from the stove, unhappily. "I drop them."

"You'll carry this one."

"There's a light-well," said Orrin, who had been looking at Kit's sketch of the Ledger House with his head on one side. "Up the middle. Look, here, the boy's drawn it. Goes straight down from the roof to the bottom of the stairs. If you needed to get something out fast that couldn't go out the door—" He patted the coil of rope on his shoulder.

"And me," said Severin.

They all looked at him.

"Everyone in the Ledger House knows my face," he said. "I can't carry a tray. I can't sit in a chair." He put his finger on Kit's map, on the long dark lines Kit had drawn under the Gilt from Severin's own description, from a boy's memories of following his grandmother down stairs nobody else used. "But the Founders built tunnels under the whole Gilt before they built the houses on top of them. The Thane passage under the river is one of them. There are others. One of them comes up under the Ledger House." He looked up. "I've been down it. When I was nine. I got lost and my father found me."

Vadra had been watching from the window all evening, silent. Now she came to the table and looked down at the map, at his finger, at the long dark line under the Gilt.

"Three days," she said.

"Three days."

"And the Renewal in eleven." She looked at him. "You'll want to read that Charter before then, Thane. Every word of it. Whatever's in it is what she means to do to the girl."

"Yes."

"Then we'd better not drop any trays," said Vadra, and for the first time since the Reckoning, the corner of her long severe mouth moved.

---

He stayed at the table after the others had gone up or down to their beds. Kit had fallen asleep with his head on the map of the Ledger House, with his pencil still in his hand and the tin watch ticking by his ear. Severin sat beside him in the lamplight and didn't move him.

Three days to the Charter. Eleven to the Turning.

And on the far side of the river, at the top of the Gilt, in a black house he'd grown up in, a girl in a room full of painted violets with four threads on her wrist, drinking from her mother and being drunk by his grandmother every morning at the eighth bell.

Eleven days.

He looked at Kit's map. Not the Ledger House. The other one, underneath it, the one Kit had started yesterday when Severin had started talking about tunnels and couldn't stop. The Gilt. The terraces, stacked like a wedding cake. And at the very top, on the highest terrace, a black house, drawn in careful pencil, with every wing and every window counted.

And inside it, faint, in lines so light you'd only see them if you knew to look, the places where Kit had drawn, from Severin's description, the walls that weren't walls.

His father had shown him. Every one. When he was six, before the long gallery, before anything. *Every Thane needs a way out of his own house, Sev. Don't tell your great-grandmother.*

He'd never told her. She'd never asked. She'd never thought to; she'd never in her life needed a way out of anywhere.

Kit stirred in his sleep and mumbled something. Severin put a hand on his back, lightly, until he settled.

"There are passages in Thane House," he said aloud, quietly, to the sleeping boy and the lamp and the map, "that my grandmother has forgotten I know."
