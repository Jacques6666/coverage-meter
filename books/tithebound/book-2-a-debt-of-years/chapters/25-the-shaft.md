# Chapter Twenty-Five

## The Shaft

::: {.pov}
Severin
:::

"Two tugs, stop," said Orrin. "Three tugs, more. One long pull, I haul you up and we pretend none of this happened and go to Calder Bay and eat herring."

"One long pull," said Severin.

"You won't do it," said Orrin. "But it's there."

It was an hour before dawn. They'd been awake all night. The lamp on the velvet table had burned down to a stub and Orrin had lit another from it, and in its light the room full of clocks looked the way it always had, the way it had the first night a Wick girl in scratchy grey wool stood just inside the door with her arms folded and looked at the door for three seconds to see if it opened from the inside. Shelves of clocks from floor to ceiling. Clocks on the mantel and the windowsills and the floor. Pocket watches laid open on squares of cloth like shellfish.

All of them going. All of them ticking. None of them agreeing. Nobody had wound a single one in a month.

In the middle of the floor, where the iron grate had been, the black square of the shaft.

Orrin had rigged it in the night. He'd climbed up into the dead works of the Horologe through the grate in the ceiling, up among the great toothed wheels with the dust falling off them in long grey streamers, and slung a block from the iron beam that ran across the top of the room, a heavy wooden pulley the size of a man's head, and run the rope through it. Then down, through the floor, into the dark. On the end of the rope, a bosun's chair: a plank of oak with a sling of rope round it, the kind Orrin said they used to send a man up a mast in a gale to cut a fouled sail free.

"Sit in it like a swing," Orrin had said. "Don't hold the rope with that hand." He'd nodded at the splint. "Hold it with the other. Lightly. Don't grip. I'll do the work. You just sit and don't think about how far down it is."

"How far down is it?"

"Six hundred and forty feet," said Orrin cheerfully. "By the boy's reckoning. He wrote *(guess)* next to it."

---

He sat at the velvet table and waited.

He'd laid it all out on the velvet, the way he laid out the inside of a watch. The little knife, Lotte's kind, plain, short, sharp, the kind you'd cut thread with at a mangle, that she'd pressed into his left hand at the chandlery and closed his fingers round. Kit's watch, cracked across the crystal, ticking. The Founders' Charter in its oilcloth. And Aldric Thane's ring, which wasn't there, because it was on a thread round a girl's neck in a carriage on the Gilt Road, and he'd been told about it in the dark at dawn and hadn't been able to answer.

*She says she's sorry it took so long.*

He picked up the knife and the watch and put them in his coat. He left the Charter where it was, on the velvet, beside the grey book of the Table of Hours, still open at the last lesson. Beside the cup with the brown ring of dried tea at the bottom.

He didn't move the cup.

---

At dawn, the thread warmed.

*Crossing the bridge.*

He got up and went to the window. The one that looked down over the court, six hundred feet below, and the river, and the Hours Bridge with its twenty-four lamps going grey in the dawn.

Three black carriages, crossing. Small as beetles.

He watched them come off the bridge and through the gatehouse and into the court. He watched the crowd in the court part for them, the Long-Lived in their furs, the stewards, the binders. He watched the first carriage stop at the foot of the tower and a small figure in white get down, and walk to the tower door, and not look back.

He watched the second carriage stop, and the door open, and a big man in a grey coat get out, red-faced. And then someone smaller. In white. With a white cloak over it. Holding the hand of a boy in a coat too big for him.

She looked up.

He didn't know if she could see him. Six hundred feet. A face at a high window in a tower, in the grey light. She couldn't. She was looking at the clock. But she looked up, and stood there in the court with her brother's hand in hers and her face turned up toward the tower, toward the clock, toward him, for one long moment. And then she looked at the door, and at the line of Greycoats by it, and at the man at the end of the line in a coat worn shiny at the elbows.

He reached down into the thread and spent an hour.

*Ready.*

---

*Going down,* she said.

He turned from the window. Orrin was already at the rope, standing with his feet braced on either side of the black square in the floor and the rope taken twice round the iron beam behind him and through his brick-red hands. He looked at Severin. He didn't grin, for once.

"Sit, Thane," he said quietly.

He sat.

The bosun's chair swung out over the black square, over nothing, over six hundred and forty feet of dark, and caught, and hung. He held the rope with his left hand. Lightly. Not gripping. His feet hung down into the shaft.

He looked up. The room full of clocks. Orrin's face, braced, red, steady. Above Orrin, through the grate in the ceiling, the great gears of the Horologe, half shaken free of three hundred years of dust, waiting. The escapement, high up in the dark. *Caught.*

"Every Thane needs a way out of his own house," he said. He didn't know why.

"This isn't a way out," said Orrin. "This is a way in." He took the strain. "Steady, Thane."

He let the rope run.

---

The dark took him.

It took him all at once, the way water takes you when you go off a pier. One moment there was lamplight on his face from the room above, and the ticking of a hundred clocks, and Orrin's voice. Then there was the edge of the floor going past his eyes, the black square getting smaller above him, a square, a smaller square, a grey postage stamp with Orrin's head in it, and then nothing. Nothing but black. Nothing but the rope creaking in its block somewhere high above, and the chair swaying very slightly under him, and the cold.

It was so cold. Colder than the cisterns. Colder than the black water at the iron gate. A dead cold, stone cold, three hundred years of cold, breathing up at him out of the shaft below.

He went down.

Slowly. Orrin was good. He'd known Orrin would be good. The rope paid out smooth and steady, an inch at a time, a foot, ten, and the chair hardly swung at all. He couldn't see the walls. He could feel them. Close, on every side. Dressed stone, smooth as glass, cold enough to burn if he touched it. He didn't touch it. He hung in the black in his swing with his splinted hand against his chest and his left hand light on the rope, and went down, and counted.

Because she would have.

---

At about a hundred feet, he felt the rod.

He'd known it was there. Kit's map showed it, a single dark line down the very middle of the shaft between the two lines of the walls: the pendulum's rod, iron, running from the escapement at the top of the tower all the way down through the rock to the great disc at the bottom. He'd seen the top of it every night for three years through the grate. He'd never thought what it would be like to go down beside it.

His shoulder brushed it in the dark. Iron. As thick as a tree. Cold. And under the cold, very faint, a vibration. A hum. As though the whole length of it, six hundred feet of iron, were a string that someone a long way below was very gently touching.

He moved his shoulder away. He went on down.

At about two hundred feet, he felt the Stay.

He didn't mean to. The chair swung, a little, as Orrin shifted his grip far above, and his good hand came off the rope for balance and went out into the black and touched something.

Not iron. Not stone.

*Warm.*

He went still in the chair, in the black, with his hand on it.

It ran down the shaft beside the rod. Close against it, almost touching, the way a vine runs up a tree. Thick as his wrist. Braided; he could feel the strands under his palm, twisted round each other, smooth and fine as silk and hard as wire. Silver, he knew, though he couldn't see it. The well's own thread, from the ring at her throat all the way up to the works, holding the hand.

And it was alive.

He felt it under his hand the way he felt Tamsin's heart through the thread at his wrist. Slow. Deep. A long beat going up it, from far below, from the bottom of the dark, and then a long silence, and then another. A heart. Her mother's heart. Coming up the Stay into his palm, three hundred years of other wells' hearts behind it, and the cord shivering with every beat, the way a rope shivers when something very heavy hangs on the other end.

He could cut it. Right here. He had Lotte's knife in his coat. He could feel for the slack, the way she'd taught him, on two hundred black threads in a cold steam-house, with his left hand. He could cut it here, in the dark, two hundred feet up, alone, and nobody would have to pay for it but him.

*Paid in full by the hand that makes it.*

*I wasn't asking, Thane.*

*It's her choice. Like it was yours.*

He took his hand off the Stay.

He put it back on the rope. Lightly. Not gripping.

He went on down.

---

At three hundred feet, Orrin's voice came down the shaft, very faint, a long way above.

"*Steady!*"

At four hundred, he couldn't hear it anymore. He could hear the rope, creaking. And below him, rising, faint, the singing.

He knew it. He'd heard it outside the silver door, three nights before the Reckoning, with Tamsin's hand flat on the metal and her face white. High and sweet and endless, like a fingertip run round the rim of a glass. He'd thought it came from the door. It didn't. It came from below. From the bottom of the shaft. From the round room. It came up the Stay and the rod and the stone, and the higher it rose, the warmer the air was, until he was sweating in the dark in his coat, and the cold was gone, and there was something else coming up the shaft with the singing.

Light.

Faint. Gold. So faint at first that he thought it was in his eyes, the way light comes into your eyes in a dark room when you've been staring too long. Then less faint. A glow, far below, at the bottom of the black, like the last coal at the bottom of a grate.

And the shape of something hanging in it. Round. Black. Huge. Getting bigger.

The pendulum.

He reached up with his left hand and pulled on the rope. Two tugs.

The rope stopped.

---

He hung in the dark above the pendulum.

It was right below him. A disc of black iron as wide as a cartwheel, hanging at the end of its rod, perfectly still. He'd seen it every night for three years from above, through the grate, a black shape in the dark at the bottom of the works. He'd never seen it like this: from a few feet above, with the light coming up round its edges from below.

Gold light. Pouring up round the rim of the disc like light round the edge of a door.

He leaned. Carefully. The chair swayed. He leaned out past the edge of the disc and looked down.

The round room.

He'd felt it through her thread, twice. He'd never seen it. It was bigger than he'd thought. A great round chamber of black stone carved with clock faces, going down and down, and at the bottom, twenty feet below him, under the pendulum, a pool of light on the floor. Gold. Spreading. Like water.

And in the light, two women, kneeling, side by side, with their arms held out and up in silver.

One with long grey-dark hair, and arms made of light from her fingertips to her shoulders.

One in white. With her hair tied back with a grey ribbon.

Around them, in a ring, at the edge of the light, the Long-Lived. On their knees in their furs, with threads at their wrists, faces turned up. Exactly like the engraving. Exactly.

And kneeling in front of the two women, in white, with a silver knife in one hand and her other hand flat on the grey-haired woman's breast, his grandmother.

At the edge of the ring, a big man in a grey coat, with a boy held against his chest, and a long black needle at the boy's wrist.

At the edge of the ring, in plum velvet, white as paper, Hollis. With his hand on the arm of a tall young man in green.

By the silver door, alone, in grey, with her hands clasped, his mother.

He hung above them all in the dark, twenty feet up, with the light pouring up round the edges of the pendulum onto his face, and nobody looked up. Nobody. They were all looking at the Keeper.

And he heard her.

His grandmother's voice. Coming up off the black stone, soft, sweet, warm, the voice that had said *darling* to him every day of his life.

"Willing, dear?"

He saw Tamsin look past her. At the boy. At the needle.

At the shaft.

Right at him. Right up into the dark over the pendulum. She couldn't see him. He knew she couldn't. He was in the black, above the light. She looked up anyway, for one long moment, the way she'd looked up at the tower from the court an hour ago.

Then she looked back at his grandmother.

"Yes," said Tamsin.

---

He stopped breathing.

He hung in the bosun's chair in the dark with his hand clamped on the rope so hard the tar cut into his palm, and watched his grandmother smile, and turn back to the kneeling woman with her palm on her heart, and bow her head, and close her eyes.

Listening. Waiting for the slack.

He could feel it coming. Through Tamsin's thread. Through the Stay, humming in the dark beside him. A beat of the well's heart, slow, deep, going up the cord past him into the works. And then the silence after it, going out, and out, longer and longer, the slack, the one blink between two beats when nothing moves down any thread in the world.

His grandmother's other hand rising with the silver knife in it.

And the thread at his wrist went hot.

*Now.*

He didn't think. He didn't do the sum. He didn't count what he had, or what it would cost, or how far it was to the floor.

He slid forward off the oak plank, out of the sling, into the light.

And let go of the rope.
