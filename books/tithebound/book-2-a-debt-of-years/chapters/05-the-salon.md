# Chapter Five

## The Salon

"White," said Nessa, holding up the dress. "She said white, miss. Like a fresh cloth."

I looked at it. It was beautiful, the way everything in Thane House was beautiful: white silk so heavy it poured, with a high neck and long fitted sleeves that buttoned at the wrist, a row of tiny pearl buttons from the elbow down. Long sleeves. Of course. In the Gilt, showing your count is vulgar.

"She wants to unbutton it herself," I said. "In front of them."

Nessa didn't say anything. She was sixteen, from Candle Row, two streets over from Tallow Lane, with freckles and bitten nails and twenty-three years on her arm, and she'd been told to dress me and brush my hair and walk behind me through the house, and she'd done all three for twelve days without once looking me in the eye. She was looking at the floor now, holding the dress up between her two hands like a shroud.

"It's all right," I said. "It's not your fault, Nessa."

She flinched as though I'd hit her.

---

I'd spoken to him four times.

Four words from him, in eight nights. *Here.* *Yes.* And then, on the third night, the long one, four whole words that cost him four hours of his life, and I'd lain in the enormous bed with my face in the pillow and cried until Nessa knocked to ask if I was ill.

*Kit winding the watch.*

That was all. That was everything. My brother was alive, and in a cellar on Broker's Row with a man who'd bought him a watch, and winding it every morning, not too tight.

I talked to Severin more than he talked to me. I couldn't help it. A word cost me a minute, and the minute came home before I'd finished the sentence; I could have talked all night. I didn't. Every word I sent, he could feel arriving, and every word he felt arriving, he wanted to answer, and every answer was an hour. I'd felt him pay for *yes*. I'd felt it go out of him, the long drop, down the silver thread toward me. I knew what four years was in hours. I'd done the sum the first night, lying in the dark, the way he'd taught me to do sums at a velvet table. Thirty-six thousand.

So I rationed for both of us. A few words a night. Things that didn't need an answer.

*Safe tonight.*

*She drank at the eighth bell. It came back.*

*Bry's pacing again. I can feel her.*

*Your father had your face.*

That one he answered. One word. An hour.

*Yes.*

---

The salon was held in the Orrery Room, at the heart of the west wing, and I'd never been in it. Nessa had walked me past its doors a dozen times. They were always shut.

They were open now. And I understood why they were usually shut, the moment I walked through them in the white dress with my arms buttoned to the wrist and Nessa a step behind me.

It was round. A tall round room with a domed ceiling, and the dome was painted dark blue, deep night blue, and covered with gold stars in the shapes of the constellations, like the ceiling of the ballroom. But in the middle of the painted sky hung a clock. A huge painted clock face, pale as the Horologe, with gold numerals and a single iron hand. Pointing at eleven.

Painted. It would never move.

Under it, in a circle of gilded chairs, sat the Long-Lived.

I'd seen them in the gallery at the Tithe Market and under the black silk at the Reckoning, but always from far away, in rows, like a choir. Up close they were worse. There were perhaps twenty of them. They looked forty, most of them, or thirty, or fifty, with smooth unlined faces and good teeth and hair that had been dressed by people who did nothing else all day. And they sat the way I remembered, too still, without fidgeting, without shifting their weight, the stillness of people who've had two hundred years to stop moving more than they have to.

I knew some of them. The man with the ear trumpet from the gallery at the Dial, who'd bet a whole year on a tithe once and had the duel declared void when he lost. The woman from the fountain at Thane House, the one who'd held up her glass and said *Wick, eighteen years old, very light, very fresh*.

And at the end of the circle, in a gilded chair that was too big for him, in plum velvet that had been mended so cleverly you could hardly see where, with a tin cuff on his arm and an expression of polite, intense, fixed interest, sat Hollis Penhallow.

He didn't look at me. I didn't look at him. My heart was going so hard I was afraid they'd hear it.

"*There* she is," said Lady Aurelia Thane.

---

She was standing in the middle of the circle, under the painted clock, in black lace sewn with jet beads that caught the lamplight, her count blazing through her sleeves. She held out her hand to me.

I went to her. What else was there to do? I walked across the round room in the white silk with twenty of the oldest people in Lanthorn turning their heads to watch me, the way the court had turned at the Tithe Market, the way the gallery had turned on the Dial. She took my hand and drew me up two steps onto a small round dais in the very center of the room, under the painted clock, and turned me to face them.

"My dears," she said. "May I present Miss Tamsin Fenn. Of Tallow Lane."

Silence. A long, hungry silence.

"Oh, *look* at her," said the woman from the fountain, softly. "She's so *young*."

"Nineteen," said Lady Aurelia, fondly, as if she'd raised me herself. "Seventy-one years and four months. And will be, I'm happy to say, for as long as we need her to be." She turned to me with both dimples. "Your arm, dear."

I held it out.

She unbuttoned the sleeve herself. I'd known she would. One tiny pearl button after another, slowly, from the wrist to the elbow, her small cool fingers working each one free, while twenty of the Long-Lived leaned forward in their gilded chairs. When the last one was undone she folded the white silk back, carefully, the way you'd fold back the cloth on a tray of something good, and turned my bare arm to the lamps.

Gold. Seventy-one rungs of it.

Someone in the circle made a sound. I don't know who. A small sound, almost a moan, like someone hungry who'd just smelled bread.

"There," said Lady Aurelia. "Isn't that lovely?"

---

There was a table beside the dais, I saw now. A little round table with a white cloth, and on the cloth twenty crystal vials in a silver rack, each one no bigger than my finger, each one stoppered with a tiny silver cork. And beside them, a silver needle on a velvet cushion.

A broker's arrangement. Pruett's counter, in silver and crystal.

"You've all felt it," said Lady Aurelia, turning to the circle. "Since the clock moved. Don't pretend you haven't, Corisande. I can see it on your face." The woman from the fountain looked away. "At night, mostly. In the small hours. You wake up, and you look at your arm, and it's—" She made a small fluttering motion with her fingers. "Flickering. Like a candle in a draft."

Nobody answered. But all around the circle, I watched hands move. Toward left arms. Toward jeweled cuffs. Touching them, the way you'd touch a sore tooth with your tongue.

"Show them, Corisande," said Lady Aurelia gently.

The woman from the fountain hesitated. Then she unclasped her cuff, a broad band of gold set with sapphires, and slid it down her wrist, and held out her arm.

Her count was very long. It ran up past her elbow, almost to her shoulder, two hundred years of gold packed so close the lines blurred together. And it was trembling. Not all of it. Patches. Here and there along the length of it, a cluster of lines would dim, and brighten, and dim again, shivering, the way candle flames shiver when somebody opens a door in another room.

"Every night since," whispered Corisande. "Every night. I lie awake watching it. I can't sleep. I've stopped sleeping."

"I know, dear." Lady Aurelia took her hand and patted it. "It's the clock, slipping. That's all. A little slack in the Stay. It's nothing that can't be mended." She smiled around the circle. "And in the meantime, I have something for the nerves."

She took my wrist.

She didn't close her eyes this time. She looked at the circle the whole time she did it, smiling, like a hostess pouring wine. She picked up the silver needle from the cushion with her free hand and touched it to the top of my count, and drew, and the gold came up out of my arm through the needle and down a fine silver thread into the first crystal vial, and the vial filled with light. Gold. Glowing. Like honey with the sun behind it.

A month. I felt it go, the small falling. Not much. Hardly anything, after a year every morning at the eighth bell.

She corked it. She held it up to the lamp.

"Wellwater," said the Keeper of the Ledger. "Straight from the source. Not Wick years from the Row, my dears, that have been through three brokers' hands and a vat. Not the fountain." She smiled. "From the well."

And then she filled another. And another.

Twenty vials. Twenty months. I stood on the little round dais under the painted clock that would never move, in a white dress, with my sleeve folded back like a cloth on a tray, and felt them go out of me one by one, and felt them come back up from below, warm, from across the river and under it and under the tower, from a round black room where a woman knelt in silver with her arms held out.

*I'm sorry,* I thought down the deepest thread. *I'm sorry, Mama. I'm sorry.*

No answer. She was too tired, at this hour. Or too far down. Or listening, and saying nothing, the way she'd said nothing for ten years.

The Long-Lived drank.

---

I've seen people drink on the Row. Gin, in the Sump. Laudanum, in Saltmarket. Years, from a broker's vial, the poorest of the poor buying back a week of their own life at five times what they'd sold it for. I'd seen the faces they made.

The Long-Lived made the same faces.

They pulled the tiny silver corks with their teeth. They tipped the vials back. And then they sat in their gilded chairs with their eyes closed and their heads back and their mouths open, and the color came up into their smooth faces, and on their arms, under their cuffs, I could see the gold steadying, the flicker going out of it, the shivering patches going still and bright.

The man with the ear trumpet was crying. Tears ran down his smooth face into his collar.

"It tastes," he said, in a cracked, wondering voice, to nobody, "it tastes like when I was *young*."

Corisande had both hands over her mouth. Her count, along her bare arm, was perfectly steady. "Oh," she kept saying. "Oh. Oh."

Only two of them didn't drink.

One was Hollis. He held his vial between his finger and thumb and looked at it, and then, with great ceremony, he tucked it into the inside pocket of his mended plum coat.

"For Mother," he said brightly to the room. "She's very delicate. She sends her regards, by the way, Lady Thane. And her smelling salts. She said you'd understand."

Lady Aurelia laughed.

The other was a woman at the far side of the circle. She was tall and very thin, with silver hair cut short as a boy's and a long clever face like a fox's, and a white fur around her shoulders, and she hadn't taken her eyes off me since I came in. Her vial sat untouched on the arm of her chair. She was watching me the way Severin used to watch the inside of a watch through his loupe.

When the others had finished, and were sitting flushed and steady and murmuring in their chairs, she rose, and came to the edge of the dais, and looked up at me.

"Ismay Lisle," she said. Her voice was low and dry. "We haven't met."

"No, my lady."

"A hundred and ninety years," said Lady Ismay Lisle, "and I have never seen anything as wasted as you are in this room." She said it quite pleasantly, very quietly, so that Lady Aurelia, turning to speak to Corisande, didn't hear. "Wellwater in thimbles. For *nerves*." Her fox's eyes moved over my face. "You could be so much more than a cup, child."

Then she turned and walked back to her chair before I could say anything at all.

---

Hollis came up to the dais afterward, while the others were standing, stretching, laughing too loudly, flushed with my mother's time. He had a small plate in his hand with a single pastry on it. A little round thing, golden, glazed, with a twist of lemon peel on top.

"Miss Fenn," he said, with a bow so deep and so absurd that Lady Aurelia, across the room, laughed again. "I don't believe we've been introduced. Hollis Penhallow. I'm terribly sorry about all this." He held out the plate. "You look faint. Have a cake. Penhallows always bring their own, you know; we can never afford the ones at other people's parties."

I took the pastry. Our fingers didn't touch.

"Pell made it," said Hollis, very low, without moving his lips. "Eat the note."

Then he bowed again, and turned, and went to ask Lady Corisande Marchbank whether her sapphires were real, and she was so startled she told him.

I ate the pastry standing on the dais under the painted clock, slowly, in small bites, the way a lady eats. Lemon. Butter. Pell's hands. And in the middle of it, folded small as a fingernail, a twist of thin paper.

I didn't look at it. I didn't need to. I put it on my tongue with the last of the pastry, and felt the words with my teeth before I swallowed them, the pencil ridges pressed too hard into the paper by somebody who'd learned late.

*We're coming. Learn the house.*

---

"My dears," said Lady Aurelia Thane.

The room went quiet. She was standing beside me on the dais again, small and bright in her black lace, with one hand resting lightly on my folded-back sleeve.

"You'll all have heard," she said, "about the little slip. The minute." She smiled, as if it were a dress somebody had worn twice. "And I know some of you are frightened. Don't be. It's the Stay, that's all, the old cord under the clock that the Founders tied. It's grown a little loose, the way old things do. It wants retying."

Murmurs. Nods.

"At the Turning of the Year," said Lady Aurelia, "nineteen days from tonight, I'll go down to the well as I do every year, as every Keeper has done since the Founders, and I'll renew the Stay. The Renewal. Most of you have never seen it. This year, I'd like the Inner Ledger to witness it. I'd like you all to see with your own eyes that the clock is held." She looked around the circle. "And this year the Stay will be stronger than it has ever been. It'll never slip again. Not by a minute. Not by a second."

She turned to me. And smiled. Both dimples. The warmest smile I'd ever seen on her face.

"Because this year," said the Keeper of the Ledger, "it will be tied with her."

All around the round room, under the painted clock that would never move, twenty of the oldest people in Lanthorn began, slowly, to applaud.

*At the Turning of the Year,* my mother had said, in the dark, from under the clock, *she means to bring you down to me.*

*And keep you.*
