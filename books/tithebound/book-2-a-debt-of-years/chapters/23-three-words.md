# Chapter Twenty-Three

## Three Words

::: {.pov}
Tamsin
:::

Nessa brought the ring at the tenth bell, with the warming pan.

She came in the way she always came in at the tenth bell, quietly, past the two of Durrow's men on the door, with the long-handled brass pan full of coals to warm the sheets and her eyes on the floor. She slid the pan between the sheets at the foot of the bed, where Kit was asleep with his knees up, and moved it slowly back and forth, the way you're supposed to. And when she straightened up, she put her other hand on the coverlet beside mine for a moment, and took it away, and there was something small and hard and warm under my fingers.

"Lady Evadne," she whispered, to the floor. "She said to give it to you. For him." A pause. "She said, tell him his father would have wanted him to have it. She said tell him she's sorry it took so long."

She went out with the warming pan. The Greycoats shut the door behind her.

I opened my hand.

A ring. Heavy, old, silver gone dark with handling, a man's ring, too big for any finger of mine. On the flat face of it, worn almost smooth, a single engraved shape: a clock hand. The Thane mark. And inside the band, in tiny letters I had to tilt to the firelight to read:

*A.T. — Every Thane needs a way out.*

I sat on the edge of the enormous bed with it in my palm for a long time.

Then I took a length of thread from Nessa's sewing basket, plain grey thread, nothing magic in it at all, and strung the ring on it, and tied it round my neck, under the white silk nightgown she'd laid out for me, next to Kit's map against my ribs.

---

*Tomorrow,* I sent down the deepest thread.

*I know.* My mother's voice, from under the clock, faint, and very calm. *I can feel them. All evening. Up the Lines, through the walls. The Long-Lived. Their threads are humming like bees. They're frightened, and they're excited, and they can't sleep.* A pause. *And the Stay. It's been shivering since dusk. It knows.*

*Does she know? About us? About tomorrow?*

*She knows something's coming,* said my mother. *She's known since the Charter went. She doesn't know what.* A pause. *She came down tonight. Alone. She stood at the edge of the light and looked at me for an hour and didn't say anything. She's never done that before.*

I sat with my hand over my wrist.

*Mama. If it goes wrong—*

*Then give her the blood,* said my mother. *Willing. With your heart in it. And save your brother.*

I closed my eyes.

*I know,* I said. *I already decided.*

A long silence. I felt her down there in the dark, in the pool of light, with her arms held out in silver and her face turned toward the closed door.

*And if it doesn't go wrong?* she said at last.

*Then we go home.*

Another silence. Longer.

*Home,* said my mother, as though it were a word in a language she'd known as a child and forgotten. *Tallow Lane. Two rooms over Tobias Wren's chandlery. The window that sticks.*

*It still sticks.*

Down in the dark under the clock, I felt her start to laugh, and then stop, and then not stop.

---

Kit was asleep. Properly asleep, the deep heavy sleep of the old physic's tar-and-honey draught, curled on his side under the coverlet with his fists by his face and his breathing slow and almost clear. I lay down beside him on top of the covers in the white nightgown, with Aldric Thane's ring on a thread at my throat, and held my wrist, and reached for the far thread.

*Where are you?*

The answer came at once. Not one word. Not rationed. It came down the silver line in a long slow breath, word after word, and I lay very still and felt every one of them cost him and arrive.

*In my room. In the tower. At the Meridian.* A pause. *The clocks are all still going.*

I didn't understand.

*All of them,* he said. *Every one. Nobody's wound them in a month. Not since the Reckoning. I came in an hour ago with Orrin and a lamp, and I thought they'd all have run down, and they haven't. Every clock in this room is ticking. Hundreds of them. None of them agree.* A pause. *I don't understand it. I've fixed clocks since I was ten. A clock that isn't wound stops. It's the one rule.*

*Since the minute,* I said slowly. *Since the first tick.*

*Yes.* A pause. *Liesl's watch started by itself the same night. And Kit's watch is still going in my pocket and I haven't wound it once, I've been afraid to, it's cracked.* Another pause. *I think everything in Lanthorn that keeps time knows the big one's moving.*

I lay in the dark and thought about that. About every clock in the city, in every room, in every shop on the Row and every tower in the Gilt, ticking on by itself because three hundred feet under an island a woman was kneeling in silver with her heart beating against a cord.

*Severin. You're spending hours.*

*I know.*

*You're not rationing.*

*No,* he said. *I've stopped. You told me to spend some.* A pause, and something in it that was almost a laugh. *You told me in a room full of painted violets with your hand on my chest. I'm doing as I'm told.*

---

He told me about the room.

He was sitting at the velvet table, he said, with the lamp turned down. The tools were still laid out where he'd left them on the morning of the Reckoning, in their rows, every tweezer parallel. The grey book of the Table of Hours was still open at the page we'd stopped at, the last lesson, a whole winter ago. There was a cup on it. Mine. With the tea dried to a brown ring at the bottom.

*I didn't move it,* he said.

The grate in the floor was up. He and Orrin had pried it out an hour ago with an iron bar, and laid it aside, and now there was a square black hole in the middle of the floor of the room full of clocks, and through it, below, the dark. And the shaft. And somewhere down at the bottom of it, six hundred and forty feet by Kit's reckoning, the round room.

And above, through the iron grate in the ceiling, the great gears of the Horologe. Not dead anymore. Not stopped. He said he could see them from his chair. The great toothed wheels, three hundred years under dust, with the dust shaken off them in long grey falls where they'd moved, three times now, a notch at a time. And the escapement, high up in the dark. The fork and the wheel. *Caught,* he said. *Waiting. Like a held breath.*

*Orrin's asleep,* he said. *On the floor. With his head on a coil of rope. He says he's slept in worse places in a gale. He snores like a seal.*

I laughed. Into the pillow, quietly, so I wouldn't wake Kit.

*What will you do,* I said. *After?*

He didn't answer for a long time.

*I don't know,* he said at last. *I've never thought about after. Not once. Not since I was sixteen. There wasn't going to be one.* A pause. *There's a bed in Calder Bay. Orrin says. Marta says bring your own blanket.*

*A window over the sea.*

*A window over the sea.* A pause. *Green.*

*Kit can check.*

*Kit can check,* he agreed, and I felt him smile, three miles away in a room full of clocks over a hole in the floor.

---

Kit woke up near midnight.

He did it the way he always did, all at once, sitting up with his eyes wide. Then he saw me lying beside him in the firelight with my hand over my wrist, and he knew. He'd seen me do it all day. He lay back down slowly and looked at my wrist.

"Is it him?"

"Yes."

He was quiet for a moment.

"Tell him," said Kit. "Tell him to keep winding the watch. Every morning. Even if I'm not there." He swallowed. "Not too tight."

I sent it. Every word.

The answer came back. A long one. I felt every hour of it go.

*Tell him I haven't wound it. Not once. It's cracked, and I've been afraid to touch it. And it hasn't stopped. It's been going in my pocket for two days with nobody winding it, and it hasn't lost a minute.* A pause. *Tell him it's waiting for him. Tell him I'll give it back to him tomorrow, with my own hand. And then he can wind it himself.*

I told him.

Kit lay on his back in the enormous bed with the firelight on his face and listened. When I'd finished, he didn't say anything for a long time.

"Tomorrow," he said.

"Tomorrow."

He reached over and took my hand, the one that wasn't on my wrist, and held it. Then he closed his eyes. I watched his breathing go slow and even, and his fingers go loose in mine, and I didn't let go.

---

*If it goes wrong,* I said, very late, when the fire was almost out. *Tomorrow.*

*It won't.*

*If it does.* I held my wrist. *If I'm kneeling in silver beside her, and you're not there yet, and Durrow's got Kit on the floor in front of me, and she says the words.* I swallowed. *I'll give it to her, Severin. My blood. Willing. I already decided. My mother said to. I'd have done it anyway.*

A long silence. So long I thought he'd gone to sleep.

*Then I'll come down the shaft anyway,* he said at last. *Whatever she's tied. However she's tied it. And I'll cut you out of it.*

*You can't cut a Stay. Lotte said. Only in the slack of the well's heart, and it's paid in—*

*Then I'll cut you out of it,* he said again, *some other way.* A pause. *I'll find one. I'll take it apart like a watch. Every wheel. I'll find the one that's holding you and I'll take it out with tweezers.* A pause. *I've got the tools.*

I lay in the dark and cried a little, quietly, so I wouldn't wake Kit.

*Tamsin,* he said.

*What?*

A pause. And then three words, one after another, slowly, deliberately, each one wrapped in its own hour of his life and sent down the silver thread across the dark city, up the Gilt, through a wall of new brick, into my wrist.

*I love you.*

I felt them go out of him. One. Two. Three. Three hours, gone, the long lonely drop under his breastbone, the way they'd gone out of me on Pruett's counter, the way a year went out of me every morning at breakfast.

And then, before I could say anything, before I could send anything back, one more word. A fourth hour. And I felt him almost laughing as he sent it.

*Cheapest thing I ever bought.*

---

I didn't sleep. I lay beside Kit with Aldric Thane's ring at my throat and Kit's map against my ribs and my hand over my wrist, and I talked to him until the window began to go grey. Not much. Small things. Things that didn't need answering. The painted violets on the ceiling, four hundred and twelve of them, I'd counted. The sound Kit made in his sleep, a little whistle on the out-breath, the same since he was a baby. A song my mother used to sing. I hummed it to him down the thread, a breath of time with no words in it at all, rising and falling like water.

He didn't answer any of it. He didn't need to. I could feel him listening.

At the end, as the window went from grey to pale, I told him one more thing.

*Your mother sent you something,* I said. *Your father's ring. I'm wearing it. I'll give it to you tomorrow.* A pause. *She says she's sorry it took so long.*

Nothing came back for a long time. Then, at last, one word.

*Tomorrow.*

---

They came for me at dawn.

Not Nessa. Not the Greycoats. The door of the Violet Rooms opened without a knock, and Lady Aurelia Thane stood in the doorway in the grey light, and she was in white.

I'd never seen her in white. Not once. She'd been in black lace every day I'd known her, at the Tithe Market and the Reckoning and every breakfast in the Glass Room. Now she was in white silk from her throat to her feet, with long sleeves buttoned at the wrist, and her honey-gold hair up under a diadem of silver clock hands, and her count blazing through the white so that she seemed to glow from inside, like a lamp behind a paper screen.

She looked at me, sitting up in the enormous bed in my white nightgown with my brother asleep beside me and my hand over my wrist.

She smiled. Both dimples. The warmest smile I'd ever seen on her face.

"Good morning, dear," said the Keeper of the Ledger. "Today you go home to your mother."
