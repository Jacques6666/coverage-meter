# Chapter Three

## Thread-Speech

*Don't push the words,* said my mother. *Push the breath under them.*

It was the third night. I was sitting up in the enormous bed in the Violet Rooms with the curtains drawn and the fire out and my left wrist held in my right hand, and I was sweating as though I'd run up the Hours Bridge with a coal sack on my back.

"I *am* pushing the breath," I said through my teeth. Out loud, which you weren't supposed to do. "I'm pushing everything."

*You're shouting. I can feel you shouting. It's like someone banging a pot on the other side of a wall.* A pause, dry and thin and somehow amused, even from under three hundred feet of rock. *You did that when you were small, too. When you wanted something. You'd stand in the middle of the room and shout until the whole lane knew.*

"I did not."

*You did it the morning Kit was born. You stood in the doorway in your nightgown and told the midwife she was doing it wrong.*

I opened my mouth. I closed it again. I had no memory of that at all, and I knew at once that it was true.

*Again,* said my mother. *Quietly. The way you'd breathe on a coal to make it catch.*

---

She'd started teaching me on the second night.

It wasn't like being taught anything I'd ever been taught before. Not like Gannet at her counter, showing me how to feather an ink line on a pig's leg. Not like Severin at his velvet table with the Table of Hours open between us and his finger running down the columns. Those had been things you could see. This was all in the dark, inside, in the place where you know your own count without looking at it.

*It's the same thing as pushing time,* she'd said. *You've done that. Into the boy in the ballroom. Into your friend under the cistern. You reached down into the thread and pushed, and it went.*

"Years went."

*Years went because you pushed years. Push less. Push a breath. A minute. And wrap the word around it, the way you'd wrap a stone in a handkerchief before you threw it. The minute carries the word down the line.*

"And the minute comes back."

*To you, it comes back. To me, it comes back. That's the well.* A pause. *To anyone else who tries it, it doesn't. That's why nobody else does it. That's why the Gilt never found out it could be done. They'd never think to try, and if they did, they'd never pay twice.*

"How much?"

*For you, a minute a word, and it comes home. For anyone on the other end who answers, an hour.*

"An *hour*?"

*A word costs more going uphill.* She sounded tired. *Again, Tamsin.*

---

I tried for two nights to speak back to her, down the mother's tether, the thread I'd never seen. She said it would be the easiest, because it was blood, and because it was the oldest, and because I'd been holding it my whole life without knowing.

It wasn't easy. It was like trying to whistle with someone else's mouth. I'd reach down into the warmth at my wrist and push, and nothing would happen, or something would happen, a jolt, a heat, a sudden flood of time going out of me like water through a burst seam, and I'd feel her flinch at the other end of it, far down in the dark, and I'd stop.

*Too much,* she'd say. *Gently. You're not putting out a fire. You're lighting a candle.*

On the third night, sitting up in the bed with the sweat running down my back, I stopped trying so hard.

I thought about the end of my bed on Tallow Lane. About a crooked finger tracing circles in the air in lamplight. About the smell of tallow on her shawl and the sound of her humming, low, a song with no words. I thought about how I'd sat up all night with Kit squalling in my arms, waiting for a door. About the morning after. About ten years of mornings after.

I reached down into the warmth, very gently, and breathed on it.

*Mama.*

It went.

I felt it go. Not a flood. A thread of warmth, thin as a hair, a single breath, going out of me and down, and down, and down, across the river and under it and under the island and under the tower and into the dark, carrying one word wrapped inside it like a stone in a handkerchief.

And at the other end, I felt her catch it.

She didn't say anything for a long time.

When she did, her voice had changed.

*Say it again.*

*Mama.*

Down in the dark under the clock, in the pool of light, I felt my mother start to cry.

---

After that we talked every night.

Not long. She couldn't talk long. Speaking cost her too, she said, not time, but something else, the way it tires you to hold your arm out straight. She was being drunk all the time down there, by the Keeper through the Stay, by the Long-Lived through the Lines, by the clock itself; it all came out of her, every minute of every day, and there wasn't much left over for words. So we talked in snatches, a few minutes in the darkest hour, and then she'd say *enough now*, and the warmth would sink back down into the deep slow beat under everything, and I'd lie there in the dark with my face wet.

She asked about Kit. Every night, first, before anything else.

So I told her. His lungs, how they filled every winter and how I'd sold three years in Saltmarket to drain them. His maps. The Gilt with its terraces like a wedding cake and the sea he'd decided was green. The Meridian with the heart under the tower, from a story she'd told me that I'd told him. His thirty-one years. The tin watch Severin had given him, which he wound every morning, not too tight.

*Thirty-one,* she said. She was quiet for a long time. *I left him with ten.*

*I know.*

*I was going to come back.*

*I know, Mama.*

She told me things too. Not many. A few, each night, like a woman taking coins one at a time out of a purse she's been hiding.

My grandmother's name had been Wenna. Wenna Marrow. And her mother's had been Ysolt Marrow, and hers had been Hester Marrow, and the line went back like that, mother to daughter, further than anybody had written down, all the way back past the Drowning. Marrow women. Wells, one at a time, each one hiding.

*Only one at a time,* she said. *The well lives in one of us. When she dies, it wakes in her eldest daughter. It woke in me the night my mother died. I was twenty-six. I was asleep, and I woke up in the dark with my hand on fire.* A pause. *In the morning, my little finger was bent. Like it had been broken and set wrong. It's the mark. It comes with the waking. Every Marrow well that ever lived has had a finger like mine.*

I thought of the woman under the pendulum in Vadra's engraving, two hundred and twenty years old. Her hands held out in the silver threads, palms up. The crooked finger.

*Was that one of us? In the picture?*

*Which picture?*

I told her about Ilse Marrow the copyist, hanged a month after she drew it. About the second panel of the Drowning fresco, plastered over in the Year 80.

My mother was quiet for a long time.

*Ilse,* she said at last. *My grandmother used to say we had a cousin who drew. Who went up to the Gilt to work for the Ledger, and never came home.* A pause. *She must have known who she was drawing.*

*Will it come to me?* I asked. *The well? When—*

I couldn't finish it.

*When I die,* said my mother calmly. *Yes. It will. That's the way of it.* A pause. *You'll know if it ever comes to you, Tamsin. Your finger will tell you.*

I lay in the dark with my left hand held up in front of my face, looking at my little finger. Straight. Ordinary. I looked at it until my eyes watered.

---

In the daytime, Lady Aurelia took me to see the dead.

That's how I thought of it, anyway. She called it the long gallery. Nessa had shown it to me already, twice, the way she'd been told to; the light in the afternoon was quite something. But on the fourth day of my lessons, the Keeper took me herself, with her arm through mine like an old friend's, and we walked the length of it together, slowly, while the afternoon sun came slanting in through the tall west windows and lay across the floor in long gold bars.

It was where it had happened. I knew that before we were halfway down. The long gallery at Thane House. A boy of sixteen, the day after his birthday, and a Wick girl with thirty-four years and a watch on a blue ribbon, and a small cool hand laid over his on the thread. I kept looking at the floor, at the dark polished boards in the gold light, and thinking: *here? Or here?*

She didn't seem to notice. She was looking at the walls.

They were covered in portraits. Floor to ceiling, frame touching frame, all down both sides of the long room, hundreds of them. Men and women in the clothes of every age I'd ever seen in the Echoes in the library and some I hadn't: high collars and powdered hair and long trailing sleeves and armor, once, a woman in armor with a sword across her knees.

"My mother," said Lady Aurelia, stopping in front of a tall dark painting near the far end. A woman with a long pale face and pale hair and eyes that looked straight out of the canvas, cold and clever. "Keeper before me. Sixty-one years old when the clock stopped; she was there on the bridge, you know, at the Drowning. She lived another hundred and sixty." She tilted her head. "I don't think I ever liked her. But I miss her."

She walked on, still holding my arm.

"My first husband. Teodor. He used to sing in the bath." A young man with dark curls and a lazy smile. "My second. Rufus. A terrible man, but he could dance." A heavy, handsome face, red-cheeked. "My third, whose name I've genuinely forgotten." She laughed, the light silvery laugh. "Isn't that dreadful? I'll remember at three in the morning. I always do." A row of children, small, solemn, in old-fashioned lace. "Mine. All of them. All gone." She didn't stop in front of those. "My sister Clarice. Clarice's daughters. A Lisle I was in love with for thirty years who never knew. A Marchbank who was in love with me for forty and wouldn't stop telling me."

She stopped in the middle of the long gallery, in a bar of gold light, and looked up at the walls on either side, all those faces, going up and up into the shadows of the ceiling.

"Everyone I have ever loved is on these walls, dear," said Lady Aurelia Thane. "And every one of them, I buried."

I didn't say anything. I didn't know what to say. I'd come into that gallery hating her so much my teeth hurt, and I still did. But she was standing in the light with her small hand on my arm and her face turned up to two hundred years of the dead, and for a moment she looked exactly as old as she was.

"Do you know what the worst thing about a long life is?" she said quietly. "It isn't the dying. You'd think it would be. You'd think, all those funerals." She shook her head. "It's the forgetting. I can't remember Teodor's voice. I've tried. I lie awake and try. I can remember that he sang in the bath, and I can't remember what he sounded like."

Then she blinked, and smiled, and the dimples came out, and she was the Keeper again.

"Come along. There's one more I want you to see."

Near the end of the gallery there was a gap. A space on the wall at eye level, a pale rectangle on the dark wallpaper where a portrait had hung until recently, with the hook still there. And beside it, a painting of a young man.

He looked like Severin. That was the first thing I saw, and it went through me like a knife. The same height, the same dark hair too long, the same grey eyes, the exact grey of tallow ink. But he was older, and softer about the mouth, and he was smiling in a way I'd never once seen Severin smile, easily, as if he didn't know any better.

"Aldric," said Lady Aurelia. "My great-great-grandson. Severin's father." She looked at the painting without any expression at all. "He disappointed me."

"How?"

"Oh, in the usual way." She turned to the pale gap on the wall beside him, the empty rectangle with the hook. "And this was Severin. I had the painter in only last year. It was a very good likeness." She looked at the gap. "I had it taken down on the morning after the Reckoning."

"Where is it?"

"Burned, I should think. I told them to burn it." She patted my arm. "One has to keep the walls tidy, dear. Otherwise one would never be able to find anybody."

I looked at the empty space on the wall. At the hook.

And past it, down at the very end of the gallery, where a narrow door stood half open onto a back corridor, I saw someone.

A woman. Tall, thin, in a grey dress so plain it was almost a shroud, with her hair pulled back and her hands clasped in front of her. She was standing in the doorway, half in shadow, perfectly still, looking at us. At me. Her face was pale and fine-boned and very tired, and there was something about it, something about the eyes, that I almost knew.

Then she saw me see her, and she stepped back into the corridor and was gone.

"Who was—"

"Ignore her," said Lady Aurelia, without turning her head. "She's furniture."

---

That night my mother said, *Now him.*

I knew who she meant. I'd known she would say it, sooner or later. I'd been waiting for it for five nights, and dreading it, and wanting it so badly I could hardly breathe.

*His thread is silver,* she said. *Ledger-made. It's harder than mine. Stiffer. It wasn't made to carry anything but time going one way, and you'll be pushing against the grain.* A pause. *But it's the oldest one on your wrist, after mine. And it's the one you've held hardest. I can feel that from down here. All winter. You and him.*

I lay on my back in the enormous bed in the Violet Rooms with the painted violets on the ceiling and my left wrist held against my chest. Four threads. Cold. Fast. Deep. Far.

I found the far one.

It was easy to find. It was always easy to find. Slow and even, a heartbeat trained into steadiness, across the river, down in the Wick somewhere, in a cellar that smelled of tallow. It had gone fast at three in the morning every night for a week. It was slow now. He was asleep. Or trying to be.

*Gently,* said my mother. *Like breathing on a coal.*

I didn't think about the Dial. I didn't think about his grandmother's hand on his wrist, or four years, or the empty rectangle on the wall of the long gallery. I thought about a room full of clocks that all disagreed. About a velvet table and a slate full of chalk sums and a cup of tea going cold at my elbow. About a frozen balcony and a forehead against mine and our breath in one cloud. *Tamsin,* he'd said, against my mouth, the moment before every candle in Thane House went out.

I reached down into the silver thread, very gently, and breathed.

*Severin.*

It went. Stiffer than my mother's thread, harder, like pushing a breath through a reed. I felt it catch, and drag, and for a moment I thought it would stick and stop and go nowhere. And then it was through, and gone, out of my wrist and across the dark city, down the Gilt, through the Wick, carrying one word.

And across the river, under the fog, in the dark, his heart jumped.
