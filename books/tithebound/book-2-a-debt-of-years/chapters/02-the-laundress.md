# Chapter Two

## The Laundress

::: {.pov}
Severin
:::

At the eighth bell every morning, a year went out of her.

He felt it the way you feel a stair give under your foot. He'd be standing at Gannet's stove with a cup of the black tar she called tea, or bent over Kit's maps on the long table in the back room, or lying on the cot in the cellar with his eyes open, and the thread at his wrist would tighten. Then the falling: a long, lonely drop going out of her, down a different thread, a cold one, into a heart he'd have known in his sleep. One year. Every morning. Like a bell.

And then, a few minutes later, slowly, warm as water seeping up into a footprint, it would come back.

Six mornings running. He'd stopped pretending he didn't count them.

"She's drinking her at breakfast," he said, on the sixth.

Nobody in the back room answered at once. Gannet was in her chair by the stove in her dozen shawls, with her ringed hands folded over her belly and her eyes half shut. Magister Vadra stood at the curtained window, as she always did, as if she expected someone to come up the alley and wanted to see them first. Hob Marsh, who had been a footman at Thane House for twenty years before Severin paid him to sit in a room over a pie shop and watch a window on Tallow Lane, was sitting on an upturned crate by the door with his big hands between his knees. And at the long table, under the lamp, a ten-year-old boy in a coat with the sleeves rolled up four times was drawing a staircase.

"At *breakfast*," said Bry Kettle.

She hadn't sat down since she came in. She never sat down anymore. She paced the length of the back room and back again, eight strides each way, with her split knuckles taped and her black eye gone yellow-green at the edges, and every time she passed the table, she looked at the map as if it had personally insulted her.

"Eighth bell," said Severin. "Every day. A year. Over tea, I imagine. My grandmother likes ceremony."

Bry stopped pacing. "How do you know it's a year?"

"I can feel it go."

"So can I." She held up her left wrist, where a raw red welt circled the bone beside the old binder's scar: the black thread Tam had sewn there herself with a broker's needle in a room under the cistern, to pour twenty years into her. "Every morning. Like somebody pulling a tooth out of *my* head." Her jaw worked. "And then it comes back."

"It always comes back," said Severin quietly.

"I *know* it comes back. That's not the *point*." She started pacing again. "The point is that every single morning that small sweet poisonous old—"

"Bryony," said Gannet, without opening her eyes.

"—*woman*," said Bry, "sits at her table and drinks my friend like a cup of tea, and we sit here in a pawnshop drawing pictures."

At the table, Kit looked up from his staircase with an expression of deep offense.

"They're not *pictures*," he said. "They're *plans*."

---

They were, in fact, the best plans Severin had ever seen.

He'd learned that in the first two days, sitting beside the boy at the long table with the lamp between them, describing everything he could remember. The Meridian. The tower stair, six hundred and twelve steps. The narrow door in the wall of the stairwell and the Thane passage behind it, the spiral down, the brick tunnel under the river, the iron gate at the fork with the chain wrapped three times through its bars. The cisterns, which he'd only seen from the sluice end. The little square room with the iron rings. The deeper passage carved with clock faces. The silver door.

And the round room behind it, which he'd never seen at all. Only felt. Through Tamsin's thread, on the Dial, while his grandmother led her away down a red-carpeted stair.

Kit drew all of it. He drew it the way he'd drawn the Gilt and the Candleworks and the sea he'd decided was green: carefully, in pencil, every step counted, every turn exact, with tiny notes in the margins in his big careful letters that pressed too hard. *Wet here.* *73 steps (Tam).* *Gate — chain — WON'T BREAK.* And he asked questions. Endless questions. How many steps between the second landing and the narrow door? Which way did the tunnel curve, left or right? How wide was the silver door, exactly, in hands?

"I don't know exactly," Severin had said.

"Then guess," said Kit. "A guess is better than nothing. Tam says. She says a counter always guesses and then checks."

So Severin guessed, and Kit wrote *(guess)* next to the number in very small letters, and they went on.

On the fourth evening, Kit had put his pencil down and looked at him across the lamp with his mother's eyes, too big for his face, and said, "When can we get her?"

"Soon."

"That's what people say when they don't know."

Severin had looked at him for a long moment.

"Yes," he said. "It is."

Kit had nodded, as if that were a perfectly satisfactory answer, and picked up his pencil again.

---

"We can't get her," said Magister Vadra, at the window, "until someone can cut that thread."

She turned around. In the lamplight her long severe face was grey with tiredness; she'd been out every night since the Reckoning, going from cellar to cellar through the Wick ahead of the Greycoats, and it showed.

"The Ledger thread on her wrist," she said. "Tied in the Keeper's presence, by a Ledger binder, with a Ledger knot. As long as it's there, it doesn't matter where Tamsin is. The Keeper can drink her from Thane House or from the moon. And if we take the girl and run, Lady Thane doesn't need to chase her. She only needs to go downstairs."

To the round room. To the woman under the pendulum. Severin had thought it himself, every night, lying on the cot in the cellar with his hand over his wrist.

"Then cut it," said Bry.

"A Ledger thread," said Vadra, "is not black thread off a bale in this shop's back room, Miss Kettle. You don't snip it with a knife. Cut it wrong and it kills one end of it. Usually the weaker heart." She looked at Severin. "And we don't know which heart is weaker. The Keeper's is two hundred and forty-seven years old, and the girl's refills from a well. It could go either way." A pause. "Or both."

"You cut threads," said Severin. "The Unwound. That's what you do. That's what you're for."

"Black threads. Broker's twists. We cut a hundred a month off Wick arms that some broker's got a claim on." Vadra shook her head. "A Ledger thread is different. I've seen it tried twice in thirty years. Both times, both ends died."

Silence. The stove ticked.

"There's one," said Mother Gannet.

Everyone turned to her. She hadn't opened her eyes. She was sitting exactly as she'd been sitting, with her rings glinting on her folded hands.

"One woman in Lanthorn," said Gannet, "who's cut a Ledger thread and left both ends breathing. Did it twice, if the talk's true. Once for a girl in Saltmarket that the Ledger had sold to a Lisle, and once for her own sister." Her small black eyes opened, and fixed on Severin. "She's not Unwound. Not properly. She won't come to meetings and she won't take orders. But she cuts threads when she feels like it, and nobody in the Sump has ever seen her get one wrong."

Vadra had gone very still at the window.

"Gannet," she said.

"He asked," said Gannet. "He wants to know who. He ought to know who." She kept looking at Severin. "She works the steam-houses at the bottom of the Sump. Varga's the name. Lotte Varga."

Severin didn't move.

He couldn't. He was sitting at the long table with his hand flat on Kit's map of the Undercroft, and the name went into him like a needle under the skin, cold and then burning. *Varga.* He hadn't heard it said aloud in five years. He'd thought it every day.

*Liesl Varga. Her mother was a laundress at Thane House.*

"She left," he said. His voice sounded strange to him. "After. She left the house. Nobody would tell me where."

"She went down to the Sump," said Gannet, "and she's been washing other people's sheets in it ever since." She closed her eyes again. "And cutting their threads."

---

The steam-houses at the bottom of the Sump stand on the river flats where the spring floods come in, a row of long low brick sheds with tin roofs and no windows, and you can find them in any fog by the smell. Lye. Wet wool. Boiling linen. Coal smoke from the furnaces under the vats. And over all of it, coming out of every door in a white rolling cloud, steam.

He went alone. Bry had wanted to come; he'd said no, and for once in her life she'd listened, which frightened him more than anything she could have said. He went in his plain dark Wick coat with the collar up and the sleeves pulled down over his bare left arm, with no cuff, because the cuff was lying on the marble of the Dial where it had fallen, as far as he knew, and he hadn't wanted it back.

The third shed was the one Gannet had named. He stood in the doorway for a long time, in the steam, before he went in.

It was like walking into a cloud with a fire inside it. Heat hit him in the face. The light came from the furnace mouths under the great iron vats, red, flickering, and from a few lamps hung from the rafters, haloed in steam. Women moved through it. Dozens of them. Sleeves rolled to the shoulder, forearms scalded red, wooden paddles as long as oars, stirring the boiling linen in the vats, hauling it out steaming on poles, feeding it through great iron mangles that groaned like ships. Their counts glowed on their bare arms in the red light, short most of them, Wick counts, twenty years, fifteen, ten.

And they were singing.

No words. Just sound, rising and falling, the way water rises and falls. An old song. He'd heard it once before, a long time ago, from the kitchens of Thane House, coming up a back stair: a girl's voice humming it over a sink, with no idea anyone was listening.

He stood in the doorway with the steam on his face and couldn't move.

The singing stopped.

Not all at once. It went out the way a count goes out under a long slow draw, one voice after another, from the nearest vat to the farthest, until the only sounds left were the furnaces roaring and the linen bubbling and the mangles groaning to a stop. Forty women in the red steam, turning, looking at him. At his height, and his face, and his good boots, and his collar turned up against nothing, as if a coat could make him anything other than what he was.

At the farthest vat, a woman put down her paddle.

She was big. Not tall, broad, with forearms like hams scalded raw to the elbow and iron-grey hair tied back under a cloth. Fifty, perhaps. Her count, on her bare left arm, was eleven years. She wiped her hands on her apron, slowly, and came toward him down the long aisle between the vats, and the other women drew back out of her way the way the Refectory drew back for his grandmother.

She stopped in front of him.

She had Liesl's eyes. He'd known she would. He hadn't known it would be like this.

She hit him across the face.

Open-handed, with her whole arm behind it, a laundress's arm that had hauled wet sheets out of boiling water for thirty years. His head snapped sideways. The steam house rang with it. He tasted blood where his teeth had cut the inside of his cheek, and he didn't lift his hand to his face, and he didn't step back.

"You've got her face on your conscience," said Lotte Varga, "and the nerve to walk in here."

"Yes," said Severin.

"Get out."

"I need your help."

She laughed. It was a terrible sound, short and dry, with no laugh in it at all.

"*Help.*" She looked him up and down. "The Thane boy. In a Wick coat, with his collar up. Come down to the Sump to ask the laundress for *help*." She turned her head and spat on the wet brick between them. "I heard what happened on the Dial. The whole Wick heard. The Keeper's got your girl now, hasn't she? Your Wick tithe. Drinking her every morning in that black house." Her lip curled. "Good. Let her. Now you'll know."

He'd thought she would say something like that. He'd thought it on the whole walk down through the courts of the Sump in the fog. It didn't help.

"I'm not asking for me," he said.

"No? Who, then?"

"There's a woman under the Meridian," said Severin. "Under the clock. Chained in silver thread under the pendulum. She's been there ten years. My grandmother drinks her. The Long-Lived drink her." His voice was very steady. He'd practiced. "She's the girl's mother. And there's a boy in a cellar on Broker's Row with thirty-one years on his arm who's never met her. And the girl has a Ledger thread on her wrist, and as long as it's there, nobody can take her out of that house without my grandmother going downstairs and putting her hand on her mother's heart." He swallowed. "I need someone who can cut a Ledger thread in the slack and leave both ends alive. They tell me there's one person in Lanthorn who can. They tell me it's you."

Lotte Varga looked at him for a long time in the red steam.

"No," she said.

She turned to go back to her vat.

He went down on his knees.

He hadn't planned that. It wasn't pride; he'd left most of his pride on the white marble of the Dial along with his cuff. It was simply that his legs went, the way Hollis's had gone after the Echo. He knelt on the wet brick of the steam-house floor in his Wick coat, with lye water soaking through to his knees and forty laundresses watching, and reached into his pocket.

"Mrs. Varga," he said.

She stopped. She didn't turn around.

"I have something of hers."

He held it out on his open palm.

Small. Gold. Cheap gold plate, worn thin at the edges. The crystal smashed into a star. And on the case, worn almost smooth by somebody's thumb, a single letter.

*L.*

And it was ticking.

He heard her hear it. Even over the furnaces, even over the bubbling vats, in that silent shed, the tiny dry sound of it carried: tick, tick, tick, the smallest voice in the world.

Lotte Varga turned around.

She looked at the watch on his palm. All the color went out of her red, scalded, furious face, the way it had gone out of his own on the morning they found Joss.

"It stopped," said Severin. "The afternoon she died. Twenty past four. It hit the floor of the long gallery and stopped, and I picked it up, and I've tried to make it go every day for five years. I fix clocks. It's the only thing I'm any good at. I couldn't fix that one." His hand was shaking. He let it. "And seven days ago, when the clock moved, it started again. By itself."

She took it.

She took it off his palm in her big raw hand as if it were made of eggshell, and held it up to her ear, and closed her eyes.

For a long time she didn't say anything. The steam rolled around them. Somewhere behind her, one of the women began, very softly, to hum the old song again, and then stopped, as if she'd thought better of it.

"It was mine," said Lotte Varga at last, without opening her eyes.

He didn't understand.

"The watch. It was mine first." Her voice had gone rough. "L for Lotte. My mother gave it to me when I went into service at Thane House at fourteen, so I'd know when my half-day started. And I gave it to Liesl when she went up to the big house at twelve. On a blue ribbon." She opened her eyes and looked at him. "So she'd always know what time it was. So she'd know when to come home."

He couldn't speak.

Lotte Varga stood in the red steam with her daughter's watch, her own watch, ticking against her ear. Then she put it in the pocket of her apron, very carefully, and buttoned the pocket.

"You'll learn from me," she said.

He looked up.

"Cutting. Ledger thread. In the slack." Her voice was flat and hard again, a laundress's voice, a voice for giving orders across a shed full of boiling water. "I won't do it for you. You'll do it yourself. I'll teach you. And it'll cost you."

"Anything."

"Don't say *anything*, Thane. You don't know what anything costs. Your kind never does." She folded her arms. "Every day you come down here to learn, you'll hear about my girl. Every single day. What she ate and how she laughed and which of the other girls she fought with and what she said the first time she saw the sea. You'll hear it all. Until you know her better than you know yourself." Her jaw set. "That's the price."

"Yes," said Severin.

"You haven't heard any of it yet."

"Yes," he said again.

She looked down at him for a long moment, kneeling in the lye water. Then she reached down and took hold of his left arm, hard, and shoved the sleeve of his Wick coat up to the elbow, and turned his arm over to the red light of the furnaces.

All that grey. And at the wrist, four gold lines. Two notches.

Lotte Varga looked at it without any expression at all.

"Four years," she said.

"And two months."

"Then we'd better start tonight."
