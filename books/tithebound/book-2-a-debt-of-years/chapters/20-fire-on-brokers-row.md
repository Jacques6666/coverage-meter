# Chapter Twenty

## Fire on Broker's Row

::: {.pov}
Severin
:::

"I'm engaged," said Hollis.

He said it the way you'd say *I've been shot*. He was standing at the bottom of Gannet's cellar ladder in yet another of his grandfather's ruined coats, with coal dust on his cheekbones and a crumpled letter in his hand, and his long freckled face had gone the color of skimmed milk.

"To be married," he said. "Mother arranged it. Yesterday. While I was at the Regatta pretending to be drunk on the Penhallow barge, which only has six oars and leaks." He held up the letter. "She's been negotiating it for weeks, apparently. Without telling me. She says a Penhallow with six years needs to *settle* before he's *unsettled*." He swallowed. "She means dead."

"Who?" said Bry, from the stove.

Hollis looked at the letter.

"Ambrose Vey," he said faintly.

There was a short silence in Gannet's back room.

"Vadra's nephew?" said Bry. "The tall one? With the hair?"

"Her late husband's sister's son. Yes. The tall one. With the hair." Hollis sat down abruptly on the bottom rung of the ladder. "He reads poetry, Bry. In *public*. At parties. He stood up at the Lisle Midsummer last year and read a sonnet about the Sallow at dawn, and everyone laughed, and he didn't even *notice*." He put his face in his hands. "He has forty years. He's very handsome. He wrote to me this morning. A *letter*." His voice came out muffled through his fingers. "It was quite a good letter."

Bry looked at Severin across the room. Severin looked back.

"Penhallow," said Severin. "Do you *want* to marry Ambrose Vey?"

Hollis lifted his face out of his hands. It had gone from skimmed milk to pink, all the way to the tips of his ears.

"I didn't say that," said Hollis Penhallow. "I didn't say anything of the sort."

---

They went up on the roof that afternoon. It was the only place in Gannet's where you could talk without Kit hearing, and the only place in the Wick where you could see the clock.

Three minutes past eleven.

Hollis sat with his back against the chimney stack and his knees pulled up and the letter still in his hand, and didn't read it. Severin sat beside him on the wet slates. Below them, Broker's Row was full again, the queues at Pruett's going all the way to the corner now, footmen in livery stamping their feet in the cold. TWELVE CROWNS THE YEAR, said the sign in Pruett's window today.

"Six years," said Hollis, after a long time. "Did I ever tell you I'm frightened?"

"No."

"I'm not supposed to be. It's the one thing Penhallows are good at. Dying young. We're very graceful about it. There's a whole wing of our portrait gallery that's nothing but beautiful people under thirty looking resigned." He turned the letter over in his fingers. "I've been making jokes about it since I was seven. It's easier. Everyone laughs, and nobody has to be sad, and I don't have to think about it."

He was quiet for a moment.

"I think about it every night," he said. "Every single night. I lie in bed and look at my arm."

Severin didn't say anything. He looked at the clock across the roofs.

"I'm frightened too," he said.

It surprised him, saying it. He'd never said it out loud. Not to Tamsin, not to Lotte, not to himself. He sat on the wet slates with his splinted hand against his chest and the words out in the cold air between them, and he didn't take them back.

Hollis turned his head and looked at him.

"You've got eight," he said.

"Eight and some months."

"A Thane with eight." Hollis's mouth twitched. "That's practically a Penhallow. We should start a club. Very exclusive. Meetings on roofs."

"You'd make jokes the whole time."

"Of course I would. Someone has to." Hollis looked down at the letter in his hand. "You're the only person I know with fewer years than me who isn't a Wick child, Thane. Did you know that? Everyone else in the Gilt has fifty, a hundred, more. And they're all frightened now. All of them. Looking at their arms. Fainting at regattas." His voice went strange. "Welcome to how the rest of us have always felt."

The clock hung over the island. Three minutes past.

"The Charter," said Hollis slowly. "Wat read it. *What was bought returned to the seller.*" He looked at Severin. "Penhallow sold. A hundred years ago. My great-great-grandfather. To Thane, for a racehorse that died. That's why we're short. That's why we've always been short." He swallowed. "If it strikes. If it really does go home. All of it." He couldn't finish.

"He's dead," said Severin gently. "The seller."

"I know he's dead." Hollis's voice was very small. "I know. I just—" He stopped. "I'd like to think they'd come home to somebody. That's all. Even if it isn't me. I'd like to think they'd go somewhere."

They sat on the roof in the cold and watched the clock and didn't say anything else for a long time.

"Marry him," said Severin, at last.

Hollis went pink again.

"He reads poetry at *parties*, Thane."

"Then you'll never have to," said Severin. "Marry him."

---

In the back room below, Pell was teaching Bry to knead dough.

He was sitting up on the cot by the stove, still in Gannet's shawls, with his face still purple and green down one side but the eye open now, and a bowl of dough in his lap, and Bry was kneeling beside the cot with her sleeves rolled up and her taped fists covered in flour, pounding it as though it had insulted her mother.

"Gently," Pell was saying. "Bry. *Gently.* It's not a Greycoat."

"It's *resisting*."

"It's supposed to resist. That's how you know it's alive." He reached over, very slowly, and put his huge hands over hers in the bowl. "Like this. Push, and fold, and turn. Push, and fold, and turn."

Bry went very still, with his hands over hers.

Then she said, "Fine," in a voice Severin had never heard her use, and pushed, and folded, and turned.

Earlier, Severin had come down the ladder and found them the other way round: Pell on his feet for the first time, swaying, by the stove, and Bry in front of him with her fists up, showing him how to hold his thumb outside so he wouldn't break it. "You held a door with your *back*," she'd been saying. "Next time you'll hold it with *these*." And Pell had looked down at his own huge fists with an expression of mild alarm, as though someone had handed him two loaded pistols.

Severin stood at the bottom of the ladder now and watched them knead, and went back up without saying anything.

---

*I said no,* Tamsin told him that night. Down the silver thread, in the dark, every word free. *To Lisle. On the barge. And then my grandmother-in-law-to-be asked if I'd said yes, and I said no to her too.*

He'd been lying on the cot in the cellar with his eyes open. He sat up.

*Grandmother-in-law-to-be,* he sent. Five hours. He didn't care.

A long pause. Then something came down the thread that was so plainly a laugh, a breath of time with no word in it at all, that he put his hand over his mouth in the dark.

*I said it to see if you were listening,* she said. *You were.* Another pause. *She said "good girl." She didn't believe me. She looked at me the whole way home in the carriage like I was a watch she wanted to take apart.* A pause. *She knows about Lisle's plan. She's known for a hundred years. She'll be ready for it.*

*And for us?*

*I don't know,* said Tamsin. *She hasn't said a word about you. Not since the chocolate. Not one.* A pause. *I think that's worse.*

---

The barge with Orrin's rope came up the river at dusk the next day and tied up at the Wick quay below the Candleworks.

He'd known Orrin was meeting it. They'd talked about it at the long table: eight hundred feet of tarred rope from a cellar in Calder Bay, too heavy to carry, coming up on a herring barge under a cargo of salt fish, and Orrin and Hob would unload it after dark onto a handcart and bring it up the back alleys to Gannet's, coil by coil, and nobody would look twice at two men with a cart full of rope on the river flats where every second cart had rope in it.

He'd known all that. He was sitting at the long table with Kit when the handcart came up the alley behind the shop, and he heard Orrin's boots, and Hob's, and the cart's wheels rattling on the cobbles, and the back door opening.

He didn't hear the other boots until it was too late.

---

It was the dogs he heard first.

A baying. Two, three of them, coming fast up the narrow alley behind Orrin and Hob, the deep bell-voice of hounds that have found a scent. And men behind the dogs, shouting. And then the front of the shop, the boarded window with CLOSED FOR ILLNESS in it, came in.

Not opened. *Came in.* Glass and board and splinters, all at once, across the shop floor, and lantern light pouring through the hole, and grey coats.

"*Durrow!*" Bry was on her feet, flour to the elbows. "They followed the *cart*—"

He was already moving. Kit. Kit was beside him at the long table with his maps spread out in front of him, all of them, every one, and he grabbed Kit by the collar of his too-big coat and hauled him off the bench and shoved him toward the curtain behind Gannet's chair, toward the cellar ladder, toward the chandlery tunnel and the dark.

"*Go.* Down. Gannet, the boy—"

Gannet was already up out of her chair, faster than he'd have believed a woman her size could move, with her shawls flying, reaching for Kit with one ringed hand and for a lamp with the other. Pell was off the cot. Magpie was gone; he didn't see where; she was simply not on the floor by the ladder anymore. Orrin came in the back door with a coil of rope on his shoulder and a dog hanging off his other arm by the sleeve, swearing, and Hob behind him with a cudgel.

Grey coats in the shop. Coming through the curtain between the shop and the back room.

At the front of them, red-faced, grinning, Kell Durrow.

"There he is," said Durrow. "The Thane boy. In a *pawnshop*." He laughed. "Keeper said you'd turn up somewhere that smelled of tallow."

Behind him, one of his men had a lamp. Not a lantern. A glass oil lamp, the kind that sat on Gannet's counter. He lifted it over his head.

"*No*—" said Gannet.

He threw it.

---

It burst against the doorframe in a sheet of fire.

Oil, everywhere. On the curtain, which went up all at once like paper, roaring. On Gannet's shelves behind it, the jars and the bales and the boxes of a broker's lifetime, old ledgers, old cloth, black thread on bales, all of it dry as tinder. Fire ran along the shelves as fast as water runs down a gutter. In a breath the shop was full of it. In two, the doorway between the shop and the back room was a wall of orange.

Smoke. Thick, black, choking, rolling across the ceiling of the back room and down.

He couldn't see. He couldn't breathe. He had Kit's collar in his fist still, he thought, he had it, and then a grey coat came out of the smoke and hit him in the side of the head with a truncheon and the world went white and he was on his knees on the floor with his ears ringing and his hand empty.

"*Kit!*"

He couldn't see him. He couldn't see anything. Black smoke and orange light and shapes moving in it. Bry's voice somewhere, roaring. A dog yelping. Gannet, shouting for the cellar. Orrin's voice, very close: "*Thane! The ladder! Come on!*"

"*Kit—*"

And then he saw him.

Not at the ladder. Not at the curtain. At the *table*. Kit had gone back. He'd got as far as Gannet's chair, and then he'd turned round and gone back through the smoke to the long table, to his maps, and he was standing there in his too-big coat with his arms full of paper, scrabbling the last of them up off the burning table, coughing, his face streaked black, his eyes streaming.

His maps. Every map he'd ever drawn.

"*Kit, leave them—*"

A big shape came out of the smoke on the far side of the table. Red-faced. Grinning.

Durrow picked Kit up the way you'd pick up a cat. One arm round his middle, the boy's feet off the floor, the maps scattering out of his arms in a white flurry into the smoke and the fire. Kit screamed. Not a word. Just a scream, high and terrible, the sound a ten-year-old makes when his lungs are made of wet paper and full of smoke.

He reached down into himself and pulled.

He didn't care what it cost. He didn't do the sum. He reached down to where his own life sat at his wrist in its short row of gold, and all the slow tide of years she'd poured into him on the floor of the Violet Rooms, and he pulled, and *stilled*—

And the smoke stilled.

Not Durrow. The smoke. Between them. A wall of it, frozen, solid, black and opaque as stone, hanging in the air between him and the table, between him and the boy. The working had gone into the first thing it touched, the way a working does when you cast it blind, and the first thing it touched was smoke.

He stood there on his knees on the floor of Gannet's burning back room with his hand outstretched toward a wall of stilled black smoke, and through it, very faintly, muffled, he heard Kit scream again, further away now. Going away. Out through the shop. Out through the broken window. Into the street.

He let the stilling go.

The smoke fell. Rolled. And on the far side of the table, there was nobody. Nothing. Just the flames running along Gannet's shelves and the scattered maps burning on the floor, curling, blackening, every careful pencil line going up.

Then hands. On him. Orrin's, brick-red and salt-rough, and Bry's, hard, dragging him backward across the floor through the smoke toward the curtain, toward the ladder, toward the cellar and the tunnel and the dark, while he fought them, while he twisted and shouted the boy's name into the fire.

---

He came back at dawn.

Alone. Through the chandlery tunnel and up the cellar ladder into what had been the back room of Mother Gannet's shop at the bottom of Broker's Row.

It was black. All of it. The walls, the ceiling, the floor. The shelves were gone, and the jars, and the bales, and the curtain, and Gannet's chair by the stove. The long table was a heap of charcoal. The roof was half gone, and through it, through the open black beams, the grey dawn sky, and across the river, over the island, the clock.

Three minutes past eleven.

Everyone had got out. Gannet, through the cellar. Pell, carried by Bry and Orrin between them. Hob, with a burnt arm. Magpie had turned up an hour later at the chandlery with a dog bite and three of Kit's maps, singed, that she'd snatched off the floor on her way out the back without anyone seeing. Vadra had been in the Sump. Hollis had been in the Gilt.

Everyone had got out except Kit.

He stood in the middle of the black room in the grey light and didn't move.

*Tamsin,* he'd sent, at the third bell. One word. And then he couldn't send anything else, because there was no word for it, and he'd felt her heart through the thread go still, and then very fast, and then he'd felt her start to cry.

He looked down.

At his feet, on the black floor, in the ash where the long table had been, something glinted. Small. Round. Dull tin, with a cracked crystal that someone had replaced.

He knelt and picked it up.

It was warm, from the embers. The crystal had cracked again in the heat, across, in a single line. And under it, the hands were moving.

Kit's watch. Still ticking.
