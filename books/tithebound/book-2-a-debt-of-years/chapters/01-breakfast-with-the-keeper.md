# Chapter One

## Breakfast with the Keeper

::: {.pov}
Tamsin
:::

The first thing Lady Aurelia Thane taught me in her house was how to hold a teacup steady while she drank me.

"Not like that, dear." She reached across the breakfast table and tilted my cup with one small finger, so the tea stopped trembling at the rim. "Like this. Elbow on the cloth. Wrist loose. If you hold it as though it might bite you, it will spill, and then Nessa has to fetch a fresh cloth, and Nessa has quite enough to do." She smiled at the maid standing by the door. "Don't you, Nessa?"

"Yes, my lady," whispered Nessa, to the floor.

"There," said Lady Aurelia. "Now give me your other hand."

I gave it to her.

It was the sixth morning. I'd learned by the second that there was no point in refusing. On the first morning I'd put both hands under the table and kept them there, and she'd waited, quite patiently, eating toast, for the length of time it takes to eat three slices of toast, and then she'd simply reached down the silver thread on my wrist from the other side of the table without touching me at all, and pulled. It hadn't hurt more. It had only been worse, somehow, being drunk from across a tablecloth like water through a straw, with my hands hidden in my lap like a child's.

So now I gave her my hand.

She took it in her cool dry fingers, the way she'd taken it in the Refectory a lifetime ago, and turned it palm up on the white linen between the toast rack and the honey. She looked at my count for a moment, fondly, the way you'd look at a garden you'd planted. Then she closed her eyes.

And drew.

A year. I knew exactly how much by now. It went out of me the way it had gone out on Pruett's counter, the way it had gone out at the Tithe Market under a binder's needle: a long, falling, lonely feeling under the breastbone, like missing a stair in the dark and then missing the next one too. I watched the topmost line of my count brown at the ends and go grey. I watched it go into her. Through her fingers, up the silver Ledger thread that ran from my wrist to hers, and into that cold slow heart I could feel beating at the other end of it, the heart like a clock in a cellar.

Her lips parted a little. Her eyes stayed closed.

"Mm," said the Keeper of the Ledger.

And then the other thing happened. The thing that always happened, the thing that had happened every morning of my life since I was nine years old, except that now I knew where it came from.

It came up from below.

Warm. Slow. Like water seeping up into a footprint on a beach. From somewhere deep under my ribs, from the invisible thread at my wrist that I'd never seen and had always felt, from across the river and down under the Meridian and down further, under the tower, under the rock, in a round black room where a woman knelt under a pendulum with her arms held out in silver. I felt it come. I felt the grey line at the top of my count warm, and brighten, and light.

Seventy-one years and four months. Not a day less.

My count never moved. That was the horror of it. It made me the perfect cup.

Lady Aurelia opened her eyes and let go of my hand, and patted it twice, and reached for her tea.

"Your mother's vintage," she said contentedly. "I've had ten years of it. But it comes through you so much *brighter*, dear. Like light through a clean window." She took a candied violet from the little silver box beside her saucer and put it on her tongue. "Eat something. You're far too thin. Nessa, the eggs."

---

The Glass Room was at the end of the west wing of Thane House, and it was exactly what it sounded like: a long room made almost entirely of glass, iron ribs and great curved panes, built out from the black stone of the house over the steepest drop of the highest terrace in the Gilt. It was full of plants. Orange trees in tubs, and climbing jasmine, and violets, hundreds of violets, in pots along every sill and on stands between the chairs and hanging in wire baskets from the iron ribs overhead, so that the whole room smelled the way she smelled.

And through the glass, beyond the violets, beyond the white terraces falling away below us and the dark roofs of the Wick below them and the grey ice-rimmed Sallow below that, there was the Meridian. The island. The tower. And the great pale face of the Horologe, with its single iron hand.

One minute past eleven.

I looked at it every morning. I couldn't help it. Neither could anyone in the Gilt, apparently. Nessa had told me, the third night, in a whisper, while she was brushing out my hair: they were all looking at it. The ladies in their drawing rooms. The lords in their clubs. Servants caught staring out of upstairs windows with the dusters forgotten in their hands. The hand had pointed at eleven for three hundred years and now it pointed at one minute past, and nobody on the white terraces could stop looking at it, as if it were a crack in a wall they'd always thought was solid.

"Do stop looking at it, dear," said Lady Aurelia, buttering toast. "Everybody's been staring at that clock for six days. One minute. You'd think the sky had fallen."

"Hasn't it?"

She laughed, the light silvery laugh I'd first heard in the Refectory over *seventy-one, forgive me*.

"The sky has been falling for three hundred years," said Lady Aurelia Thane. "It's never once landed on me."

But she didn't look at the clock. I noticed that. In six days of breakfasts in that glass room, with that pale face hanging in the window behind my chair, she had never once turned her head toward it.

---

I'll tell you about the first two days, because I'm not proud of them and I'd rather get it over with.

On the first day, when they carried me up out of the Undercroft in the dark and put me in a carriage and brought me up the Gilt Road to Thane House, I bit a guard. In the carriage. Hard, on the hand, the way I'd bitten Severin's glove on the Hours Bridge, and I tasted blood, and the guard didn't make a sound and didn't let go. On the second day I tried every door in the west wing, and then every window, and then I broke one: a little diamond-paned window at the end of a corridor, with a brass candlestick, so that the glass went down in pieces into the snowy garden three floors below. I'd thought I might climb out. I couldn't. There were bars on the outside that you couldn't see from the inside until the glass was gone.

Nobody punished me. That was the worst of it. Nobody shouted. A glazier came the next morning and fixed the window while I watched. The guard I'd bitten came on duty at my door that evening with a bandage on his hand and bowed to me and said, "Good evening, miss." And at breakfast Lady Aurelia said, buttering toast, "I hear you've been exploring. Isn't it a lovely house? You must let Nessa show you the long gallery. The light in the afternoon is quite something."

On the third day I stopped. Not because I'd given up. Because I'd understood.

She'd told me, that first morning, across the breakfast table, quite kindly, as if explaining something to a child.

"You may go anywhere you like in the house, dear. The gardens too, with Nessa. Not the gates; there are men at the gates who've been told to be kind to you, and they will be, right up until the moment they're not." She'd smiled. "But really, where would you go? Every year you have comes from your mother. And your mother is mine."

*Where would you go.*

If I ran, the thread on my wrist ran with me. The Ledger thread, silver, tied in the Keeper's own presence with a Ledger knot. She could drink me from across a tablecloth; she could drink me from across the city. And if I ran far enough and fast enough that she got angry, she didn't have to come after me at all. She only had to walk down her red-carpeted stair to a round black room and lay her hand on a kneeling woman's heart.

So I stopped trying the doors.

I let Nessa show me the long gallery. The light in the afternoon was quite something.

---

There were four threads on my wrist now.

I'd lie in the enormous bed in the Violet Rooms at night, in the dark, with the violet silk on the walls and the violets painted on the ceiling, and hold my left wrist against my chest, and feel them. One by one. I'd learned to tell them apart the way you learn to tell voices apart in a crowded room.

The new one was silver. The Ledger thread. Cold, and slow, and patient. A heartbeat like a corridor in a shut house: one beat, and then a long, long nothing, and then another. And under it, always, the hunger. Even asleep, she was hungry. I could feel it at the other end of the line like a mouth.

The black one was Bry's. The cut end of Lysander's black thread, sewn under my own skin with a broker's needle in a little square room under the cistern, tied off with a broker's twist. Nobody had cut it since. Bry's heart came down it fast and light, like a bird beating against the inside of a box, and every night it was angry. I'd lie there and feel her pacing somewhere in the Wick, I'd swear I could feel her *pacing*, and I'd want to laugh and cry at the same time.

The third was the one I'd never seen. My mother's. It didn't feel like a thread at all. It felt like being held. Warm, and very deep, and very slow, under everything else, a heartbeat that had been there every night of my life since I was nine, that I'd thought was my own pulse in my ears. It was quiet now. It had been quiet since the round room, since *wait for him*. But it was there.

And the fourth was silver too. The first one. The oldest, now. The one the binder had tied in the Tithe Court under the clock on Tithe Day, with the whole Gilt watching.

Severin.

I could feel him. Every night. Far off, across the river, down in the Wick somewhere: a heartbeat slow and even, trained into steadiness, the drum in another room that I'd fallen asleep to for a whole winter. He was alive. He was *there*. I'd felt him carried off the Dial. I'd felt his grandmother tear three years out of him with one small hand; I'd felt the thread at my wrist jerk with it like a rope with a body falling on the end. I knew exactly what was left on his arm. I could have told you to the notch.

Four years. Two months.

I couldn't speak to him. I didn't know how. I'd lie there in the dark and hold my wrist and say his name under my breath, and he couldn't hear it, and his heartbeat went on, slow and even, a long way off.

Some nights it went fast. In the small hours, suddenly, for no reason, as if he'd sat up in the dark. As if he'd felt something. And I'd lie very still with my hand over my wrist and think, *is that me? Can you feel me too? Is that what woke you?*

And then it would slow again, and I'd never know.

---

"Severin," I said, on the sixth morning, over the eggs. "Where is he?"

Lady Aurelia set down her cup.

It was the first time I'd asked. I'd been careful not to. I'd thought that if she knew how much I wanted to know, she'd find a way to use it, the way she found a way to use everything. But it was the sixth morning, and I'd felt his heart go fast at three in the morning again, and I couldn't stand it.

"My great-great-great-grandson," she said, "is dead to me."

"He isn't dead."

"No." She considered a violet. "Not yet. He's in the Wick, I'm told. With that Vey woman and her Wick friends, in a pawnshop that smells of tallow. The Greycoats are looking for him." She ate the violet. "They'll find him eventually. He's very tall, and he's never in his life been able to hide what he's thinking from his face. It's his great failing. He gets it from his father."

I didn't say anything. I made my face into a wall.

She watched me do it, with her head on one side, and smiled.

"And your little brother is in the same pawnshop, I hear," she said. "In the cellar. Christopher. With his thirty-one years and his maps." She brushed a crumb of sugar off her lip. "Let him stay there for now. I'm a patient woman, dear. I've had a great deal of practice."

My hand had gone tight around the teacup. Not like that, dear. Wrist loose. The tea trembled at the rim.

She reached across the table and tilted it straight again with one small finger.

"There," she said. "Now, Nessa will take you to see the long gallery again this afternoon. And this evening I want you to rest. Next week I have friends coming." She smiled with both dimples. "They're so looking forward to meeting you."

---

That night I lay in the Violet Rooms in the dark with my left wrist held against my chest and the four threads under my fingers.

Cold. Fast. Slow. Far.

Across the river, Severin's heart was beating, slow and even. Under it, Bry's, pacing. Under everything, my mother's, deep and warm and silent. And at the other end of the coldest one, the hunger, asleep, waiting for breakfast.

I'd stopped saying his name out loud. It only made it worse. I lay there with my eyes open and watched the painted violets on the ceiling go grey and then black as the fire burned down, and counted them, because counting is what I do. I'd got to four hundred and twelve, which seemed like a joke somebody was playing on me, when the deepest thread moved.

Not my pulse. Not a heartbeat. A warmth, rising. Spreading up from my wrist into my arm, into my chest, the way it had spread through me in the round room under the clock when I knelt in the pool of light with my face against my mother's neck.

And then a voice.

Not in my ear. Not in the air. Inside. Down the thread I'd never seen, from very far away and very far down, cracked and thin and dry as if it hadn't been used, but clear. Clearer than it had been in the chamber. The voice from the end of my bed, tracing circles in the air.

*Tamsin.*

I stopped breathing.

*Don't answer. You don't know how yet, and if you try you'll only frighten yourself. Just listen.*

I lay there with my hand clamped over my wrist and tears running sideways into my hair and listened.

*I can teach you to speak to him,* said my mother, from under the clock. *Down his thread. The way I'm speaking to you. It's in our blood; it's the one gift the well gives that they never found out about. I can teach you.*

A pause. Long. I could feel her gathering herself, the way you gather yourself to lift something heavy.

*But you must learn fast. She hasn't told you yet. She will.*

*At the Turning of the Year, she means to bring you down to me.*

*And keep you.*
