# Chapter Seven

## The Leak

::: {.pov}
Tamsin
:::

I felt it go out of him at a quarter past midnight.

I was asleep. I'd fallen asleep with my hand over my wrist the way I always did, and I came up out of it all at once, the way you come up out of cold water, gasping, with my heart slamming and the silver thread at my wrist on fire.

Not warm. Not his heartbeat, slow and steady and far away. *Fire.* A river going out of him. The same thing I'd felt in the ballroom at Thane House on the Long Night, with his arm raised over his head holding up the whole frozen room: his life pouring out of his wrist like blood out of an opened vein. A long hot surge. And a drop. And another surge.

A day. A week. A month.

I was sitting up in the enormous bed with both hands clamped around my wrist as if I could stop it with my fingers, the way you'd press on a wound.

*Severin. Severin, what—*

It went on. I don't know how long. Long enough. Long enough that I knew, before it stopped, almost to the week, the way I'd known Joss's count on the sluice grate and Bry's on the floor of the little square room. I'd done the sum before I could stop myself.

Then it stopped.

His heartbeat came back into my wrist. Fast. Ragged. Alive. And far less than it had been.

*Severin.* I pushed the word down the thread so hard it hurt. *What happened? What did you do?*

Nothing came back.

Of course nothing came back. Every word he sent me was an hour, and he'd just spent a year in the time it took me to wake up. He wasn't going to throw hours down the line to tell me something I could already feel. He was too careful. He was always too careful. He'd be sitting somewhere in the dark, in the Wick, rationing, with his hand over his wrist, feeling me shout at him.

So I reached for the other one.

The black thread. Fast and light, a bird in a box, and tonight it was going like a hammer. I'd never spoken down it before. I didn't know if it would work, a broker's twist and a black thread off a bale, tied by my own shaking hands under the cistern. I breathed on it anyway, the way my mother had taught me, the way you'd breathe on a coal.

*Bry.*

Her heart stopped. I felt it stop, the way you'd feel somebody freeze in the next room. Then it went on faster than ever.

*Bry, it's me. I don't know how, don't ask. What happened to him?*

A long pause. Then, down the black thread, rough and furious and so completely Bry that I laughed out loud in the dark with tears running down my face:

*Saints' teeth, Fenn, you're in my* head.

*Bry. Please.*

Another pause. Shorter. And then the words came in a rush, one after another, not rationed at all, and I felt every one of them cost her and arrive.

*Raid. Steam-house. Durrow, the red one. Twenty coats. He stilled the whole shed to get the laundresses out. Held it for ages. He's all right. He's sitting on a wall being shouted at by an old woman.* A pause. *Three years, Tam. He's got three left. And a bit.*

*You're paying an hour a word.*

*I've got eighteen years and nothing to spend them on,* said Bry down the black thread, *and Lysander Crane's dead, so I'm spending them on whatever I like. Go to sleep, Tam.*

I didn't go to sleep.

---

Three years. And two months, or a little less.

I lay on my back in the dark with the painted violets on the ceiling going grey and black and grey again as the clouds moved across the moon, and I held my left wrist against my chest, and I felt him. His heartbeat, ragged, slowing, a long way off. I felt him walk, slowly, a long way, uphill. I felt him stop, and lie down. I felt him, after a long time, go still.

Asleep.

Three years.

I thought about the ballroom at Thane House. About the frozen guests and the gold light, and his arm raised, and his face across the room turning slowly toward me, like a man moving underwater. About pushing. Not knowing how, not knowing it could be done. Taking hold of the thread with the part of me that knows my own count, and pushing, and feeling it go out of me, years, toward him, and feeling him feel it arrive.

*You stupid, stupid girl,* he'd said into my hair. *You first,* I'd said.

He was asleep now. He couldn't refuse.

I reached down into the silver thread.

*Gently,* my mother had said. *Like breathing on a coal.* This wasn't gentle. I didn't want it to be. I took hold of the thread with everything I had and I pushed, and it went, not a breath, not a word: years. A long hot pour out of me, out of my wrist, down the silver line across the dark city. One. I felt it leave me, the falling. Two. Three. I didn't stop.

And under me, behind me, coming up from the bottom of everything, I felt the other thing.

It always happened. It had always happened. When I lost time, it came back. Seeping up from below, warm, slow, like water into a footprint. But I'd never lost this much this fast while I was holding onto something. I'd never poured with one hand and drawn with the other at the same time. Years going out of me down the silver thread. Years coming up into me from the deepest thread of all, from across the river and under it, from the round black room. *From her.* Through me. Like water through a pipe.

Four.

The bed shook.

I thought it was me. I thought I was shaking. Then I heard the glass in the tall windows rattle in their frames, a high thin chattering, like teeth. The silk on the walls rustled. On the mantel, a little porcelain shepherdess walked herself to the edge and fell off and broke.

Five.

And from across the river, out of the dark, through the rattling glass, a sound.

I'd heard it once before, in the round room under the tower, with the threads singing and the pendulum swinging out over my mother's bowed head. Deep. Enormous. Grinding. Like a door three hundred years shut, with somebody's shoulder against it.

*Tick.*

It rolled out over the city. Over the river and the Wick and up the white terraces of the Gilt. Every window in Thane House answered it, a long shuddering ring of glass.

I let go of the thread.

I got out of the bed and went to the window and pulled back the violet curtain with both hands. Across the black river, on the island, the great pale face of the Horologe hung in the dark, lit from beneath by its lamps, as it had every night of my life.

The single iron hand pointed at two minutes past eleven.

---

*Tamsin.*

My mother's voice. Not thin, not dry. Sharp. Frightened. I'd never heard her frightened.

*Tamsin, what did you do?*

*I gave him five years.* I was holding the curtain so hard my knuckles hurt. *He spent one saving a shed full of washerwomen, and he's only got three, and he was asleep, and I—I gave him five.*

Silence. Long.

*It went through me,* she said at last, slowly. *I felt it. Five years, out of the well, up through me, into you, out of you, down the boy's thread. All at once. All in one breath.* A pause. *The clock felt it too. The Stay shivered, all the way up. Like a rope when you pluck it. Like a harp string.*

*That's what moved it? Me?*

*That's what moved it the first time,* said my mother. *In this room. When she tied you to her and the well came up through me, through you, into her. And it moved again tonight.* A long breath, from under three hundred feet of rock. *It's the blood, I think. My time going hard through my own blood. The clock knows us. It was made by one of us.*

I stood at the window and looked at the hand. Two minutes past eleven.

All across the Gilt, the lamps were coming on. I could see them, terrace after terrace, window after window, yellow squares appearing in the dark houses below me. All the Long-Lived of Lanthorn, woken up in the small hours by a tick and a rattling of glass, getting out of their beds and going to their windows and looking out across the river at the clock.

And looking down at their arms.

---

She didn't wait for breakfast.

The door of the Violet Rooms opened at the sixth bell without a knock, and Lady Aurelia Thane came in. Not dressed. In a long grey silk wrapper with her honey-gold hair down her back in a plait, and her feet bare on the carpet, and no box of violets. I'd never seen her without the violets.

She didn't sit down. She came and stood at the end of the enormous bed where I was sitting up with the blanket to my chin, and she looked at me.

Her face was not sweet. It was not anything. It was the face of the woman on the dais at the Reckoning, after Lysander, before *there you are*.

"Something left you last night, dear," she said.

I didn't say anything.

"Five years. Near enough." Her voice was very soft. "I felt it. Not through my thread. Through the well. A great deal of time, all at once, going up through your mother and through you and *out*. Down a thread that isn't mine." She tilted her head. "Where did it go?"

"The clock took it," I said.

She raised her eyebrows.

"When it moved." I made my voice flat and tired and a little frightened, which wasn't hard. "I was asleep. It woke me. It felt like being pulled. Like something reaching down through my mother and through me and pulling, all the way up. I thought I was dying." I swallowed. "And then it ticked."

Lady Aurelia looked at me for a long time.

Then she came around the side of the bed, and sat on the edge of it, and held out her hand.

I gave her my wrist.

She turned it over in the grey dawn light from the window. She looked at my count, seventy-one years and four months, full, every line lit. Then she looked at the threads. The silver Ledger thread, hers, cold. The raw red welt of the black one, Bry's. And the other silver one, the old one, from the Tithe Court under the clock.

She laid one fingertip on it.

"This one's warm," she said.

My heart was going so hard I could feel it in the thread under her finger. I couldn't stop it. I didn't try. Let her think it was fear. It was.

"It's always warm," I said. "It's the oldest."

"Mm." She kept her finger there a moment longer. Then she took it away, and patted my wrist twice, and stood up.

"We'll see," said Lady Aurelia Thane.

---

Nessa came in at the seventh bell to dress me. She didn't look at me. She never looked at me. But this morning her hands were shaking so badly she dropped the hairbrush twice.

The third time, I caught her wrist.

She went rigid. She stared down at my hand on her arm as if it were a snake.

"Nessa," I said. "How often do you tell her?"

She didn't answer. Her face had gone white under the freckles.

"Every morning?" I said. "Every night? Everything I eat and where I walk and how long I stand at the window looking at the clock?" I kept my voice low. "Every time I cry?"

She was trembling all over now. Twenty-three years on her arm, from Candle Row, two streets over from Tallow Lane.

"Every hour," she whispered. "From today. She said. Every hour, miss, I'm to go down to the study and say what you're doing." Her eyes filled. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I'm *sorry*—"

"Why?"

"Pip," said Nessa.

She said it as if it explained everything. And then, when I waited, it came out of her all at once, in a rush, the way things come out of people who've been holding them for a long time.

Her little sister. Seven years old. Born short, three years on her arm, and a cough like Kit's, worse than Kit's. And when Nessa went into service at Thane House at fourteen, the House had paid her family a Grace, the way the Gilt houses do with their Wick servants, instead of wages, because a Grace keeps a family grateful in a way silver never does. Five years. To Pip. Put in by a Ledger binder with a silver needle, the safe way.

"And she can take it back," said Nessa. Her voice cracked. "She told me. The first day. When she gave me you. She said the Grace is the Ledger's, and the Ledger is hers, and it can come out the same way it went in. Any time she likes. If I'm—" She swallowed. "If I'm not *useful*."

I sat there on the edge of the enormous bed in the Violet Rooms, with Nessa's thin wrist in my hand, and I thought about Kit. About thirty years of Grace paid into a ten-year-old's arm on a Tithe Day by a boy in black who'd bought me. About the Keeper at breakfast, buttering toast. *Christopher, isn't it? Thirty-one years. Let him stay there for now.*

Every little sister on every lane in the Wick, with a Grace in her arm and a Gilt hand on the other end of it.

"Nessa," I said. "Look at me."

She did. For the first time in twelve days.

"You've seen my arm," I said. "You've seen her drink from it every morning. You've seen it come back."

She nodded, barely.

"If she ever takes Pip's Grace," I said, "I'll give Pip ten years. Twenty. However many she needs. Out of this arm. I can. It doesn't cost me anything; it comes back by breakfast. You've watched it come back." I held her eyes. "And when I'm out of this house, and I'm going to be out of this house, I'll find Pip on Candle Row, and I'll give her enough that nobody in the Gilt can ever hold her over your head again."

Nessa stared at me. Her mouth opened and shut.

"Why?" she whispered. "Why would you—for *me*? I've been telling her everything."

"Because I'm from Tallow Lane," I said, "and you're from Candle Row. And I'm sick to my back teeth of the Gilt holding our little sisters over our heads."

Nessa looked at me for a long time. Then she sat down, abruptly, on the floor beside the bed, with the hairbrush in her lap, and put her face in her hands, and cried the way Pell had cried beside Anni's empty bed: without any noise at all.

When she was done, she wiped her face on her sleeve, and looked up at me.

"Every hour," she said. "What should I tell her?"

"The truth," I said. "Mostly. I ate breakfast. I walked in the long gallery. I stood at the window and looked at the clock." I let go of her wrist. "Just leave out the parts where I'm talking to people who aren't in the room."

Nessa looked at me. Then at my wrist. Then back at my face, and something in hers changed, a door opening a crack.

"You *talk* to them?"

"Every night," I said.

---

Lady Aurelia passed me in the long gallery that evening.

I was standing in front of the pale gap on the wall where Severin's portrait had hung, the empty rectangle with the hook. I'd been standing there a while. Nessa was a few steps behind me, and every hour she'd gone downstairs to the study and come back, and every hour she'd looked at me on the way past and given me the smallest nod.

The Keeper came down the gallery from the far end in black lace again, with her hair up and the silver box in her hand and the dimples back in her face, as though the woman in the grey wrapper at dawn had never existed. She didn't stop. She walked past me in a cloud of violets, and as she passed, she said, quite pleasantly, without turning her head:

"Oh, and dear. If you leak again, I'll find where it goes."

She ate a violet.

"And I'll drink him."
