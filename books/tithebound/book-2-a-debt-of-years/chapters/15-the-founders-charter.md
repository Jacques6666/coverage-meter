# Chapter Fifteen

## The Founders' Charter

::: {.pov}
Severin
:::

"Hold still," said Lotte Varga, "or I'll do it again from the beginning."

He held still.

She'd come up from the Sump at dusk for the first time, in her apron, with her sleeves rolled and the watch ticking in her pocket, and walked into Gannet's back room as though she'd been coming there for years. Gannet had looked up from her chair by the stove and said, "Lotte," and Lotte had said, "Gannet," and that was apparently all the two of them needed to say to each other. Then Lotte had looked at his right hand, wrapped in a strip of Gannet's shawl, and said something about Thanes and their fists that made Bry laugh for the first time since the stable yard.

Now she had his hand flat on the long table under the lamp, and she was setting the knuckles.

Two of them were broken. He'd known that since the vault, since he'd hit Corwin Lisle and felt something in his own hand give. He hadn't known how much it would hurt to have them put back. She did it without warning, the first one, a sharp pull and a grind he felt in his teeth, and he made a sound he wasn't proud of, and Kit, at the end of the table, put both hands over his ears.

"There's a quickening for that," said Magister Vadra, from the window. "Two weeks of mending in a breath. I'll pay it, if you like, Thane."

"No," said Severin, through his teeth.

"No," agreed Lotte, without looking up. "He's not spending a minute on his own knuckles. Not with what he's got." She pulled the second one. He didn't make a sound this time; he'd bitten the inside of his cheek. "Splint and wait, like the rest of us. Six weeks. You'll learn to cut left-handed." She bound his fingers to a strip of wood with a length of linen, hard, in neat laundress's knots. "You were going to have to anyway. A Ledger cut's no place for a hand that shakes."

"Mine doesn't shake."

She looked at him.

"It will," said Lotte Varga.

---

The Charter lay on the long table between them.

He'd unrolled it from his coat in the Founders' tunnel under the Ostrander gardens, on his knees in the dark, with Orrin holding the lamp and the thief girl, Magpie, keeping watch at the bend. He'd checked it was all there. Two dozen leaves of old vellum, brown at the edges, soft as cloth from three hundred years of being handled by Keepers, stitched together at the left edge with a cord of silver thread and sealed at the bottom of the last page with seven red seals, cracked now, all seven, where they'd hit the floor of the vault.

He hadn't read it. He'd wanted to. He'd wanted to sit down in the tunnel with the lamp and read every word. He hadn't, because he'd known that if he read it alone, he'd decide alone what to do about it.

He'd waited.

They were all there now. Gannet in her chair. Vadra at the window. Lotte with her hands on her knees on Gannet's stool. Orrin on an upturned crate with his coil of rope at his feet. Magpie, who had followed them home through the tunnels without asking and sat down on the floor by the cellar ladder and hadn't left, with her arms around her knees and her thief's eyes going everywhere. Kit at the end of the table with his pencil. Hollis, who'd come down from the Gilt on the coal barge again, white to the lips, who hadn't said a word since he came down the ladder. And Bry, standing behind Hollis's stool with her taped fists clenched, because she couldn't sit down. She hadn't sat down since the stable yard.

"Seven," said Bry. "She took seven off him in the time it took to butter toast. Tam says. And one more every morning till she gets that back." She jerked her chin at the vellum. "So before anybody reads anything, I want to know. Are we giving it to her?"

"Yes," said Hollis.

Everybody looked at him.

He was sitting very straight on the stool with his hands in his lap, and his long freckled face had no joke anywhere in it.

"Give it to her," said Hollis Penhallow. "Tonight. Leave it on the steps of the Ledger House with a ribbon round it. Pell's worth more than a book." His voice cracked. "He held a door for me. He held a *door*, Thane, with his back, while they broke it in on him, and he was singing, and I was in the stable yard pretending to be drunk." He swallowed. "Give it back."

Nobody said anything.

"Pell's worth more than a book," said Magister Vadra, quietly, at the window. "He's not worth more than every tithe that will ever go under that clock after him, Mr. Penhallow. And neither are you. And neither am I." She looked at the vellum on the table. "She won't stop taking his years if we give it back. You know her. She'll take them anyway, to show us what it cost to cross her, and then she'll have her Charter *and* a lesson."

Hollis didn't answer. He looked down at his hands.

"We read it first," said Severin. "All of it. Then we decide. Together." He looked at Hollis. "And whatever we decide about the Charter, we get Pell out. Tamsin says there's a way into the cellars. My mother knows it."

Hollis lifted his head.

"Your *mother*," he said.

"My mother," said Severin. He'd been saying it to himself for three days, since the Violet Rooms. It still sounded like a word in someone else's language. "She walked into the Glass Room this morning and told my grandmother to stop. In front of Durrow. Tamsin says she's never seen anything like it."

Hollis looked at him for a long time.

"All right," he said at last. "Read it."

---

Wat Sorrel read it.

It had to be Wat. Nobody else at the table could. The Charter was written in the old court hand of the Year of the Clock, all loops and long esses and abbreviations, in an ink that had faded to the color of weak tea, and Wat had spent two years copying ledgers in the Ledger House archive, some of them almost as old. He sat at the head of the table under the lamp with his long nose almost touching the vellum and his inky finger moving along the lines, and read aloud, slowly, in a thin nervous voice that got steadier as he went.

*"In the Year of the Clock, at the eleventh hour of the Turning, We the Seven, being Thane, Crane, Lisle, Vey, Ostrander, Marchbank and Penhallow, set down this Charter and bind ourselves and our blood to it forever."*

Hollis made a small sound at *Penhallow*. Nobody looked at him.

*"Whereas the Great Horologe of Lanthorn was made by the Well called Auda, of the women of Marrow, who did fashion it to draw upon her own well, gently and without end, and to give unto each child born in this city its Allotment of years, and at the twelfth hour of every day to settle all accounts of time therein, so that what was bought returned to the seller, and what was taken returned to the taken, and each day began fair."*

At the end of the table, Kit had stopped drawing.

*"And whereas We have judged this settling a ruin to good order and to the Houses, that no estate may be kept nor fortune built where time returns each night to whence it came,"* read Wat, *"We have taken the Well called Auda into our keeping, and set her beneath the Horologe in the chamber made for her, and bound her there with her own thread. And We have Stayed the Hand at the eleventh hour, that the twelfth come not, and that what is bought remain bought, and what is taken remain taken, forever."*

Nobody moved. The stove ticked. Somewhere above them, through the floor of the shop, a cart went by on Broker's Row, and its wheels rattled on the cobbles and were gone.

"*Remain taken forever,*" said Lotte, very quietly. "There it is. In their own hand."

"*It's a recipe,*" said Severin.

---

Wat read on.

The Stay: *a cord of the Well's own thread, drawn from the ring about her throat and carried up the shaft of the pendulum unto the escapement of the Horologe, and there made fast.* The Keeper, chosen from among the Seven to hold the Stay and the Well in trust for all, and to draw from the Well through the Stay *such years as are needful to the keeping*. The Lines: *threads of the Well carried out through the walls of the chamber unto the houses of the Seven, that each may drink of the Well in its measure.*

And the Renewal.

*"At each Turning of the Year,"* read Wat, *"the Keeper shall go down unto the Well, and open her palm with the Keeper's Knife, and lay her hand upon the Well's heart, and in the slack of that heart shall tie again the Knot of the Keeper upon the Stay, with her blood upon it. And so long as the Knot be tied each year, the Hand shall not move."*

He turned the page. The vellum crackled.

*"And should the Stay weaken, as old cords will,"* read Wat, *"it may be made strong again by the blood of a second Well of the same line, given into the Knot at the Renewal. But let that blood be given and not taken; for the Stay is the Well's own thread, and knows the difference, and taken blood will not hold it."*

"Given," said Hollis slowly. "Not taken."

He looked up at Severin.

"That's why," he said. "That's why she offered Tam the heirship. On the bed. With the chocolate." His face had gone white. "She can't just tie her in. It won't work. She needs her *willing*."

Severin had already thought of it. He'd thought of it the moment Wat read *given*. He'd been standing behind a finger's width of dark in the violet silk, listening to his grandmother say *a well's blood, given, not taken; the Founders' Charter is very particular about that*, and he hadn't understood what she meant, and now he did.

"She'll make her willing," he said. "One way or another."

---

The last page.

Wat turned it carefully. It was the one with the seals: seven of them in a row along the bottom edge, cracked red wax, each one stamped with a House's mark. A clock hand for Thane. A crane for Crane. Above them, a short paragraph in a different ink, darker, as if it had been added later, or written by a different hand in a hurry.

Wat read it, and his voice changed.

*"Let no hand cut the Stay. But should it be cut, which God forbid, let it be cut in the slack of the Well's heart and no other moment, lest the Well die of it. And the cut shall be paid in full by the hand that makes it."*

He stopped.

"Go on," said Severin.

*"And thereafter,"* read Wat, *"the Hand shall walk one minute in each day unto the twelfth hour, and at the twelfth hour the Settlement shall be made, as the Well intended."*

He looked up.

"That's it," he said. "That's the end. Then the seals."

The back room was very quiet.

"*Paid in full,*" said Magpie, from the floor by the ladder. It was the first thing she'd said all evening. "What's that mean? Paid what?"

Nobody answered her.

Severin was looking at Lotte.

She was sitting on Gannet's stool with her big raw hands on her knees, the way she sat on her basket in the steam-house, and she hadn't moved. She was looking at the vellum on the table. At the last paragraph, in its darker ink. *Paid in full by the hand that makes it.* Her face didn't change at all. It didn't need to. He'd learned that face over twelve nights on a wet brick floor. He knew exactly what it looked like when Lotte Varga had decided something and wasn't going to tell you.

She felt him looking. She lifted her eyes and met his across the lamp.

She didn't say anything.

---

"Let it walk," said Vadra.

She'd come away from the window. She was standing at the end of the long table, beside Kit, with her hand resting on the back of his chair, looking down at the Charter.

"*As the Well intended,*" she said. "There. In the Founders' own hand. They knew. They always knew what that clock was for. They stopped it because it was fair." Her long severe face was pale. "Cut the Stay. Let the hand walk. A minute a day to midnight. And at midnight, every year they ever stole goes home." She looked around the table. "That's the whole of it. That's what we're for. That's what the Unwound have been for, for thirty years, without even knowing it."

"We save them first," said Severin.

She looked at him.

"Tamsin," he said. "Her mother. Pell. Before anybody cuts anything, we get them out. All three." He held her eyes. "Whatever it costs, Magister, I'm not letting that hand walk with Tamsin bound under it."

Vadra looked at him for a long moment.

"No," she said. "Of course not." She took her hand off the back of Kit's chair. "First things first, Thane."

---

They talked until the second bell. About Pell, mostly. About the cellars of Thane House and the way in behind the copper in the old laundry, which Tamsin had sent him that morning in a rush of words down the silver thread, every one of them hers and free, describing what Evadne had told her in the long gallery. About Durrow's men, and the guards at the gates, and the masons who'd bricked up every passage in the west wing but hadn't gone below the ground floor because his grandmother never thought about anything below the ground floor that wasn't under the clock.

Tomorrow night. They settled on tomorrow night. Pell had forty-two years, and every morning at breakfast he'd have one fewer, and that was arithmetic even Hollis could do.

Then, one by one, they went to bed. Orrin down the ladder to the cellar, with his rope. Bry up the stair to Gannet's attic, without a word, Hollis behind her. Magpie curled up on the floor by the stove with her coat over her head like a cat. Lotte, home, to the Sump, through the back door into the alley, with a last look at him over her shoulder that he didn't know how to read. Gannet, asleep in her chair, snoring softly under her shawls. Kit he carried down the ladder himself, asleep, with the pencil still in his fist.

He came back up, alone, to the long table.

The lamp had burned low. The Charter lay where Wat had left it, open at the last page, with the seven cracked seals. He sat down in front of it and drew the lamp closer and turned back to the first page, to read it again, by himself, all of it, every word, slowly.

That was when he saw the numbers.

Small. In the bottom corner of each leaf, in the same faded court hand, so small he'd missed them in the tunnel and missed them again with Wat reading. A numeral. One. Two. Three. He turned the leaves, one after another, under the lamp, counting, because she would have.

Nine. Ten.

Twelve.

He stopped. He turned back. Ten. He turned forward again. Twelve. He lifted the leaves apart at the stitching with his good hand and looked at the silver cord that bound them at the edge, and there, between ten and twelve, at the stitching, was a stub. A narrow strip of vellum no wider than a fingernail, still caught in the silver thread, with a clean straight edge where the rest of the leaf should have been.

Not torn. Not lost in the vault, swept up and dropped by a thief on the run. *Cut.*

He looked at it for a long time.

The stove ticked. Gannet snored. And at the window, where he'd thought nobody was, a long figure in a dark green gown stood with her back to the curtain and her sleeves down over her knuckles, watching him.

He hadn't heard her come back up. He didn't know how long she'd been there.

Magister Vadra didn't say anything. She didn't look away.

He looked down again, at the narrow stub in the silver stitching. At the clean straight edge.

Page eleven was missing.
