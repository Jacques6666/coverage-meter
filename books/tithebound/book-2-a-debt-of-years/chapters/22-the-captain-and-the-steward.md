# Chapter Twenty-Two

## The Captain and the Steward

::: {.pov}
Severin
:::

Captain Hesketh Marr lived in two rooms over a cooper's yard in Saltmarket, and he came home at the second bell with his hat in his hand and his coat unbuttoned and his face grey, the way a man comes home who has spent the whole day keeping other men from beating people with truncheons.

Severin was sitting on his stairs.

Marr stopped at the bottom with his hand on the rail. He looked up the narrow dark stairwell at him for a long moment. He didn't reach for anything. He didn't call out. He just looked, with his washed-out eyes, the way he'd looked at Joss's arm on the flagstones under the clock.

"You've got some nerve, son," he said.

"Yes," said Severin.

Marr looked at him a moment longer. Then he came up the stairs, heavily, past him, and unlocked his door, and left it open behind him.

---

The rooms were bare. A bed. A table. Two chairs. A stove gone cold. A shelf with three books on it and a framed drawing in pencil, small and faded, of a girl of about eighteen with her sleeve rolled up and her count showing.

Marr lit the lamp. He didn't sit down. He stood by the cold stove with his back to it and his hands behind him and looked at Severin, standing just inside the door with his splinted hand against his chest and Kit's watch, cracked and ticking, in his coat pocket.

"Durrow took a boy out of a burning pawnshop last night," said Marr. "On the Row. Carried him out under his arm. I heard about it at the Watch House this morning. Heard the boy was screaming. Heard the shop's ash." His jaw worked. "Heard the boy's at Thane House now, with his sister."

"Yes."

"Your girl's brother."

"Yes."

Marr looked at him for a long time.

"And you've come to me," he said. "Why?"

"Because you caught a man's wrist on Broker's Row," said Severin. "Three days ago. When he raised his truncheon over a woman who wouldn't sell." He held the captain's eyes. "And because on the morning they found Joss Mallory, you looked at the knot on his wrist and you knew what it was. And on the Dial, at the Reckoning, you said *let it be shown*."

Marr didn't answer.

"The Turning," said Severin. "The day after tomorrow, at dawn. My grandmother goes down her stair under the Horologe with the Council and the Inner Ledger to tie the Stay. With Tamsin. And the boy." He took a breath. "She'll want Greycoats on the stair. Durrow's. I need them to be yours."

---

Marr turned away. He went to the shelf with the three books on it and stood in front of it with his back to the room, looking at the small framed drawing of the girl with her sleeve rolled up.

"My sister," he said, after a while. "Bess. She was a tithe. Forty years ago. House Marchbank." He didn't turn around. "Bought for six. Came home at the Reckoning with four years on her arm. Four. She'd gone with fifty-one." A pause. "She lived to be twenty-four. She used to sit at our mother's window and watch the Gilt Road and not say anything. All day." He touched the edge of the frame. "I drew this the week before she went. I was fourteen. I was going to give it to her when she came home."

Severin didn't say anything.

"I joined the Greycoats the year after she died," said Marr. "I thought, if I wore the coat, I could make sure it didn't happen to anybody else's sister." He laughed, short and dry. "Twenty-six years. I've stood in the Tithe Court eleven times with a blank arm at my feet and a clerk telling me the Ledger considered the matter closed. I've taken the Keeper's silver on the first of every month. I've watched Durrow do what Durrow does and written it down and handed it to a clerk and watched the clerk lose it." He turned around. His long creased face was grey. "And then I stood on the Dial and watched a little woman in black lace take her own kin by the wrist and empty him in one breath, and call it justice, and the whole Gilt clapped."

He looked at Severin.

"I've had enough, son," said Captain Hesketh Marr. "I've had enough for a long time. I just didn't know what to do about it."

"Then do this," said Severin.

---

It was simple, the way the best plans were. Marr was the Captain of the Greycoats. Every guard detail in Lanthorn went through the Watch House on his desk. Durrow's men had been guarding the Keeper since the Reckoning because the Keeper had asked for Durrow's men, and Marr had signed the orders because he'd had no reason not to.

On the morning of the Turning, Marr would sign different orders.

"Durrow will be with her," said Marr. "In the procession. With the boy. She'll want him close; she trusts him. I can't change that." He was sitting at the table now, across from Severin, with the lamp between them. "But the stair's mine. The door at the bottom of the Horologe, the stair down, the passage at the bottom. Twelve men. I'll pick them myself. Men I've known twenty years. Men who've stood in the Tithe Court with me." He tapped the table. "We let the procession go down. Then we close the stair behind them. Nobody comes down after. No more of Durrow's. No Lisle men. Nobody."

"And when it starts?"

Marr looked at him across the lamp.

"When it starts," he said, "I'll come down the stair myself. With my twelve. And I'll arrest Sergeant Kell Durrow for the murder of eleven tithes and the abduction of a child." He paused. "And anyone who tries to stop me."

"Including the Keeper?"

Marr didn't answer for a long time.

"I don't know," he said at last, honestly. "I don't know if I can arrest a woman who can empty a man with one hand. I don't know if anyone can." He looked at the small drawing on the shelf. "But I'll be on the stair."

---

The Meridian was closed for the recess. The court was empty under the clock, with old snow in the corners of the flagstones, and the gatehouse warden at the island end of the Hours Bridge was asleep in his box when Severin went past him in the grey hour before dawn.

He went down the black stair to the Tithe Hall. The iron door at the bottom was shut. He knocked, three times, and waited.

It opened. A lamp. A ring of keys the size of a dinner plate. A short square grey woman with one brown eye and one milky one, in a grey wrapper, with her hair down her back like a rope.

Agathe Rook looked at him.

"Thane," she said. "The Hall's shut. There's nobody in it but me and the damp."

"I know," he said. "It's you I've come to see."

---

She let him in. The Tithe Hall was cold and dark and enormous without its tithes, the iron beds stripped to their frames in two long rows under the arches, the green light through the frosted windows faint and grey. One bed at the end, by the brazier, still had a blanket on it. Hers. And beside the iron door, her chair.

She sat down in it. He stood.

"The cistern door," he said. "At the end. The little one, with the chain." He looked down the long room at it. "I need it open. At dawn on the Turning. For people to go through. Twenty, perhaps. And to come back."

Rook looked at him with her good eye. The milky one roved over his face.

"*Do not go below the Hall,*" she said. "I tell them that every year. Every Tithe Day. I've told it to thirty-one terms of Wick children." She turned her ring of keys over in her lap. "I've never once told them why."

"Do you know why?"

"I know there's something down there the Meridian doesn't want found," said Rook. "I know Lady Thane comes and goes through it some nights, with her binders, by the other way. I know eleven of my tithes came up out of it on the sluice grate over ten years with their arms gone blank." Her jaw set. "I packed them all into boxes. And one I packed who wasn't dead at all." She looked at him. "Calder Bay, Penhallow said. A baby called Wren."

"Yes."

"Burn me," said Agathe Rook softly, for the second time in his hearing. Then she held up the largest key on the ring. "The cistern door's yours. Dawn on the Turning. I'll have it open and I'll be standing at it, and I'll count every one of them through, and I'll count every one of them back." Her milky eye fixed on him. "*Every* one, Thane."

"Thank you."

"Don't." She put the keys back in her lap. Then she was quiet for a while, looking down the long empty Hall, at the stripped beds, at the two that had been Joss's and Anni's.

"The madman on the Row," she said. "The one on the crate. Shouting about midnight. *Every hour goes home.*" She didn't look at him. "Is it true?"

"The Charter says so."

"Bought hours to the seller."

"Yes."

Rook nodded slowly.

"The Meridian pays me in years," she said. "You know that. I've told every term. *Every term I keep you in line, I get to live through another one.*" She held up her left arm, in the grey light, and pushed the sleeve of her wrapper back. Her count ran a little way up her forearm. A few lines. Not many. "I went home from this Hall at the Reckoning thirty-one years ago with nine years on my arm. Those nine were gone by the time I was thirty. Everything since has been paid in by the Ledger. Wick years. Bought on the Row. Thirty-one years of other people's." She lowered the arm. "If that clock strikes twelve, Thane, those go home. Every one. And I go with them."

He didn't know what to say.

"I've lived thirty-one years past my own count," said Agathe Rook, "on years bought from Wick children's mothers. To keep Wick children in line in a Hall under a river while the Gilt drank them." She looked at him with both eyes, the brown and the milky. "If it strikes, I'll go home with them. And gladly." A pause. "I'll have the door open at dawn."

---

They planned it at the chandlery that night.

It wasn't Gannet's. Nothing would ever be Gannet's again. It was the derelict chandlery at the bottom of the alley behind the Row, the one with the cellar full of empty crates and the trapdoor to the Thane tunnel, with its broken windows boarded and a stove someone had dragged in from somewhere, and Gannet sitting beside it in a borrowed chair that was too small for her, grumbling about the draft. Hob with his burnt arm in a sling. Pell on a heap of sacks, sitting up, with Bry beside him. Orrin with his rope, all eight hundred feet of it now, in coils on the floor like sleeping snakes. Magpie by the door. Lotte on an upturned crate with her sleeves rolled. Hollis, down from the Gilt on the coal barge one last time, with Ambrose Vey's letter folded in his breast pocket. Vadra at the boarded window. And a dozen faces Severin had come to know over the last three weeks, Unwound, Wick men and women with old white thread-scars on their wrists, crowded in the shadows round the walls.

On the floor in the middle, weighted at the corners with bits of brick, the three maps Magpie had saved from the fire. Singed at the edges. One of them was the Meridian, from the side, with the long straight double line of the shaft going down through the tower and the rock to the round room at the bottom.

*HIS ROOM*, it said at the top, in letters that pressed too hard.

He laid it out for them. All of it. Piece by piece, the way he'd lay out the inside of a watch on black velvet.

The procession, at dawn, going down the Keeper's stair. Marr's twelve men on the stair, letting it pass, then closing it behind.

Rook at the cistern door. The Unwound going through: Vadra, Lotte, Bry, Magpie, Hob, and the dozen. Across the cisterns in the dark, to the iron door with the clock faces. Up the steps into the passage. Past the little square room with the rings. Down the carved passage to the bend before the silver door. Waiting there. Silent. Out of sight.

"We can't open it," said Vadra. "The silver door. Not from outside. Not for anything but the Keeper's hand."

"Not from outside," said Severin. "From inside, it opens for any hand." He looked round the room. "Tamsin asked her mother. Her mother said it was made to keep people out, not in. *Who'd ever try to get out?*"

A ripple went round the walls.

"And who's inside?" said Bry.

"I am," said Severin.

He put his finger on the long double line of the shaft.

"Orrin and I go up the tower to my old room. In the night, before dawn. We pry up the grate in the floor. Orrin rigs the rope." He looked at Orrin. "I go down."

"Six hundred feet," said Orrin, cheerfully, from the floor. "Six hundred and forty, by the boy's reckoning. In the dark. With a pendulum the size of a cartwheel hanging at the bottom." He patted a coil of rope. "I'll lower you. Not too fast. You'll want to stop above it."

"Tamsin will tell me when." He touched his wrist. "Down the thread. When my grandmother opens her palm. And I drop." He moved his finger to the round room at the bottom of the shaft. "Into the ring of the Long-Lived. They'll be kneeling round the well with their threads, the way they are in Ilse Marrow's engraving. I still them. All of them. All at once." He took a breath. "Then I open the silver door from inside, and you come in."

"*Still* them," said Hollis faintly. "The whole Inner Ledger. In one working. That's—Thane, that's the ballroom again. That's five years. More."

"Yes," said Severin.

"You've got *eight*—"

"I know what I've got." He looked at Hollis. Then, slowly, round the whole room. At all of them. "And Tamsin will be on the other end of my thread. In silver. Beside her mother, with the well coming up through her. She'll feed me. Down the thread, while I hold it. As much as I need." He said it plainly, out loud, in front of everyone, the way he'd never said anything in his life. "Ours. That's the arrangement."

Nobody said anything. In the corner, on her heap of sacks, Bry was looking at him with an expression he'd never seen on her face, and couldn't read, and didn't try to.

---

The rest, they went through twice.

Bry and Magpie: Kit. Durrow would have him, in the chamber, close to the Keeper. When the door opened, they'd go for Durrow. Pell would go with them. Pell had insisted. Pell had sat up on his sacks and said, in his slow gentle voice, "I'm coming. I'm the biggest," and Bry had opened her mouth to argue, and closed it again, and said, "Fine. Thumb outside."

Hollis: in the procession. Penhallow had a seat on the Council; Penhallow had been invited to witness. Hollis would walk down the stair with the Long-Lived in plum velvet with his mother's smelling salts in his pocket and stand in the ring and wait. "And do what, exactly?" he'd said. And Severin had said, "Whatever you can see needs doing. You'll see it before any of us." And Hollis had gone pink to the ears and not said anything else.

Marr: on the stair. When it started, down.

And the two cuts.

"The Ledger thread on Tamsin's wrist," said Severin. "Hers, to my grandmother. I cut it. In the slack. When my grandmother's palm is on the well's heart and she can't draw or cast for one blink, the way Ismay Lisle told Tamsin on the barge. If I cut it then, she can't drink Tamsin, and she can't drink the boy through her, and she can't reach the well through her." He swallowed. "Left-handed."

"And the Stay," said Vadra.

The room went quiet.

"And the Stay," said Lotte Varga, from her crate.

Everyone turned.

She was sitting with her big raw hands on her knees, the way she always sat, and she didn't look at anyone except Severin.

"I cut the Stay," said Lotte. "In the slack of the well's heart. That's my job. That's decided."

"It's not decided," said Severin.

"It's decided," said Lotte. "I decided it. In Gannet's back room, with the boy reading the Charter. You saw me decide it. You've been pretending you didn't for four days."

"*Paid in full.*" His voice cracked. He didn't care who heard it. "You read it. Whatever it costs, the hand that cuts it pays. All of it. You'll—"

"I'll pay it," said Lotte Varga. "Yes. That's what *paid in full* means, Thane. You're the one who can read."

He opened his mouth.

And the thread at his wrist warmed. A breath of time, coming down it from across the river and up the Gilt and behind a wall of new brick, and a voice inside it, quiet, the way she spoke when she'd been listening for a long time and had decided something.

*It's her choice, Severin.*

He went still.

*Like it was yours,* said Tamsin. *On the Dial. And in the ballroom. And on the roof of Gannet's with a shed full of laundresses. Nobody asked you. You just chose.* A pause. *Let her choose.*

He stood in the middle of the derelict chandlery with his splinted hand against his chest and Kit's watch ticking in his pocket and twenty people watching his face, and he couldn't speak.

Lotte was watching him. She'd seen his face change. She knew, he thought, exactly who'd spoken to him and exactly what she'd said.

"Your girl's got sense," said Lotte Varga. "More than you."

He looked at her for a long time. Then, slowly, he nodded.

---

They went, after that. One by one, into the dark, to sleep for a few hours on sacks and crates in the cellar of a derelict chandlery, before the last day.

Vadra stopped him at the trapdoor.

She'd been at the boarded window all evening, the way she always was, saying almost nothing. Now she stood in front of him at the top of the cellar ladder with her sleeves down over her knuckles and her long severe face pale in the light of the dying stove.

"Thane," she said. Very low. So that nobody else could hear.

He waited.

"When the hand starts walking," said Magister Ines Vadra, "don't let anyone stop it."

He looked at her.

"Not your girl. Not the Lisles. Not the Council. Not your own heart, when you see what it costs." Her eyes held his. "Whatever happens down there. Whatever it costs. Whoever it costs." A pause. "Don't let anyone tie it again."

She didn't wait for an answer. She went past him down the ladder into the dark, and he stood at the top of it for a long time, alone, with the stove going out and Kit's watch ticking in his pocket and a narrow stub of vellum, no wider than a fingernail, still caught in the silver stitching of the Founders' Charter that lay wrapped in oilcloth inside his coat.
