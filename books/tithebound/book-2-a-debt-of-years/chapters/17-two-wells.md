# Chapter Seventeen

## Two Wells

::: {.pov}
Tamsin
:::

She took me down that night.

Not in the morning. Not at the Turning. That night, at one in the morning, in a closed black carriage with the curtains drawn, down the Gilt Road and through the dark sleeping Wick and across the Hours Bridge, with its twenty-four lamps going by the window one by one like the beads of a rosary. She sat across from me in the carriage in a fur thrown over her grey wrapper, with the silver box in her lap, eating violets, not speaking. Two of Durrow's men rode on the box. Evadne she had sent to her rooms, with a guard on the door.

Corran, she'd sent somewhere else. I didn't know where. I'd looked back at him once, on the stair, standing very straight in the kitchen doorway with his scarred hand at his side, and he'd looked back at me, and I'd seen in his face that he knew exactly what he'd done and would do it again.

"He'll be dealt with," Lady Aurelia had said, when I asked. And then nothing else.

---

The Meridian was dark. Closed for the spring recess, the wrights gone home to the Gilt, the tithes gone home to the Wick. The court was empty under the clock. Snow lay in the corners of the flagstones. Nobody was there but a single warden at the gatehouse, who bowed so low when he saw who was in the carriage that his hat fell off.

She took me in through the narrow door at the base of the Horologe that I'd walked past for a whole term and never noticed. Down the stair. Red carpet. Silver sconces shaped like clock hands. The carved clock faces all the way down the walls, every hand on eleven. I counted. Three hundred and four steps. I'd counted them once before, on the Dial day, going down behind her with my sleeve rolled up and my count bare.

The passage. The warmth. The singing.

The silver door.

She laid her palm on it, and it parted like water, and the singing stopped.

---

They bound me beside her.

Two binders, in Ledger grey, who had come down ahead of us and were waiting in the round room with their velvet cushions and their silver needles. They didn't look at me. They didn't look at her. They worked quickly and quietly, the way a binder works at a Tithe Market, without warning: a silver thread through the skin of my left wrist, and my right, and around, and up, into the dark above the pendulum, to wherever all the other threads went. Not to the Stay; I saw that much. Not to the braided cord that rose from the ring at my mother's throat up the shaft into the dark. Just up. To the works. To hold me.

It hurt. Like being sewn shut. I didn't make a sound.

When it was done, I was kneeling on the black stone beside her, in the pool of light that ran off her arms, with my own arms held out and up at my sides by the threads. The way hers were held. The way the woman in Ilse Marrow's engraving was held. Like someone about to catch a child.

"There," said Lady Aurelia Thane.

She stood at the edge of the pool of light, with the binders behind her and the parted silver door behind them, and looked at us. At the two of us, kneeling side by side under the hanging pendulum. She looked for a long time.

"One night," she said. "To help you think about my offer, dear. A taste of the Turning." She smiled. "I'll come down for you in the morning. If you've thought hard enough, you can walk back up the stair beside me. If you haven't—" She tilted her head. "Well. You'll have had a rehearsal."

She turned to go. At the silver door, she stopped.

"Don't stay up too late talking," she said. "I know you will. Your mother can talk to you without a word, I'd imagine; the Marrow women always could, my mother said." Her dimples came out. "But you'll find you can't do anything else. The threads see to that. And the clock's listening, dear. It always is."

The silver door closed behind her. The singing began again on the other side of it, high and sweet and endless, like a fingertip on the rim of a glass.

And we were alone.

---

For a long time, neither of us said anything.

I couldn't turn my body. The threads held my arms out and up, and when I tried to turn, they pulled, and the pulling went up into the dark, into the works, and I felt something up there tighten, a long way above. But I could turn my head. So I turned it, and looked at her.

She was already looking at me.

Up close, in her own light, she was so thin. That was what I hadn't been able to see on the Dial day, in the minutes I'd had, kneeling with my face in her neck. Her cheeks had fallen in under the bones. Her wrists, in the silver threads, were like a bird's. Her hair was more grey than dark now, long, down past her shoulders, falling forward over the pale shift she wore, the only thing she wore. And her arms. From her fingertips to her shoulders. Light. No lines, no rungs, no ladder. Just one continuous blaze of gold, so bright up close that it hurt to look at, spilling off her onto the stone in a pool that soaked through my wrapper at the knees, warm as bathwater.

And her eyes. Dark. Too big for her face.

Kit's eyes. Mine.

"You've cut your hair," she said.

Out loud. Her voice cracked and dry, like a door that hasn't been opened for a long time, but out loud. Not down the thread. In the air between us.

"I cut it myself," I said. "Every spring. With the kitchen scissors."

"It shows."

I laughed. It came out of me so suddenly that it hurt, in my chest, where the threads pulled. She laughed too, a thin cracked sound, and then we were both laughing, kneeling side by side under a pendulum the size of a cartwheel with our arms held out in silver, laughing until the tears ran down my face and I couldn't wipe them away because my hands were held up in the air.

She leaned sideways. As far as the threads would let her. Her shoulder touched mine.

I put my head on it.

---

We talked all night.

I don't know how long it was. There was no way to tell time down there; the only clock was the one above us, and it didn't move. But it was a long time. Long enough for me to tell her everything I hadn't been able to say down the thread a few words at a time. Long enough for her to tell me some of what she'd never said at all.

Kit first. Always Kit first. I told her everything. His maps. His lungs. The night they filled worst, two winters ago, and the physic in Saltmarket and the three years I'd sold and thrown up in the yard. The watch. The green sea. *I checked.*

She laughed so hard at *I checked* that the threads on her wrists sang.

"He gets that from me," she said. "Your father would have just believed it was green. Your father believed everything."

I'd never heard her say *your father*. Not once. Not in nine years on Tallow Lane.

"Who was he?" I said. "My father. Nobody ever—you never—"

She was quiet for a moment.

"Tobiah Fenn," she said. "He lit the lamps on the Hours Bridge. All twenty-four. Every evening at dusk, with a long pole with a wick on the end, and every morning at dawn he went back and put them out again." Her voice went soft. "He used to say he was the only man in Lanthorn who got to see the whole river every day, twice. He'd come home and tell me the colors." A pause. "He went into the river the winter you were three. Really went in. The ice on the bridge. He slipped." Her shoulder moved against my cheek. "You used to sit at the window at dusk and watch the lamps come on and wait for him. For a year. Every night."

I didn't remember. I didn't remember any of it. I sat with my head on her shoulder and thought about a man with a long pole lighting twenty-four lamps on a bridge, and I'd walked across that bridge a hundred times, I'd run across it in the fog with Severin's hand clamped over my mouth, and I'd never known.

"And Kit's?"

"Kit's father," said my mother, "was a good man for a little while." A pause. "A sailor. He didn't stay. They don't."

---

She told me about the night Kit was born.

I'd told her, down the thread, that I didn't remember standing in the doorway shouting at the midwife. She told me I had. Nine years old, in my nightgown, with my hair standing up, and the midwife from Candle Row who was drunk, and I'd stood there with my fists clenched and told her she had the baby turned wrong and she was going to kill us all.

"And you were right," said my mother. "She did have him turned wrong. She looked at you like you'd sprouted a second head. And then she turned him." A pause. "He came out blue. With eleven years on his arm. And you took him off her before I could and held him up to the window to look at his count, the way I'd taught you, and you said, *Mama, he's short*." Her voice went strange. "And then you said, *That's all right. I'm long. I'll share.*"

I couldn't speak.

"Nine years old," said my mother. "You said *I'll share.*" Her shoulder moved against my cheek. "That's when I knew I'd tie you first. If they came. That night. I thought, she'll share. Whatever I give her, she'll share it with him. And she did. Didn't you?"

*I've sold fifteen years.* For his lungs. For coal. For the physic in Saltmarket.

"Yes," I said.

"Then I chose right," said my mother.

---

Later, much later, she said: "Take it."

I lifted my head off her shoulder.

"Her offer." My mother wasn't looking at me. She was looking straight ahead, into the dark beyond the pool of light, at the closed silver door. "When she comes down in the morning. Say yes. Give your blood to the knot, willing, and walk back up that stair beside her."

"*Mama*—"

"Listen to me." Her voice was thin and hard and cracked. "She'll make you her heir. You'll never get old. You'll never die. You can give Kit a hundred years. You can give your boy with the clocks seventy-three, all of it, every line he was born with." She swallowed. "And you'll be free. *You'll be out.* Not down here. Not like this."

"And you?"

"I've been here ten years," said my mother. "I can do a hundred more."

"No."

"Tamsin—"

"*No.*" I'd have stood up, if I could. I'd have walked across the room and shaken her. I was kneeling in silver with my arms held over my head, and the threads pulled and sang when I moved, and I didn't care. "I didn't spend ten years being kept so I could walk up those stairs and leave you here. I'm not leaving you here."

"And I didn't spend ten years keeping you," said my mother, "so you could kneel down here beside me!"

It rang off the black stone. Off the pendulum. Up the shaft into the dark.

We knelt there, side by side, breathing hard, in the pool of light, not looking at each other.

"You don't get to decide that," I said at last. Very quietly. "For me. Nobody does. Not anymore."

A long silence.

"Stubborn," said my mother. Her voice had gone rough. "Like your father. He'd have walked into the river after me if he'd known where I'd gone." A pause. "He'd have got here faster than you did."

"He'd have *lit* it," I said. "All twenty-four lamps, all the way down."

She made a sound. Not a laugh. Something under one.

Then she leaned over again, as far as the threads would let her, and put her cheek against the top of my head, and left it there.

---

I don't know when it started.

I didn't do it on purpose. Neither did she. I'm almost sure of that. We were just kneeling there, in the dark, close, with her cheek on my hair, and I was crying again without any noise, and somewhere in me, the part that knows my own count, reached for her. The way you'd reach for someone's hand in the dark without thinking. Down the invisible thread, the deepest one, the one she'd tied on the end of my bed when I was nine.

And something went.

Not years. Not a flood. A warmth. Out of me, into her. The way you'd hold a cold child against you.

And from her, back. Up from the well, up through her, into me. Warm. Slow. The way it had come every morning of my life.

And from me, back to her.

Back and forth. Like a breath. Like two people breathing together in the dark, in and out, the same air. I could feel it moving between us, in the thread, through our hearts. Hers slow and deep. Mine faster. Coming closer together, beat by beat, the way they'd come together through the silver door on the night of the Echo, until they were beating at the same moment, the same beat, the same instant.

Above us, the pendulum twitched.

I felt it more than saw it. A shiver in the dark over our heads, in the great black iron disc, the way a sleeping dog's leg twitches when it dreams. The threads on our wrists sang, all of them, all the way up into the dark.

"Mama," I said. "Mama, the clock—"

"I know," she said. Her cheek was still on my hair. "I know. Let it."

We didn't stop. I don't think we could have.

---

The tick came at dawn.

There was no dawn down there. But I knew it was dawn, afterward, because of the binder who came running.

It started in the stone. A grinding, deep and huge, coming down the shaft from somewhere so high above us that it took a long time to arrive. Then the threads: every silver thread in the chamber, the ones on our wrists and the ones rising from the ring at my mother's throat and the ones running out sideways through the walls, all of them at once, humming, a low enormous chord like the strings of a harp the size of the tower being swept by a hand. Then the pendulum. It swung. Out, over our heads, a long low arc through the dark, with a sound like a ship's keel grinding over stone. And back.

And far above us, in the works, a tooth went forward, and caught.

*Tick.*

It came down through the tower and the rock and the floor and my knees and my bones and my mother's cheek on my hair. The whole round room rang with it.

My mother laughed. Softly, against my hair. The way she'd laughed at *I checked*.

The silver door parted.

---

It wasn't her, at first. It was one of the binders, the younger one, running, in his Ledger grey with his velvet cushion gone and his face white. He stopped at the edge of the pool of light and stared at us.

Then she came in behind him.

Lady Aurelia Thane. Dressed this time. Black lace, the silver box in her hand, her hair up, her count blazing through her sleeves. She came through the silver door and stopped beside the binder, and looked at us, kneeling side by side in the pool of light with my mother's cheek on my head and both our faces wet.

"My lady," said the binder. His voice shook. "The hand. I came down the stair from the court, I saw it as I came in. It's moved."

She didn't look at him.

"Three minutes past," said the binder. "Three minutes past eleven, my lady."

The Keeper of the Ledger stood at the edge of the light with her silver box in her hand and looked at the two of us for a long, long time. At my mother. At me. At the threads on our wrists, still humming faintly, settling.

Then she lifted her face and looked up the shaft into the dark, toward the works, toward the hand she couldn't see.

Her face did nothing at all. Not fear. Not anger. Nothing. It was the most frightening thing I'd ever seen it do.

"Interesting," said the Keeper.
