# Chapter Twenty-Nine

## Page Eleven

::: {.pov}
Severin
:::

The Gilt was smoking.

He saw it from the river wall at the bottom of the Sump on the second morning after, standing among the laundresses in the cold with the fog lifting off the water. You could see all the way up the white terraces from there, the way you could see them from the bottom of Broker's Row on a clear day, terrace on terrace, house on house, up to the black tooth of Thane House at the top.

And over all of it, a haze.

Gold. Faint. Shining. Like heat coming off a road in summer, except it wasn't summer and it wasn't heat. It rose off the white terraces in the cold morning in a thin glittering veil, from every garden and every terrace and every shut window, and drifted up into the sky, and away. North. Across the river. Toward the island. Toward the clock.

Every Long-Lived arm in Lanthorn, smoking at once.

*Every hour that was ever bought goes home.*

Above the island, the great pale face of the Horologe hung over the river with its single iron hand. It had moved again at dawn, the way it had moved at dawn yesterday, a long grinding inch with every bell in the city ringing after it.

Six minutes past eleven.

---

They buried Lotte Varga in the river.

Not buried. The Sump didn't bury. The ground on the river flats was too wet; anything you put in it came up again in the spring floods. The Sump gave its dead to the Sallow, the way it always had, the way it had before the Founders and the Gilt and the clock. Wrapped in white linen and carried down to the water by the people who'd loved them, and laid on the tide at the turn, and let go.

The laundresses carried her.

Forty of them. In their aprons, with their sleeves rolled and their raw red arms bare in the cold, from the third shed and the second and the fourth and every steam-house on the flats. They'd washed her themselves. They'd wrapped her in a sheet she'd washed herself, they said; they'd found it folded on the pile beside her basket, mended with big stitches, the last thing she'd mended. They carried her down from the steam-house on their shoulders through the fog, and they sang.

No words. Just sound, rising and falling, the way water rises and falls.

He walked behind them. They'd let him take a corner, at the end, at the river wall, for the last few steps down the stone ramp to the water. One of the women had stepped aside without a word and he'd taken her place, and Lotte Varga had come down onto his shoulder through the wet linen, a laundress's weight, thirty years of hauling sheets on poles, and he'd carried her.

Tamsin walked beside him. She'd come down from the Meridian at dawn, through the cisterns and the Hall and over the bridge, with her mother asleep in Rook's bed and Kit asleep beside her. She didn't say anything. She walked beside him with her hand on his back between his shoulder blades, the whole way, and he felt her heart through the thread, steady, steady.

At the water, they laid Lotte down.

The tide was on the turn. The river slid past the bottom of the ramp, brown and cold and full of old ice, going out. The women set her on it, in her white sheet, gently, the way you'd set a sleeping child in a cot. And stood back.

Nobody spoke. There was no priest. The Sump didn't have priests, any more than it had graves. There was only the singing, going on, rising and falling, and the river.

And then somebody stepped forward.

A big man. A dock porter by the shoulders, forty, with a short Wick count and a face like a fist that had stopped giving up. Abel. He stood at the edge of the water above the white shape on the tide and didn't say anything. He just pushed up his left sleeve to the elbow, and held his arm up. Bare. A dozen pale scars of old threads round the wrist. And no black thread. Nothing at all.

Then a girl of fifteen beside him did the same. The one with the loaf of bread, from the night of the raid.

Then an old man. The one with the debt forty years old, who'd paid it off three times.

Then Magpie, at the back, small and quick, with her sleeve cut off at the elbow already, holding up her bare wrist.

Then dozens.

He turned and looked back up the stone ramp, and the river wall above it, and it was full. People he didn't know. Wick men and women in coats and shawls and nightshirts, who'd come down through the fog from every court in the Sump and Saltmarket and Candle Row, standing along the wall in a long line, holding up their left arms. Bare. With the white scars of old threads round their wrists. And no thread.

Twenty years of cut threads. One at a time. After dark, through the river door at the back of a shed, whenever she felt like it.

The tide took her.

She went out slowly, on the brown water, in her white sheet, turning a little in the current, toward the Hours Bridge and the island and the clock. He watched her go until the fog took her. All along the river wall, the arms stayed up until she was gone.

In his pocket, the little watch ticked.

---

His mother was waiting at the top of the ramp.

She'd come down with Tamsin, in her plain grey dress, with a shawl someone had found her over her shoulders, and stood at the river wall with the Wick through the whole of it, a little apart, not singing, not holding up her arm. When the women came back up the ramp she stepped aside to let them pass, and they looked at her, some of them, and knew who she was, and nodded.

He stopped in front of her.

Tamsin's hand left his back. He felt her go, a few steps, up the ramp, to give them the space. He didn't turn round. He looked at his mother.

They'd hardly spoken. Two days, and they'd hardly said a word to each other. In the Tithe Hall, with the well asleep in Rook's bed and Lotte under a blanket on the next one, she'd sat beside him on the floor by the iron door for a whole night without saying anything, with her hand on his shoulder, and in the morning she'd got up and gone to help Rook with the fire. He didn't know how to talk to her. He didn't think she knew how to talk to him. Fourteen years.

"I gave you to her," said Evadne Thane.

He didn't say anything.

"After Aldric. I gave you to her." Her voice was very steady. "I told myself it was to keep you alive. It was. But it was also because I was afraid. Of her. Of the dark. Of everything." She looked at the river. "Aldric showed me the way out, the night before. *Take the boy and go, Eve.* And I didn't. I sat in the music room and played three notes and let her have you."

"I know why," he said.

She looked at him.

"Tamsin told me. In the Violet Rooms." He swallowed. "You thought if I was hers, she wouldn't do to me what she did to him."

"And she did it anyway. Differently. To a girl, with your hands." Her voice cracked, very slightly, for the first time. "I should have taken you through the tunnels. Like he said. I should have taken you to the Wick and raised you over a chandlery and let you grow up short and happy and never once see the long gallery."

"You were afraid of the dark."

"I still am."

"So am I," said Severin.

It came out of him the way it had come out on Gannet's roof to Hollis, unplanned, in the cold air. He didn't take it back.

His mother looked up at him for a long time. Her eyes, his eyes, the grey of tallow ink, were wet. Then something moved in her face, slowly, the ghost of something he remembered from before he was seven, from the music room, from a woman sitting on the bench beside his father at the harpsichord, laughing.

He held out his arm. His left. With his father's ring on it.

She took it.

They walked back up the ramp together, slowly, the way you'd walk with someone who's been ill for a long time and is learning to walk again. He didn't know which of them that was.

---

Magister Vadra was at the chandlery.

At the boarded window, the way she always was, with her sleeves down over her knuckles again and her long severe face pale in the grey light through the cracks in the boards. The others were out. Hob had gone to the Watch House to stand with Marr while Marr told the Seven Houses, or what was left of them, that the Ledger was suspended until further notice and Sergeant Kell Durrow would hang. Bry and Pell had gone to Tallow Lane with a cart, to make up the two rooms over the chandlery for the well and her children. Gannet had gone with them, complaining. Orrin had come down from the tower at noon on the first day with his rope coiled over his shoulder and the empty bosun's chair under his arm and his face grey, and sat on the floor of the Tithe Hall with his back against the wall beside Severin and not said anything for an hour, and then gone to find a boat.

It was just the two of them.

He closed the door behind him.

"Page eleven," he said.

She didn't turn round from the window.

"I saw you see me," said Magister Vadra. "In Gannet's back room. The night Wat read it. You looked up from the stub in the stitching and I was standing at the window, and you looked at me, and I looked at you." A pause. "I wondered how long you'd wait."

"Until she was free," said Severin. "And the hand was walking. And it was too late for anyone to tie it again."

Vadra turned round.

She looked at him for a long time across the dim derelict room, with the cold stove and the coils of Orrin's rope on the floor and Kit's three singed maps still weighted at the corners with bits of brick. Then she reached into the long sleeve of her green gown, up past the knuckles, past the wrist, and took out a narrow folded strip of old vellum, brown at the edges, soft as cloth, warm from being held against her arm, and held it out to him.

"I never let it leave my skin," she said. "Not once. Not in a fortnight."

He took it.

---

It was short. One leaf, written on one side in the same faded court hand as the rest of the Charter, in the same weak-tea ink. A numeral in the bottom corner. *Eleven.*

He read it standing by the cold stove, with Vadra watching him.

*And at the Settlement,* it said, *every hour that hath been moved shall return to whence it came: the bought unto the seller, or unto the seller's blood; the stolen unto the robbed; the given unto the giver; and the spent unto none. And the Allotment shall be made anew.*

He read it again.

*The given unto the giver.*

He stood very still.

He thought of a ten-year-old boy in a coat too big for him, at a long table in a cellar, with a tin watch. *Thirty-one years. Thirty of them Grace.* Given. On a Tithe Day. By the Thane heir. Out of House Thane's reserves, into a child's arm, by a Ledger binder with a silver needle, the safe way.

*The given unto the giver.*

Thirty years. Going home. Back to House Thane. Back to the giver. Half a year with every minute the hand walked. And when it struck twelve, a boy with lungs like wet paper, who'd been born with eleven years and had lived ten of them, would have what was his.

A year. Less.

He thought of Agathe Rook, in the firelight of the Tithe Hall, with gold smoke rising off her arm, pulling her sleeve down over it.

He thought of Magister Vadra herself, standing in front of him, with thirty-one years on her arm that a dead man named Aurel Vey had given her when he married his tithe.

And he thought of himself.

He didn't need to look at his arm. He knew exactly what was on it. Twelve lines and a little, after the round room. And he knew exactly where they'd come from. Nine on Tithe Day, his own, what was left of seventy-three after five years of burning. And then, again and again, the years a girl had poured into him: three in a ballroom, five in his sleep, a slow tide on the floor of the Violet Rooms, a river in the round room while his grandmother drank him through her fingers. More given than he had left. Far more.

*The given unto the giver. And the spent unto none.*

Every year she'd ever given him would go back to her. And every year he'd burned would go nowhere. And at midnight, when the clock struck twelve, he'd have what was his.

He did the sum, the way she'd taught him. The way he'd taught her, at a velvet table, with chalk.

It didn't come to very much.

---

"Now you know why I hid it," said Vadra quietly.

He looked up.

"The boy," she said. "And Rook. And me. And you." She held his eyes. "The Wick gets back every year it ever sold. The Penhallow boy gets back what his ancestor threw away on a horse. The Kettle girl gets back the fifty-six years Lysander Crane stole from her. Every Wick child born from now on gets the Allotment the first well meant them to have. That's what midnight is, Thane. That's what it's always been." Her voice didn't change. "And everyone living on given years dies."

He didn't say anything.

"She'd tie it again," said Vadra. "Your girl. If she knew. She'd walk back down that stair of her own will and kneel beside her mother and give her blood to the knot, to save the boy. And to save you." She paused. "Wouldn't she?"

He thought of a voice in the dark, in his wrist, three nights ago. *If it goes wrong, I'll give it to her. I already decided.*

"Yes," he said.

"And would you let her?"

He didn't answer.

"That's why I hid it," said Magister Vadra. "So that nobody would have to answer that. So the hand would walk, and nobody would know what it cost until it was too late to stop it." Her long severe face was very pale. "Thirty years I've waited for that hand to move. Thirty years, since I inked for Aurel Vey in a Hall under a river. I decided. For everyone. Somebody had to."

"No," said Severin.

She looked at him.

"Not anymore." He folded page eleven in half, and in half again, and put it inside his coat, next to the little watch. "Nobody decides for everyone. Not you. Not my grandmother. Not me." He went to the door. "I did it all my life. In a tower. Alone. I decided who got bought and who got left and who got told and when. And a girl from Tallow Lane stood at a velvet table and told me to stop." He put his hand on the latch. "So I'm going to go and tell her. Now. All of it. And then we'll decide. Together."

"And if she chooses to tie it?"

He stood with his hand on the latch for a long moment.

"Then it'll be hers to choose," he said. "And I'll be standing next to her when she does."

He opened the door and went out.

---

He walked up through the Wick in the grey afternoon with page eleven in his coat.

The Wick was in the street. It had been in the street for two days and showed no sign of going home. Not watching the clock this time. Not afraid. Something else. On every corner, at every door, people with their sleeves rolled up, looking at their arms. Holding them out to each other. Laughing, some of them. Crying, some of them. An old woman on the step of a house on Candle Row with her arm held up to the light and three of her neighbors crowded round her, all of them staring at a short ladder of gold lines on her thin forearm, and one new line at the top of it, faint, coming in, the way a line comes in when it's been sold and is coming home.

"*Forty years,*" the old woman was saying, to nobody, to everybody. "*I sold it forty years ago for my wedding dress.*"

At the corner of the bridge, the man with no buttons had been carried up onto somebody's shoulders, and was being paraded up and down Broker's Row, still shouting.

He turned into Tallow Lane.

It was narrow and cobbled and smelled of tallow and river and coal smoke, and at the far end of it, above a chandler's shop with a dog asleep in the doorway, there were two rooms with a window that stuck. He'd been there once, on a night with fog, in a Wick coat, carrying a tin watch in his pocket. He knew the window. There was a lamp lit in it now.

He walked up the lane toward it with page eleven against his ribs, and above the roofs behind him, over the island, the clock said six minutes past eleven.
