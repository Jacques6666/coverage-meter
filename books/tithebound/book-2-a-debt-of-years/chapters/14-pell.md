# Chapter Fourteen

## Pell

::: {.pov}
Tamsin
:::

I didn't sleep.

I sat at the window of the Violet Rooms all night with my left wrist held in my right hand and the curtain pulled back an inch, so I could see the clock. Two minutes past eleven. It didn't move. Below it, the Gilt was dark. The Wick, darker. Somewhere down there, in a cellar under a pawnshop on Broker's Row, Severin was sitting at a long table in the lamplight with the Founders' Charter in front of him and his right hand wrapped in a strip of Gannet's shawl. I'd felt the bones in it go when he hit Corwin Lisle. I'd felt every rung of the rope up the light-well. I'd felt his heart at the top, on the roof, when I told him.

He'd sent me one more word, after. Just before midnight. An hour.

*Sorry.*

I didn't know what he was sorry for. For Pell. For the hand. For being alive when somebody who carried trays and baked lemon cakes and sang hymns for dead girls was in a Greycoat's cell somewhere, or worse. I didn't answer. I couldn't think of anything to say that would be worth an hour of his.

Bry talked all night. Down the black thread, in a rush, not rationing, hour after hour of her eighteen years. *They took him out the back in a cart. I saw it from the stable yard. Hollis held me. I'd have gone back in. He held me round the waist and I hit him and he didn't let go.* And later: *Durrow was laughing. He had his foot on Pell's back in the cart. He was laughing.* And later still, near dawn, very quietly, so quietly it was barely a breath in my wrist: *I should have held the door. Not him. I'm the fighter. I should have held it.*

*He wouldn't have let you,* I sent back.

A long pause.

*No,* said Bry. *No. He wouldn't.*

---

At the seventh bell, Nessa didn't come.

A footman came instead. One I didn't know, in Thane black and silver, with a face like a closed door. He stood in the doorway of the Violet Rooms and said, "Lady Aurelia's compliments, miss, and you're wanted in the Glass Room," and didn't wait for me to dress. I went in the grey wrapper, barefoot, with my hair down, because I knew. I'd known since Durrow laughed in a cart in a stable yard.

The Glass Room was full of morning. Sun pouring through the curved panes, through the violets, across the white tablecloth and the silver and the toast rack and the honey. The clock across the river in the window, pale in the sunlight. Two minutes past.

Lady Aurelia was at the table. In grey lace this morning, with the silver box at her elbow. Buttering toast.

And on the tiles in front of the table, on his knees, between two men in grey coats, was Pell.

---

They'd beaten him. That was the first thing. His gentle face was swollen out of its shape on one side, the eye closed, a cut through the eyebrow crusted black. His hands were tied behind him with cord. His grey Ledger House waiter's jacket, the one they'd hired him in, was torn half off one shoulder. He was kneeling very straight, the way he always sat on the end of his bed in the Tithe Hall, carefully, as though he were afraid the floor might break.

His left sleeve had been cut away to the shoulder. His count was bare.

Forty-nine years. And a bit.

Behind him, by the door, with his thumbs in his belt and his red face shining, stood Sergeant Kell Durrow.

"Ah," said Lady Aurelia, looking up. "There you are, dear. Sit down."

I didn't sit down.

Pell's good eye found me. It went over my face, quickly, and then away, to the floor, to the tiles in front of his knees, and stayed there. He didn't say anything. He didn't make any sign that he knew me at all.

"Someone stole my Charter yesterday," said Lady Aurelia pleasantly. She put down the butter knife. "From the vault under the Ledger House. On Council day. With the Seven Houses sitting in the chamber over their heads." She took a bite of toast, chewed, swallowed. "It's never happened before. Not in three hundred years. I'm told the Lisle boy was involved, which doesn't surprise me in the least; Ismay's been trying for a century. And I'm told my great-great-great-grandson was involved, which doesn't surprise me either." She dabbed her lips with her napkin. "And this large gentle person was carrying trays for the Ledger House, and then, at the crucial moment, he wasn't. He was holding a door."

She looked at me.

"Do you know him, dear?"

"No," I said.

Pell's good eye didn't lift from the floor.

"No?" Lady Aurelia tilted her head. "How odd. He was a Penhallow tithe. In your Hall. At the Meridian, all last term. Pell Darrow, of the Sump. Forty-nine years, and a little." She smiled. "I'd have thought you'd know everybody in the Tithe Hall."

"I didn't talk to many of them."

"Mm," said the Keeper of the Ledger.

She held out her hand. Not to me.

---

Durrow's men brought Pell forward on his knees across the tiles, one at each arm, until he was kneeling beside the table, beside her chair, with his bare left arm held out over the white cloth between the toast rack and the honey. The way mine was held out every morning.

She took his wrist in her small cool fingers.

"Where is my Charter, dear?" she said. To me.

"I don't know."

"Don't you." She closed her eyes.

I felt it. Not through my thread; she wasn't drawing from me. I felt it the way you feel a cold draft in a room, the way I'd felt Lysander's hastening drag me through Thane House on the Long Night. A pull. Huge and smooth and easy, and Pell's head came up off his chest, and his mouth opened, and he made a sound I'd never heard a person make.

A year. One line of gold at the top of his count, brown at the ends, grey, gone. Into her.

She opened her eyes.

"Where is it?"

"I *don't know*," I said. "I don't. I swear. I don't know where they took it."

That was true. I knew who had it. I knew he was sitting at a long table in a cellar on Broker's Row with it in front of him. But I didn't know where the cellar was. Not exactly. Not the door. Not the street. I'd never let Bry tell me, and I'd never let him tell me, and now I understood, standing barefoot on the tiles of the Glass Room, why he'd never offered.

She closed her eyes again.

Another year.

And Pell began to sing.

---

Softly. Under his breath, at first, so I almost didn't hear it. Then louder. That big gentle voice, cracked now, catching on the swollen side of his mouth, filling the Glass Room the way it had filled the vaulted Tithe Hall on the night of the vigil, with the two rows of tallow stubs burning on the stone floor.

*The hymn about the lamb.*

Every verse. He knew all the words. Anni had never known more than the tune; she'd hummed it under her breath whenever she was frightened, which was always. Pell had learned the words, all of them, after she died. I'd never asked him why. I knew why.

Lady Aurelia opened her eyes and looked at him. She looked genuinely puzzled, for a moment, the way you'd look at a dog that had begun to recite poetry.

Then she smiled, and closed her eyes, and drew again.

Three years. Four.

"*Stop.*"

It came out of me so loud that one of the Greycoats flinched. I was across the tiles. I didn't remember moving. I was standing beside her chair, beside Pell, with my left arm held out over the table, bare, my count blazing in the sunlight, seventy-one years and four months.

"Drink me," I said. "Drink *me*. Take all of it, take a hundred years, I don't care, it comes back, you know it comes back. Leave *him*."

She opened her eyes and looked up at me.

"I drink you every morning, dear," said Lady Aurelia gently. "You're breakfast." She didn't let go of Pell's wrist. "He's a lesson."

Five. Six.

Pell's voice faltered. He swayed on his knees between the two Greycoats. He found the next line of the hymn and went on.

"Where is my Charter, Tamsin?"

"*I don't know—*"

Seven.

---

The door at the end of the Glass Room opened.

Nobody came in at first. It just opened, the tall glass door at the far end that led back into the west wing, and stood open, and a draft came through it and moved all the violets at once, a long shiver of purple down the length of the room.

Then a woman came through it.

Tall, thin, in a plain grey dress, with her hair scraped back and her hands clasped in front of her. She walked down the length of the Glass Room between the orange trees and the violets, past the Greycoats, past Durrow by the door, and nobody stopped her, because nobody in that room, I think, had ever seen her walk into one before.

Evadne Thane stopped beside the table.

She didn't look at me. She didn't look at Pell. She looked at the Keeper of the Ledger, who was sitting with her small hand still around Pell's wrist and an expression on her round sweet face of absolute, delighted astonishment.

"Enough, Grandmother," said Evadne Thane.

Her voice was very quiet. It shook. It didn't stop.

Lady Aurelia laughed.

"Evadne," she said. "You've remembered how to talk."

"He's a boy who carries trays." Evadne's hands were clasped so tightly in front of her that her knuckles were white. "You've made your point. Whatever you want from the girl, you won't get it by making her watch this. She doesn't know where your Charter is. Look at her face. Anyone could see she doesn't know." A breath. "You've taken seven years from him in the time it takes to butter toast. Let him go."

The Glass Room was perfectly silent. Even Pell had stopped singing.

Lady Aurelia let go of Pell's wrist.

She stood up. She was a head shorter than Evadne; she had to tip her face up to look at her. She looked at her for a long moment, sweetly, with her head on one side, the way she'd looked at Bry in the Refectory and said *Crane's*.

Then she slapped her.

It wasn't a hard slap. It didn't need to be. It was the sound of it, in that glass room full of sunlight: small and flat and final, like a door closing in another part of the house. Evadne's head turned with it. Her pale cheek went red.

She didn't move. She didn't lift her hand to her face. She turned her head back, slowly, and looked down at the Keeper of the Ledger again, and her eyes, his eyes, the grey of tallow ink, were wet and absolutely steady.

"Fourteen years," said Lady Aurelia softly, "and *now* you find your spine. For a tithe." She shook her head. "Aldric would have been so proud."

Evadne said nothing.

The Keeper looked at her a moment longer. Then she turned away, and sat back down, and picked up her cup.

"Sergeant," she said.

Durrow straightened.

"Take him down to the cellars. Alive, please. He'll be more useful alive." She sipped her tea. "And feed him something. He looks like a man who eats."

---

They lifted Pell up off the tiles between them. His legs wouldn't hold him at first. His good eye found me, once, as they turned him toward the door, and there was nothing in it, nothing at all, no sign that he'd ever seen me before in his life. He was protecting me. With seven years gone and his face broken, he was still protecting me.

I looked at his arm as they took him past.

Forty-two years. And a bit.

"He has forty-two left, dear," said Lady Aurelia, behind me. "I'll take one every morning with my tea. Until somebody gives me back my Charter." She set down her cup. "You might mention that, if you happen to speak to anyone. I know you can't. But if you could."

I stood on the tiles in my bare feet and the grey wrapper, in the sunlight, with my arm still held out over the table, and the clock in the window behind me at two minutes past eleven.

"Sit down, Tamsin," said the Keeper. "Your eggs are getting cold."

---

I sat down. I ate the eggs. I don't remember what they tasted like. She held out her hand across the table at the eighth bell and I gave her my wrist and she drank a year from me, the way she did every morning, with her eyes closed. It came back up from below, warm, from across the river and under it.

*Mama,* I sent down the deepest thread, while it came. *Mama, they have Pell.*

*I felt her draw,* said my mother, faint and far. *Seven, all at once. I felt it in the clock. It shivered.* A pause. *Who is he?*

*He bakes,* I sent. *He sings.* And then I couldn't send anything else.

---

Evadne Thane was waiting for me in the long gallery.

I didn't see her at first. It was the hour after breakfast, when the light came in low through the tall west windows and lay across the floor in long gold bars, and the gallery was empty, or I thought it was. Then I passed the pale rectangle on the wall where Severin's portrait had hung, with the hook still in it, and she stepped out of the shadow beside it.

Her cheek was still red.

She didn't stop me. She fell into step beside me, as if we were two ladies taking the air, and walked with me slowly down the long room under two hundred years of faces, past Aldric with his easy smile. Behind us, at a polite distance, Corran the guard. Behind him, Nessa.

"She bricked up the walls," said Evadne, very low, without turning her head. "In the west wing. All of them. I watched the masons yesterday. Every passage Aldric showed the boy." A pause. "Every one *he* showed the boy."

"I know," I said. "Mine too."

"He came through yours." It wasn't a question. Her voice was perfectly steady. "Two nights ago. I heard you laugh, at midnight, through the wall of the music room. I've never heard anybody laugh in the Violet Rooms. Hortense certainly never did."

I didn't say anything. My face was hot.

"The cellars," said Evadne Thane.

I stopped walking.

She didn't. She walked on a step, two, and then turned, in a bar of gold light, and looked at me with her son's eyes.

"They haven't touched the cellars," she said. "The masons didn't go down. Grandmother doesn't think of the cellars. She doesn't think of anything below the ground floor that isn't under the Meridian." She swallowed. "There's a way into them that isn't a door. From the old laundry, behind the copper. I know it's there. It's the only one I ever learned." Her voice cracked, very slightly. "Aldric showed me. The night before he died. He took me down with a candle and showed me, and said, *if anything happens, Eve, take the boy and go*." She looked at the floor between us. "I didn't. I was afraid of the dark."

The light lay across the floor of the long gallery in long gold bars.

"I'm still afraid of the dark," said Evadne Thane. "But I know the way."
