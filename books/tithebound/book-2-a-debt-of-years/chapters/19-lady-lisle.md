# Chapter Nineteen

## Lady Lisle

::: {.pov}
Tamsin
:::

"You're coming to the Regatta," said Lady Aurelia, at breakfast, on the second day of my locked door.

I looked up from my eggs.

"The Thaw Regatta," she said, buttering toast. "On the river. Every year, the first warm day after the ice goes out. The Houses put their barges in at the Gilt landing and row up and down the reach above the Meridian, and drink, and pretend they're young." She took a bite. "I wasn't going to bother this year. But the Gilt is frightened, dear. They've been frightened since the tick. Lord Ostrander's great-uncle fainted in his bath yesterday, watching his arm. They need to see something that isn't frightening."

"Me," I said.

"You. In white. Beside me. With your arm full." She smiled with both dimples. "The well, safe in the Keeper's hand, four days before the Turning. Nothing could be more reassuring."

She held out her hand across the table. I gave her my wrist. She drank.

It came back.

---

The river was full of boats.

I'd seen the Thaw Regatta every spring of my life, from the bottom of Broker's Row, where you could stand on the river wall and look upstream past the Hours Bridge to the Gilt reach and watch the barges go by, tiny and bright with their flags and their awnings, like toys someone had floated on a pond. The whole Wick turned out for it. You stood on the wall with your bread and watched the Gilt enjoy itself, and somebody always said *I hope one of them sinks*, and everybody always laughed.

Up close, nobody was enjoying anything.

The barges were beautiful. That was true. Long and low and lacquered in the Houses' colors, Crane gold and Lisle white and Ostrander blue, each one rowed by twenty Wick oarsmen in livery and hung with silk awnings and lanterns, though it was broad daylight. Musicians on some of them. Tables with white cloths and silver and crystal. The water of the Sallow, brown and cold and full of last week's ice, sliding past underneath.

And on every barge, the Long-Lived, in furs, holding their glasses, laughing too loudly, and looking at their arms.

They did it constantly. They couldn't stop. A woman in white fur on the Lisle barge, mid-laugh, glancing down at her wrist, at her jeweled cuff, pushing it up a fraction with one finger, looking, pushing it down again. A man in Ostrander blue lifting his glass to toast someone across the water, and his eyes going to his own forearm halfway through, and his glass stopping in the air. All of them. All up and down the reach.

Because it was flickering. In the daylight, now. I could see it from Lady Aurelia's barge, across the water, on the bare arms of the oarsmen's masters who'd pushed their cuffs up to look: the gold under their cuffs going dim and bright and dim again, shivering, the way Corisande's had shivered in the Orrery Room, but worse. Patches of it guttering like candles in a draft.

And above all of it, above the barges and the river and the awnings and the music, hanging over the island just downstream like a second moon in the white spring sky, was the clock.

Three minutes past eleven.

Every face on every barge turned toward it, sooner or later. And then away, quickly, the way you'd look away from someone in the street who was staring at you.

---

The Thane barge was black.

Of course it was. Black lacquer, black silk awning, silver at the oars, and twenty Wick oarsmen in black livery pulling us slowly up the middle of the reach so that every other barge could see. Lady Aurelia sat under the awning in black lace with her fur round her shoulders and her silver box in her lap, and I sat beside her in white, as she'd said. White silk, with long sleeves, the left one unbuttoned and folded back to the elbow. My arm full.

Two of Durrow's men sat in the stern. Durrow himself was on the bank somewhere, with more of them; I'd seen his red face at the landing.

The other barges came to us. That was the point, I understood after the first hour. We didn't go to them. We rowed slowly up and down the middle of the reach like a black swan, and one by one the others pulled alongside, and their Long-Lived leaned over the rail and greeted the Keeper, and then looked at me. At my arm. At seventy-one years and four months of gold, steady, in the daylight, not flickering at all.

You could watch them calm down. It was the strangest thing I'd ever seen. They'd come alongside with their faces stretched tight, their cuffs pushed up, their eyes going from their own arms to the clock and back. And then they'd look at mine. Full. Steady. Owned. And their shoulders would come down, and they'd smile, and lift their glasses to the Keeper, and say something about the weather, and row away.

*The well, safe in the Keeper's hand.*

I sat under the black silk with my hands in my lap and let them look.

---

The Ostrander barge came alongside in the second hour, and the old man with the ear trumpet was on it.

Lord Alder Ostrander. Two hundred and nine. Fitch Ostrander's great-great-uncle, who'd bet a whole year on a tithe going below forty once, and had a duel declared void when he lost. He was sitting in a high chair under a blue awning, wrapped in furs to the chin, with his ear trumpet in his lap and a young man in Ostrander blue standing behind him with a hand on his shoulder.

The young man was Fitch.

I hadn't seen him since the morning they found Joss. He looked older. Thinner. He looked at me across the narrow strip of brown water between the barges, and his soft worried face went red and then white, and he looked away.

"Keeper," said Lord Alder, in a high cracked voice. "Keeper. Is it true? What they're saying on the Row?" He lifted his ear trumpet to his ear, as though the answer might be easier to hear that way. "That it's all coming back? At midnight? All of it?"

"Midnight's a long way off, Alder," said Lady Aurelia, pleasantly. "Fifty-seven minutes. And the clock doesn't move unless I let it. At the Turning I'll tie it again, and you'll never think of it twice."

"Fifty-seven minutes," said the old man. "Fifty-seven." He was looking at the clock over her shoulder. His hand had gone to his arm, under the furs. "Do you know how many years I've bought, Keeper? Do you know where they all came from? Do you know what happens to a man like me if they all go home?"

"Uncle," said Fitch, very low.

"I'll tell you what happens," said Lord Alder Ostrander, and his voice rose, high and thin and cracking. "I was born with forty-one. *Forty-one.* I spent them by the time I was thirty, gambling, and I've been buying ever since. A hundred and eighty years of other people's—"

He stopped.

He was looking down at his arm. He'd pushed the furs off it, and the cuff up, and his count was long, very long, two hundred years of gold running past his elbow. And it was going.

Not flickering. *Going.* A whole stretch of it near the top, near his elbow, dimming. Brown at the ends. Grey. Like a row of candles in a gust of wind, ten, twenty, thirty lines, guttering, going dark.

The old man made a thin high sound like a kettle.

And then it came back. All at once. The thirty lines brightened, steadied, held, the way a candle steadies when somebody shuts the door.

But he'd seen it. Everybody on both barges had seen it.

Lord Alder Ostrander's eyes rolled up in his head, and he slid sideways out of his high chair into the furs, and Fitch caught him.

---

They rowed the Ostrander barge away fast, with Fitch kneeling on the deck holding his great-great-uncle's head in his lap and a physic shouting for salts. All up and down the reach, the music had stopped. People were standing at the rails of the other barges, staring. Staring at the Ostrander barge. Then at the clock. Then at their own arms.

Then at me.

"Row on," said Lady Aurelia to the oarsmen. Her voice hadn't changed at all. "Slowly. Up the middle." She ate a violet. "It's a lovely day, isn't it, dear? You can almost smell the spring."

---

The Lisle barge came alongside an hour later, when the music had started again, thinly, and the Long-Lived had gone back to their glasses with their hands not quite steady.

It was white. White lacquer, white silk, white fur on the rail, and in the stern under a white awning, in a white fur of her own with her silver hair cut short as a boy's and her long clever face like a fox's, sat Lady Ismay Lisle.

Her grandson wasn't with her. Corwin. I found myself looking for a white-gloved man with a bruised jaw and was glad I didn't see him.

"Aurelia," said Lady Lisle, across the water. Dry. Low.

"Ismay," said Lady Aurelia. Sweet. Warm.

They looked at each other for a long moment over the strip of brown river between the barges. Two women who'd been looking at each other like that, I thought, for two hundred years.

"The Council wants a word," said Lady Lisle. "About Alder. And about the Row. The Crane steward's on my barge; he's very insistent. Something about his master's nephew and a Dial." Her fox's eyes moved to me, and away. "Come aboard. It won't take long. The Crane man is very tiresome, but I'd rather he were tiresome on my barge than shouting it across the reach."

Lady Aurelia looked at her a moment longer. Then she smiled.

"Of course," she said. And to me, without turning her head: "Stay here, dear. With the men. I won't be a moment."

She rose. A plank was put across from rail to rail. She walked over it lightly in her black lace, as though it were a carpet, and onto the white barge, and under the white awning where a heavy man in Crane gold rose to meet her, red in the face, already talking. I watched her take his hands in hers, sympathetically. I watched him stop talking.

And Lady Ismay Lisle got up from her chair in the stern, and came along her own rail to where it lay against ours, and leaned on it, beside me, with only a strip of brown river and two lacquered rails between us.

"You're wasted on her," she said. "I told you."

---

I didn't say anything. I looked at the river.

"I'm going to make you an offer," said Lady Lisle, very low. "And you're going to listen, because in four days she's going to tie you under that clock, and I'm the only person on this river who can stop her."

The two Greycoats in the stern of our barge were watching the white barge, and the Keeper. Not us. Lady Lisle had chosen the place on her rail very carefully, I saw. Out of their eyeline. Behind a fold of the black awning.

"I'm listening," I said.

"Come to Lisle." Her voice was dry and quick and perfectly steady. "Not to a chamber under a clock. Not chained. As a guest. My house has a wing nobody's slept in for fifty years; it looks over the sea. You'd have your own rooms, your own garden, your own maid. You could have your brother. We'd drink from you, of course." She said it quite simply, the way you'd mention the rent. "A little. Once a week. Not a year every morning over toast like a common broker."

"And the thread?" I lifted my wrist, an inch, so the silver Ledger thread caught the light. "She can drink me from anywhere. She'd drink me dry from your sea wing."

"I have a cutter," said Lady Lisle.

I went very still.

"Not Varga," she said, watching my face. "Oh, yes, I know about Varga. Everyone who's anyone in this city knows about Varga. No. Mine's better. Mine's cut three Ledger threads for my House in sixty years, and both ends lived every time." She smiled. "Ask her, if you like. Ask your Keeper. She'll tell you she's never lost a well to Lisle. She'll be lying."

I looked at her fox's face, and I thought: *you're lying too*. But I didn't know which part.

"And at the Turning?" I said. "When she goes down to tie the Stay?"

"Ah." Lady Lisle leaned a little closer on the rail. "Now that's the interesting part. Do you know how the Renewal's done, child?"

"Her blood on the knot," I said slowly. "Her palm on my mother's heart."

"In the slack of that heart." Lady Lisle nodded. "My mother saw it once, two hundred years ago, when the Seven still witnessed it. She told me every detail. The Keeper opens her palm with the knife. She lays it on the well's breast, over the heart. And in the slack between two beats, she ties the knot." Her voice dropped further. "And in that moment, child, for that one blink, the Keeper's heart and the well's are the same heart. Joined. Through the blood. She's open. She can't draw. She can't cast. She can't do anything at all except tie." She smiled. "And whoever is holding the Keeper's knife in that moment holds the Stay."

The river slid past under the two rails. On the white barge, under the awning, Lady Aurelia was patting the Crane steward's hand.

"You mean to take it," I said. "The knife. At the Turning."

"I mean to take the Ledger," said Lady Ismay Lisle. "I've meant to for a hundred and fifty years. The knife's only the handle." Her fox's eyes held mine. "My people will be in that chamber at the Turning; the Inner Ledger witnesses, and Lisle is the Inner Ledger. When she opens her palm, they'll take her. And I'll tie the knot. Myself. With your blood in it." A pause. "*Willing* blood. The Charter's very particular, I'm told. Which is why I'm making you this offer on a barge in front of the whole Gilt, instead of having you taken off it in a sack."

"And my mother?"

Lady Lisle looked at me for a moment as if I'd asked about the weather in a country she'd never visited.

"The well stays, of course," she said. "Someone has to hold the clock."

---

I didn't say yes. I didn't say no.

I said, "I'll think about it," the way I'd said it to Lady Aurelia over chocolate, and Lady Lisle smiled her dry fox's smile and said, "Of course you will," exactly the way Lady Aurelia had, and pushed herself off the rail and walked back along her white barge to her chair in the stern.

I sat under the black awning with my hands in my lap and my heart going, and reached down two threads at once.

*Severin. Bry.* Free, both words. *Listen.*

And I told them. All of it. Every word, the way Lady Lisle had said it. The cutter who'd cut three Ledger threads in sixty years. The sea wing. Her people in the chamber at the Turning. The knife. The one blink, in the slack, when the Keeper's heart and the well's are the same heart and she can't draw, can't cast, can't do anything but tie.

*She means to take the Ledger. At the Renewal. In the chamber. With her own people.*

Down the black thread, Bry swore, long and inventively and at an hour a word, and I let her.

Down the silver one, a long silence. Then two words. Two hours.

*One blink.*

I knew what he meant. I'd heard Lotte's voice in my head every night for a fortnight, through Bry. *A Ledger thread's got a slack the length of a blink. Less.* Whoever was in that chamber at the Turning, the Unwound and the Keeper and the Lisles and the Inner Ledger and the Council and Durrow's men, everything was going to come down to one blink. In the slack of my mother's heart.

---

The plank went across again. Lady Aurelia came back over it in her black lace as lightly as she'd gone, and sat down beside me under the black silk, and arranged her fur, and opened her silver box.

"Row on," she said to the oarsmen. "Home now, I think. It's getting cold."

The black barge turned in the reach and began to pull slowly downstream, toward the Gilt landing, with the clock hanging ahead of us over the island at three minutes past eleven. Behind us, the white barge sat on the brown water. I didn't look back. I could feel Lady Lisle's fox's eyes on the back of my neck all the same.

Lady Aurelia ate a violet. She looked out at the river, at the barges, at the bright silly flags and the awnings and the thin music, and didn't say anything for a long time.

Then, without turning her head:

"Ismay has been trying to steal my wells for a hundred years," she said.

I went cold all over.

"The last one. And the one before. She sent a man down the old Lisle tunnel once, with a cutter, in the Year 190. I found him in the passage outside the silver door. What was left of him." She ate another violet. "She's very persistent. I admire that, in a way. It's the only thing about her I've ever admired."

The oars dipped and rose. The water slid past.

"The Crane steward," said Lady Aurelia, "had nothing at all to say that he hadn't said to me in a letter last week. I could have recited it to him. I knew exactly why Ismay wanted me on her barge for half an hour, out of earshot, behind a fold of my own awning." She smiled. "I let her have it. I wanted to see what she'd offer you. And I wanted to see what you'd say."

She turned her head at last, and looked at me, with her warm brown gold-flecked eyes and both dimples.

"Did you say yes, dear?"
