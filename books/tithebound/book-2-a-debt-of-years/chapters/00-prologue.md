# Prologue

## The Mother's Tether

::: {.pov}
Maren
:::

*Ten years ago*

The baby fed badly, the way he always did, in short desperate pulls with long frightened gaps between them, as though even at three weeks old he understood that there wasn't enough of anything.

Maren Fenn sat by the window in the dark with him at her breast and watched the street.

The fog had come up off the Sallow at dusk and filled Tallow Lane to the eaves, so thick that the lamp at the bottom of the lane was only a smear of yellow, like a thumbprint of butter on grey paper. Nothing moved under it. Nothing had moved under it for an hour. That was what frightened her. On any other night there would have been somebody: the night-shift girls from the Candleworks going home arm in arm, a drunk singing, the chandler's dog nosing at the gutter. Tonight the lane was empty all the way down, as if the whole street had decided to stay indoors.

As if the whole street had been told to.

Two days ago, there had been a man under that lamp. Young, big, red in the face, in a grey coat with silver buttons, standing in the cold with his hands in his pockets and his eyes on her window. He'd stood there for an hour in the afternoon, and another hour in the evening, and in the morning he'd been gone, and she'd told herself it was nothing.

It wasn't nothing. She'd known it wasn't nothing since the day she'd done the stupid thing.

Kit stopped feeding. She moved him to her shoulder and rubbed his back in small circles, and he made the little creaking sound he made instead of crying, because crying took breath and he didn't have breath to spare. She turned his left arm to the faint light from the window, the way she had every hour of every night since he was born. One gold line, and a scatter of notches like crumbs. Eleven years, he'd come with. Ten and a bit, now.

*Well,* the midwife had said, looking from his arm to her face. *Love him quickly.*

That was why she'd done the stupid thing. Midwives cost money, and physics cost more, and coal cost the most of all in a February like this one, and a baby with lungs like wet paper needs a room that's warm. So eight days ago she had walked into a broker's shop on the Row under the name of Anna Pike and sold three years. And four days after that, because the coal was gone again and the physic wanted silver, she had walked into a different broker's shop under the name of Wenna Sallow and sold the same three years again.

The same three. She'd watched them drain out of her arm onto two different counters, and she'd watched them come home both times before morning, the way her years always came home, the way her mother's had, and her mother's mother's.

*Never sell twice in a season,* her mother had told her, a hundred times, when she was a girl. *Never the same count to the same street. Somebody's always writing it down.*

Somebody had written it down.

---

She put Kit in the cot.

He fussed and then settled, his small fists up by his ears, his breath coming fast and shallow. She tucked the blanket around him and stood looking down at him for a long moment. Then she turned to the other bed.

Tamsin was asleep on her side with one arm flung out over the edge of the mattress and her dark hair all over the pillow. Nine years old. All elbows and knees and opinions. She slept the way she did everything, hard and completely, as though sleep were an argument she intended to win. Her left arm hung down toward the floor, palm up, and in the dark the count on it glowed softly from her wrist almost to her elbow, gold, steady, long. Seventy-one years and four months. A long count for a Wick girl. The longest on the lane.

Not a well's count. Not yet. Maybe not ever. Maren had looked at that small arm every morning of her daughter's life and seen it tick down a day at a time like anybody's, and been glad. *It only wakes when the last one goes,* her mother had said. *When I die, it'll come to you. When you die, to her. That's the way of it. That's why we hide.*

Maren sat down on the end of the bed.

She'd never done this before. She'd seen it done once, when she was smaller than Tamsin, on a night very like this one, with fog at the window and the lane gone quiet. Her mother sitting on the end of her bed in the dark, humming. A warmth at her wrist, hot and then cold, like a hand closing around it. And in the morning her mother gone, and three days later, back, thin and grey and shaking, with a story about a broker's men and a long walk through the Sump to lose them. *It held,* her mother had said, holding her so tight it hurt. *It held. Whatever they'd taken from me, you'd have had half of it. That's what it's for.*

The mother's tether. No needle. No thread. No binder. Just blood, and a sleeping child, and a song.

Maren pricked the end of her own left little finger with the pin from her shawl. The crooked one: bent sideways at the middle joint, the way it had bent on its own the night her mother died, five years ago, the night the well woke in her. A bead of blood came up, dark in the dark.

She took Tamsin's hand. Gently. Turned it over. Laid the bleeding fingertip against the inside of her daughter's wrist, just below where the count began.

And hummed.

It was an old song. A Wick song, the kind laundresses sing over the vats and mothers sing over cots, with no proper words, only sounds, rising and falling like water. Her mother had sung it. Her mother's mother. It went back further than anybody knew, further than the Drowning, further maybe than the clock. Maren sang it under her breath, so softly the sound barely left her, and felt for the thing inside her that she'd been hiding all her life.

There it was. The well. Deep down, under her ribs, under her heart, a cold endless brightness like water at the bottom of a shaft, always full, never still.

She drew up a thread of it.

It came up through her and down her arm and into her crooked finger, and out through the drop of blood into Tamsin's wrist, and Tamsin stirred in her sleep and frowned and said something that wasn't a word. Maren kept humming. She felt the thread go in under the child's skin and settle there, warm, invisible, and she held it, and she thought as hard as she had ever thought anything: *Hold. Hold where you are tonight. Whatever you lose, I'll fill. Whatever they take from me, half of it is yours. Hold.*

Tamsin's count flickered. All seventy-one lines of it, from the wrist to the elbow, brightened for a moment like a fire when a door opens, and then steadied.

And held.

Maren took her finger away. There was nothing on Tamsin's wrist. Not a mark. Not a line. Only a faint warmth, fading, where the blood had been.

She sat there with her daughter's hand in hers and wept without making any sound at all.

---

She'd meant to do Kit too.

She'd meant to do it first. She'd sat by the window all evening with him at her breast meaning to, and she hadn't, because she was afraid. Because a mother's tether is a hard thing to tie, and the well gives up only so much in a night, and her own mother had told her you could only tie one; the second would have to wait for the well to fill again. And because a baby with eleven years and lungs like wet paper might not survive the warmth going in.

And because, if she was honest, she'd thought there would be time.

She went and stood over the cot. Kit was asleep with his mouth open, breathing in quick little sips. She put out her hand and almost touched him, and didn't.

*Tomorrow night,* she told him silently. *I'll lead them off. I'll lose them in the Sump the way your grandmother did. I'll come back tomorrow night when the well's full again and do yours. I promise. I'll come back.*

She bent and kissed the top of his head, where the hair was soft as down.

Then she went back to Tamsin and knelt by the bed and kissed her on the forehead. Tamsin's eyes came half open, dark and unfocused, the way they did when she was more asleep than awake.

"Mama?"

"Shh. Go back to sleep."

"Where are you going?"

"Just out. Mind the baby."

"Mm," said Tamsin, already going under again. "Mind the baby."

"Promise me something."

"Mm."

"If you ever—" Maren stopped. *If you ever feel me. If your wrist ever burns. If anyone ever tells you the clock has a heart.* There wasn't time. There was never time. "Never go looking for me," she said. "Promise. Whatever happens. Never."

But Tamsin was asleep again, with her arm hanging over the edge of the bed and her count glowing soft and steady in the dark, and didn't answer.

---

The stairs creaked the way they always creaked. The chandler's dog lifted its head in the doorway at the bottom and looked at her and didn't bark. She stepped over it and went out into Tallow Lane, and the fog closed around her like a wet hand.

She didn't go toward the river. She'd planned that much, sitting at the window: away from the house, away from the children, up the lane and left into Candle Row and down through the courts toward the Sump, where the alleys run together like knots in a net and a woman who knows them can be gone in a breath. Lead them off. Lose them. Come back tomorrow.

She'd gone eleven steps when she saw the lamp.

It was the one at the bottom of the lane, the smear of yellow in the fog, and there were people under it now. Three men in grey coats with silver buttons, very still, the way men stand when they've been told to wait. One of them was the young one with the red face. He took his hands out of his pockets when he saw her.

And in front of them, in the middle of the circle of lamplight, a woman.

She was small. That was the first thing Maren saw. Small and slight, no taller than a girl, in black lace from her throat to the cobbles, with a cloud of pale gold hair pinned up loosely and a fur thrown over her shoulders against the cold. She had a little silver box in one hand. As Maren watched, she opened it, and took something out between two fingers, small and dark, and put it in her mouth.

The fog smelled suddenly of violets.

Maren stood still in the middle of Tallow Lane. She thought about running. She thought about the alleys of the Sump, and the river, and the window above the chandler's where a baby was breathing in quick little sips and a girl was asleep with her arm hanging over the edge of the bed. She thought: *if I run, they'll go up the stairs.*

She didn't run.

The small woman in black lace swallowed her violet and smiled, and two dimples came out in her round sweet face, and through the lace of her sleeves her count shone like the Gilt seen from the river at night.

"There you are," she said.
