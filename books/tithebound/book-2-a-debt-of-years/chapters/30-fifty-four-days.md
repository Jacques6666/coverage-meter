# Chapter Thirty

## Fifty-Four Days

::: {.pov}
Tamsin
:::

We brought my mother home on a laundry cart.

It was the same cart. I knew it the moment I saw it waiting at the island end of the Hours Bridge in the thin afternoon sun: the big flat bed, the iron-rimmed wheels, the tired brown horse asleep on its feet between the shafts, and the same two old men on the box in the same two caps. Every Seventhday after supper they drove it over the bridge to the Meridian's kitchen door, loaded it with sacks of dirty linen, and drove it back before the tenth bell. One night they'd driven it back with me under the nightshirts, and never known.

The laundresses had sent it. Lotte's women, on the same day they'd given Lotte to the river. They'd made up the bed of it with clean sheets, six deep, folded square, and a pillow with a darn in one corner. When Pell came across the court from the Tithe Hall with my mother in his arms, the older of the two old men climbed down off the box and took off his cap and held it against his chest. He didn't put it back on until we were over the bridge.

Pell laid her on the sheets the way he'd set a loaf in an oven. Kit climbed up after her without asking anybody and sat cross-legged by her head with her hand in his lap. I walked by the near wheel. Bry walked by the far one with her taped fists in her pockets and her eyes on everything, the way she'd walk beside anything she meant to see home in one piece.

The horse sighed, and leaned into its collar, and we went.

---

Kit told our mother the city.

He did it the way I'd done it for him every night for ten years, in the dark, from the end of his cot: carefully, in order, street by street, so she wouldn't get lost. *That's the Hours Bridge. Twenty-four lamps. That's the Long Way; it's really the Gilt Road, but nobody calls it that. That's the Row, where the brokers are. That's Pruett's. That's the Sump, you can see the steam. That's Candle Row.* She lay on the clean sheets with her eyes closed and her crooked finger hooked round his, and listened to every word.

"I know, love," she said at last. "I used to live here."

Kit considered this.

"It's changed," he said.

"Has it?"

He looked round at the Wick going by on either side of the cart. The soot-black brick, the washing strung between the windows, the gutters, the smoke.

"No," he admitted.

My mother laughed. It was the cracked, surprised laugh Pell had got out of her in the round room. The old man on the box turned round at the sound of it, then turned back quickly and wiped his face on his sleeve.

---

Nobody in the Wick was indoors.

Bry said nobody had been since the round room. It wasn't like the night the clock ticked and cracked Mrs. Achterberg's windows, when the whole of the river flats had stood out in the cold in their nightclothes staring at the clock as though it might fall on them. Nobody was looking at the clock now.

They were looking at their arms.

On every step. In every doorway. Sleeves shoved up past the elbow in the raw spring wind, arms held out to the light, held out to neighbors, held out to strangers. Two dockers at the corner of the Row with their forearms side by side, comparing, like boys comparing conkers. A woman leaning so far out of an upstairs window on the Long Way that two of her children were hanging on to her skirts, shouting down to somebody in the street: "*Two! Two of them, Ma! Two!*" An old man on an upturned crate outside a gin shop with his sleeve rolled to the shoulder, not showing anybody, only looking at it, the way you'd look at a letter from someone you'd buried.

I'd worked Mother Gannet's counter for seven years. I'd read ten thousand counts with a loupe in my eye under the lamp in her shop. I'd watched more lines go out than anyone on Tallow Lane: brown at the ends, then grey, then nothing, the way they go when they're sold.

I had never once seen one come in.

They came in at the top. The counter's part of my brain noticed that before the rest of me caught up; it never stops working, that part, not even at a funeral. At the top of the ladder, above the last bright line, where there'd been nothing but bare skin for years, there was a new line. Faint. A scratch of light like a hair caught in a sunbeam, so pale you had to tilt your arm to the sky to find it. In the morning it was brighter. The morning after, it was gold, with another one coming in above it.

Coming home.

The old women were the worst of it. Or the best. I couldn't tell which. They sat on their steps with their arms laid out on their knees and their sleeves pushed up over thin brown forearms that had been short so long they'd stopped bothering to cover them, and they cried, openly, in the street, in front of their grandchildren. Years they'd sold at fifteen to bury a father. At twenty for a cradle. At forty because the broker's men were on the stairs. All of it coming home to them one faint line at a time, from whatever Gilt arm had drunk it.

"*Forty years ago!*" An old woman on the corner of Candle Row was holding her arm up at the cart like a torch. I knew her face; she'd sold to Gannet twice when I was on the counter. "*Forty years ago I sold it! For my wedding dress!*" Her voice cracked. "*And it was a horrible dress!*"

Somebody laughed. Then the whole corner did. Then she did too, still crying, still holding her arm up, and the laundry cart rolled on past her into the noise.

Up the hill behind us, over the white terraces of the Gilt, the gold haze I'd seen from the river wall that morning was still hanging in the air, thin and shining, like heat off a road in summer. It looked as though somebody had left the whole Gilt on the stove.

And at the bottom of the Row, in Pruett's window, where the sign had said TWELVE CROWNS THE YEAR a week ago, there was a new sign. ONE PENNY THE YEAR, it said. And under it, smaller, in a different hand: ANYONE?

Nobody was buying. You'd have had to be mad. Anything you bought now, you only got to keep until midnight.

---

Somebody had chalked a number on the wall at the bottom of Tallow Lane.

On the soot-black bricks of the corner house, under the iron lamp. Big. White. As tall as a child.

*54*

It had gone up the evening after the round room, Bry said, while we were still under the river: *56*. Then *55*. This morning, when the bells rang and the hand moved, somebody had come out with a wet rag and rubbed it out and written the new one, and nobody had seen who. Everyone in the lane had a theory. Mrs. Achterberg thought it was the Candleworks girls. Tobias Wren thought it was the Unwound. Bry thought it was Mrs. Achterberg.

The cart stopped under it. Past the corner, the lane was too narrow for the wheels.

I saw my mother open her eyes and look up.

Not at the number. At the lamp above it. An ordinary iron lamp on an iron bracket, unlit, with a crack in one pane, the same lamp that had hung at the bottom of our lane my whole life. She looked at it for a long moment, and her crooked finger tightened round Kit's.

Then she looked at the number.

"Fifty-four days," said Kit, with enormous satisfaction, as though he'd chalked it himself. He studied the wall with his mapmaker's eye. "I'm going to do it tomorrow. Before whoever it is. I'm going to get up before the bells."

"You'll do no such thing," I said. "You'll be in bed."

"Fifty-three," said Kit to the wall, ignoring me completely. "Fifty-two. Fifty-one."

Pell lifted my mother off the sheets.

---

The whole lane walked up behind us.

I didn't notice them until we were halfway up. I was watching Pell's feet on the wet cobbles, and my mother's hair hanging down over his arm, and Kit walking beside him with one hand up, holding hers. When I looked round, the lane was full. Wick people in shawls and aprons and shirtsleeves, coming out of their doors and falling in behind us without a word, the way they'd fall in behind a coffin. Except that nobody was crying. Or some of them were, but not that way.

Tobias Wren was standing in the door of the chandlery in his leather apron. Mr. Wren, who'd charged us for every candle stub for ten years and once tried to charge Kit for the smell. He looked at my mother in Pell's arms. He took his pipe out of his mouth.

"Mrs. Fenn," he said, and nodded, as though she'd been gone a week.

"Mr. Wren," said my mother.

And on the step, across the doorway, where she'd lain every day of my life, the chandler's old dog lifted her grey muzzle off her paws and looked up at my mother going by, and her tail went, twice, on the boards.

---

Two flights. Pell had to take them sideways, with my mother held against his chest and her bare feet tucked up under Rook's grey blanket, and his shoulders brushing the walls of the stairwell on both sides. The stairs creaked the way they always creaked. Third step, seventh step, the one at the turn. My mother's eyes moved at every one, as though she were counting.

Mrs. Achterberg was on the landing.

She was standing in the open door of her own rooms, across from ours, in her shawl, with her big arms folded. The way she'd stood at the corner of the Long Way on the morning of the Renewal, when the Keeper's carriage went by with Kit's face pressed to the glass. When Pell came round the turn of the stair, she unfolded them.

"Maren Fenn," said Mrs. Achterberg.

"Hanne," said my mother.

I hadn't known Mrs. Achterberg had a first name.

Mrs. Achterberg looked at her for a long time. Then she said, "Ten years. You left me a baby that couldn't breathe and a girl of nine who bit."

My mother looked at me over Pell's arm. "Did she bite you?"

"Twice."

"Once," I said. "The second time I had a fever. It doesn't count."

Mrs. Achterberg pulled her apron up over her face and cried into it, standing there on the landing, huge and shaking. I'd never seen her cry. Not when her husband died. Not the second winter, when Kit's lungs filled and she sat up with me three nights running with the kettle steaming and her big hand on his back. She stood on the landing and cried into her apron, and my mother reached out from Pell's arms as he went by and put her hand on Mrs. Achterberg's shoulder, and left it there until Pell had to turn in at our door.

---

I didn't recognize our rooms.

I stood in the doorway, and for a moment I thought Pell had gone into the wrong ones. There was a fire in the grate. In the afternoon. In *spring*. There was a sack of coal beside it, a whole sack, with the top rolled down, and on the table a basket of eggs, a ham, a loaf, a crock of butter, and a jar with a cloth tied over it that turned out to be honey from the Ostrander hives, sent down from the Gilt by Fitch Ostrander with no note. There were four chairs. We'd had one chair for ten years. None of the four matched.

There were candles everywhere. Along the windowsill, on the mantel, on the table, on the floor by the wall. Forty of them at least, plain tallow, still with the ribbon round the bundle they'd come in. Mr. Wren, said Bry. Which was thirty-nine more candles than we'd ever had at once, and thirty-nine more than Mr. Wren had ever given anybody for nothing.

And on the bed by the window, my bed, somebody had spread Mrs. Achterberg's quilt. The good one, with the patches cut from her mother's dresses, which she kept folded in a chest and had never let anybody so much as sit on.

"Saints' teeth," I said.

"Don't swear in front of your mother," said Mother Gannet.

She was sitting in the best of the four chairs, by the fire, with her shawls round her and her ringed hands folded on her stomach and her feet up on the coal sack. "Two flights," she said. "Two flights, with my knees. I want it known."

"You didn't have to come, Gannet."

"Didn't have to come," said Gannet to the ceiling. "Listen to her. I taught her everything she knows." Then she looked at my mother in Pell's arms, and her small sharp eyes went over her the way they'd go over a count on her counter, and something in her face changed.

"You look like something the river sent back," said Mother Gannet.

"You look exactly the same," said my mother.

Gannet looked pleased.

Pell laid my mother down on my bed, on Mrs. Achterberg's quilt, under the window that stuck. The bed she'd sat on the end of ten years ago, in the dark, while I slept, and tied me to her with a drop of blood and a song.

She lay back on the pillow and looked up at our ceiling. At the crack across it, the long one shaped like the Sallow, that I'd looked at every night of my life.

"Open the window," said my mother.

"Mama, it's cold."

"Open it, Tamsin. I want to hear the lane."

I went to it. Everybody always tried to lift it. That was the mistake. It had been painted shut and unstuck and painted shut again so many times it didn't think of itself as a window anymore. You put the heel of your hand on the bottom left corner of the frame and hit it, once, hard, upward and in, the way Bry taught me to hit a man under the chin. Then you lift.

"Bottom left," said my mother from the bed, before I'd moved. "Heel of your hand."

"I know, Mama."

I hit it. It shrieked and went up, and the lane came in: the cold, and the smell of tallow and river and coal smoke, and the noise of the whole Wick out in the street.

When I turned round, my mother was looking at me with an expression I couldn't read.

"You showed me," I said. "When I was six. You said one day I'd be tall enough to do it on my own."

"I remember."

"I've done it every morning since," I said. "Ten years."

She didn't say anything. She held out her hand, and I went and took it.

---

It was Kit who made her look at the door.

He'd been everywhere at once since we came in, showing her things. The crack in the ceiling. The loose board by the grate where we kept the money, when we had any. His cot, which he was much too big for now, so that his feet stuck out of the end when he slept, and the drawer underneath it, full of maps. He'd shown her the maps one at a time, sitting on the end of the bed, until Bry took them off him and told him to let the woman breathe.

Then he said, "Mama. You have to see the door," and before I could stop him, she was trying to get up.

"Mama—"

"Help me, then," said my mother. "Or get out of my way."

So I helped her. Pell on one side and me on the other. Her feet on our bare boards were white and thin, and her knees shook, and it took us a long time to cross the room. When we got to the door, she put her hand flat on the frame and held on to it, breathing, and looked.

Two ladders.

That's what they looked like. I'd never thought of it before, and then I couldn't think of anything else. On the inside of the door frame, in pencil, two ladders of short level lines, one on each side, like two counts drawn on the wood.

On the left, Kit's. In my writing. *K 1*, down below my hip, where I'd held him up against the wood the first winter, before he could stand, while Mrs. Achterberg held the pencil. *K 2. K 3.* Up and up. *K 6*, very small, which I'd done after the bad winter, because I hadn't been sure there'd be a *K 7*. *K 9. K 10*, at the height of my shoulder.

On the right, older and fainter, in a rounder hand: *T 2*, low down by the hinge. *T 3. T 4.* Up and up. *T 8. T 9.*

And then nothing.

Nothing above *T 9* but bare wood, all the way up the frame.

Nobody had measured me since. There hadn't been anybody to.

My mother stood with her hand on the frame and looked at the two ladders for a very long time. At Kit's first. All ten rungs of it, in my writing. She touched them one by one with the tip of her crooked finger, going up, the way you'd read a count. *K 1. K 2. K 3.* All the way to the top.

"You minded the baby," said my mother.

I couldn't say anything.

Then she looked at mine. She touched *T 9*, the top one. Then the bare wood above it.

She held out her hand to Kit without looking at him.

"Pencil," she said.

Kit always had a pencil. He put it in her palm, and she closed her fingers round it.

"Stand there," said my mother.

"Mama, you need to lie down—"

"Tamsin," said my mother. "Stand *there*."

I stood there. With my back to the frame and my heels to the skirting, the way I'd made Kit stand every birthday for ten years. *Stop wriggling. Stand up straight. Chin in.* I felt her hand come down flat on the top of my head, level, light, shaking. I felt the pencil scratch the wood behind my hair. Then her hand went away, and I heard her writing, slowly, and her breathing, short and hard.

I didn't turn round. Not for a while.

When I did, there it was. *T 19*, in the rounder hand, gone shaky.

And between it and *T 9*, the longest gap on either ladder. A whole forearm of bare wood. Ten years with no rungs at all.

"There," said my mother. "Now it's right."

"Me," said Kit. "Do me."

She did him. *K 10* again, not even a fingernail's width above the last one. Kit stepped back and looked at it and was outraged.

"That's *nothing*," he said. "That's hardly *anything*."

"It's something," said my mother. Then her knees went, and Pell caught her, the way he'd caught her in the round room, and carried her back to the bed.

---

Pell had made a cake.

It sat in the middle of the table under a cloth, and when he took the cloth off, Mrs. Achterberg said "*Oh*," and Gannet sat up in her chair. It was enormous. It was iced white all over, smooth as a new sheet, and on the top, in currants, Pell had made a clock face. All twelve numbers, in tiny careful currants, and one hand, pointing straight up. At twelve.

"For midnight," said Pell shyly, looking down at his huge floury hands. "For when it comes. Only we didn't think we could wait fifty-four days to eat it."

Bry had flour to the elbows. She'd helped. You could tell which bits; the seven was backward.

Pell had a new line too. It was coming in at the top of his count, faint as a hair, where the Keeper had taken eight years from him at breakfast in the Glass Room while I watched. He kept pushing up his sleeve to look at it and pulling it down again, the way he checked on bread in an oven, as though it mightn't rise if he stared.

---

Hollis came late in the afternoon, up the stairs three at a time, shouting.

I heard him from the bottom of the lane. Everybody heard him from the bottom of the lane. "*Tam! TAM!*" And then the whole of Tallow Lane turning to look, and his feet on the stairs, third step, seventh step, the turn, and he burst through our door in plum velvet with his hair on end and coal dust on his cheekbones, which meant he'd come down from the Gilt on the barge again, and his sleeve already shoved up to the shoulder.

"Look at it," said Hollis Penhallow. "*Look at it.*"

I looked. I couldn't help it. Seven years at a counter.

"Eight years," I said. "And four months. And one coming in."

"*Eight years and four months!*" Hollis flung both arms in the air. "Three days ago I had five and eleven. Five and *eleven*, Tam. I've had the number written on the inside of my wardrobe door since I was seven, every birthday, going down. And now look at it. It's going *up*." He stared at his own arm as though it belonged to somebody far more interesting. "Counts don't go up. Not Penhallow counts. Penhallow counts go down, and then the painter comes." He drew a breath. "I've written to the painter. This morning. I've cancelled him."

"Hollis—"

"The racehorse years," said Hollis. "My great-great-grandfather's. The ones he sold to Thane." His voice did something strange. "They came home to somebody, Tam. They came home to me."

Then he saw my mother.

She was sitting up in the bed by the window, against the pillow, with her chin on her hand, watching him with an expression on her thin face that I hadn't seen there before. Delight, I think.

Hollis went pinker than his velvet. He pulled his sleeve down. He smoothed his hair, which made no difference at all. Then he bowed, the whole Gilt bow, all the way down, plum sleeve sweeping our bare boards.

"Mrs. Fenn," he said. "Hollis Penhallow. Of the short Penhallows. Formerly." He straightened. "I've heard so much about you. Mostly from a map."

"I've heard about you," said my mother. "Tamsin says you're the only person in the Gilt who ever made her laugh on purpose."

Hollis put his hand on his heart. "On *purpose*," he said. "She's never once admitted that to me."

Behind him, ducking under our door frame, came a tall young man in Vey green, with a book under one elbow and an armful of white narcissus.

"This is Ambrose," said Hollis. "He's my—" He stopped. He went pinker. "We're engaged. Our mothers arranged it. Against our will. It's a tragedy. We're bearing it bravely."

"I brought flowers," said Ambrose Vey to my mother, a little helplessly. "I didn't know what one brings. To someone who's been under a clock."

"Flowers," said my mother. "One brings flowers."

He gave them to her. Narcissus from the Vey glasshouses, smelling of spring. She held them in her lap. I noticed, the way I noticed everything, that Ambrose Vey kept his green cuffs buttoned to the wrist, and that he handed her the flowers carefully, with his arm turned in, so nobody would see it.

Half the Gilt had been doing that for three days. That was what midnight was for. I thought: he'll have what's his, and Hollis will have what's his, and they can be old together. I didn't think about it any more than that.

"I'm going to be old," said Hollis, as if he'd heard me. He'd sat down on the end of the bed without being asked and taken a slice of Pell's cake without being offered. "I'm going to be *old*, Tam. Fat and gouty and boring. I'm going to tell the same story at every party until people hide behind the curtains when they see me coming."

"I'll listen to it," said Ambrose Vey.

Hollis went pink all the way to the tips of his ears and didn't say anything for nearly a minute, which was a record.

---

Bry didn't eat any cake.

I found her on the landing later, while Hollis was telling my mother about the Thaw Regatta with all the parts acted out, and Ambrose was laughing in the right places. She was sitting on the top step in the dark with her back against the wall and her sleeve pushed up, looking at her arm in the light that came out of our door.

She didn't pull it down when she saw me. That was how I knew it was bad, or good, or both.

I sat down on the step beside her and looked.

Twenty years, near enough. And at the top, a new line coming in, faint as a hair. Three days ago she'd had eighteen and one.

"It's mine," said Bry. Her voice was rough. "What Lysander took. It was in him, and then it was in her, and now it's—" She stopped. She pressed the back of her taped hand against her mouth. "Coming home. Like the old women. Like I sold it." Her voice cracked straight down the middle. "I didn't *sell* it, Tam."

"I know."

"I'm going to be old," said Bry. "I'm going to be an old woman on a step. I'm going to be *horrible*."

"You're horrible now."

She laughed, wet and furious, and wiped her face on her floury sleeve, and left a streak of white across her cheek like war paint.

On my wrist, the black thread was warm.

It had been warm since the round room. Both of them had, the black one and the silver. Warm all the time, steadily, the way a stone stays warm after the sun's gone off it. I'd first noticed it in the Tithe Hall, lying awake on the floor by my mother's bed, and I'd put my hand over them in the dark and thought: that's what it is. That's what happens to a thread when the people on both ends of it are finally safe.

---

At dusk, the lamps came on along the Hours Bridge.

I watched them from the window, with my elbows on the sill and the cold coming in round me. One, and then the next, and the next. All twenty-four, a long gold dotted line across the dark water to the island. My mother had told me, under the clock, that I used to sit at this window when I was three and wait for my father to light them. I didn't remember. But I'd sat here a thousand evenings since without knowing why, and watched them come on, and felt better.

Somebody else's father lit them now.

Above them, over the island, the clock. Its great pale face, lit from inside the way it always was at night, and its single iron hand. Six minutes past eleven.

Behind me the room was full of noise. Hollis and Ambrose and Mrs. Achterberg, and Gannet snoring in the good chair with her feet on the coal, and Pell washing cups in the basin, and Bry drying them and calling him a fool for something. My mother had fallen asleep in the middle of the Thaw Regatta, with the narcissus in her lap and her hand open on the quilt. Before she went, she'd caught my sleeve as I bent to take the flowers, and held it.

"It's very quiet," she'd said. She touched her breastbone. "In here. Ten years, it was like standing under a waterfall. Now it's—" She thought about it. "A pond."

"Is that bad?"

"It's restful," said my mother, and closed her eyes.

Down in the lane, Mr. Wren was going from door to door with a taper, putting a candle in every window on Tallow Lane. I watched the light go up the lane, window by window, yellow in the blue dusk. Under them, in the street, the Wick children were playing a new game. I'd heard it start an hour ago. They lined up under the candles with their sleeves pushed up and their arms held out, and compared.

"*Mine's got one!*"

"*Mine's got two!*"

"*My nan's got forty!*"

Kit was down there. I could see the top of his head among them, and the map under his arm. All afternoon the children of Tallow Lane had been coming up the stairs in twos and threes to look at the boy who'd drawn the room under the clock, and he'd been down and up and down again a dozen times, showing them, and coughing a little on the stairs, the way he did, and I'd let him.

I'd spent my whole life wanting more time. For Kit. For Mama. For all of us. Every year I'd ever sold under somebody else's name, I'd sold to buy time for somebody, and every morning I'd looked at the clock across the river and hated it for being so slow to kill us all.

Fifty-four days.

For the first time in my life, I wanted time to go faster.

"*Kit Fenn!*" A girl's voice, down in the lane. "*Show us yours! You've got thirty-one!*"

"*Go on, Kit! Show us!*"

I smiled, and turned away from the window.

---

I felt him before I saw him.

The silver thread. All afternoon I'd felt him through it, somewhere in the Wick, his heart doing something slow and careful that I couldn't read. I hadn't sent him anything. I'd left him that morning at the river wall, walking up the ramp with his mother on his arm, and gone back over the bridge for mine, and I'd thought, for once, he should have something that was only his.

Now he was close. At the bottom of the lane. Coming up.

And his heart was going slow, and slower, and slow. Like something in deep water, sinking out of reach.

I knew that heartbeat. I'd felt it once before, in our doorway, on a night of fog, when my little brother sat up in his cot and asked him whether the clock was hungry, and he didn't answer.

That was how I'd known, that night, that it was.

I went back to the window. He was coming up Tallow Lane under the candles in his Wick coat, with his collar up and his splinted hand against his chest, and his face was set like a lid on a box. He walked past the children without looking at them. They'd gone quiet, round something in the middle of them that I couldn't see. He didn't look at the candles, or the lit windows, or the dog. He looked up at our window. At me.

I went to the door.

He came up the stairs. Third step. Seventh step. The one at the turn. He came round onto the landing and ducked under our door frame the way he'd had to the first time, past the two ladders of pencil lines, and stopped just inside.

He looked at the room. At the fire, and the four chairs, and the forty candles. At Hollis on the end of the bed with cake on his velvet, and Ambrose with his book, and Mrs. Achterberg, and Gannet asleep. At Bry by the basin with a cup in one hand and the cloth in the other, gone still. At my mother asleep under the open window with narcissus in her lap.

He looked at the cake. At the clock in currants, with its one hand pointing straight up.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he looked at me.

"Tamsin," he said. "I need to tell you something."

Hollis stopped talking.

And behind him on the stairs there were feet, fast, a child's. Third step. Seventh step. The turn. Kit came up from the lane at a run and ducked under Severin's arm in the doorway, into the light.

He was holding out his left arm, with the sleeve pushed up to the elbow, for the first time in days.

In the light of forty candles, I could see the smoke coming off it. Faint. So faint you'd have to know to look. A thin shining smoke, rising off his count the way it had risen off the Long-Lived on their knees in the round room. Drifting up past his white face, toward the open window, toward the clock.

"Tam?" said Kit. "My count's getting shorter."
