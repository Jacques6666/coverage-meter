# Chapter Sixteen

## The Cellars

::: {.pov}
Tamsin
:::

She drank from him again at breakfast.

She had him brought up from the cellars at the eighth bell by two of Durrow's men and knelt on the tiles of the Glass Room beside her chair, in the sunlight, between the orange trees and the violets, while I sat across the table with my cup held the way she'd taught me, elbow on the cloth, wrist loose. She drank a year from me first, eyes closed, the way she always did. It came back. Then she turned to Pell, and took his wrist, and drank a year from him.

He didn't sing this time. He didn't make any sound at all. He knelt with his head down and his good eye shut and his swollen face turned toward the floor, and when it was done, he swayed, and the Greycoats held him up.

Forty-one.

"There," said Lady Aurelia, letting go of him and picking up her cup. "That's today's. Take him down, please. And give him porridge; he looks like a porridge man." She smiled at me across the table. "You'll tell me, won't you, dear, if anyone happens to mention my Charter?"

I drank my tea. I held the cup the way she'd taught me. It didn't tremble once.

*Tonight,* Severin had said, at the second bell, down the silver thread. Three hours, for three words. *Tonight. Be ready.*

---

Nessa did my hair that evening the way she always did, slowly, a hundred strokes, while I sat at the dressing table in the grey wrapper and watched her face in the glass. She didn't look at me. She hadn't looked at me since the morning Pell was brought up the first time, and I understood that now. It wasn't fear anymore. It was that she knew, and she was afraid her face would show it.

At the ninety-fourth stroke, she said, to the hairbrush, very quietly: "The night man on the cellar stair likes a hot posset at the tenth bell. Cook sends one up. Cook's from Candle Row." A pause. "Cook's got a cousin who works for Mother Gannet."

I didn't say anything. I met her eyes in the glass.

"He'll sleep like a stone," said Nessa, and went on brushing.

---

Evadne Thane came for me at the eleventh bell.

She knocked on the door of the Violet Rooms the way a servant knocks, two taps, and when Corran opened it, she was standing in the corridor in her plain grey dress with a candle in her hand and a shawl around her shoulders, and her face so white that I thought for a moment she was ill.

"Lady Evadne," said Corran, surprised.

"I can't sleep," said Evadne. Her voice was perfectly steady. "Miss Fenn can't either, I'm told. I thought we might walk." She looked past him at me. "The west wing is very cold at night. I know a warmer way."

Corran looked at her. Then at me, standing behind him in the grey wrapper with a shawl of my own. He was the guard I'd bitten on the first day, in the carriage, with a white scar now across the back of his hand where my teeth had been. He'd said *good evening, miss* to me every night for a month.

"I'll walk behind, my lady," he said.

---

She took me through the kitchens.

I'd never been there. They were enormous and dark and still warm, the great ovens banked down to red, the copper pans on the walls throwing back the light of Evadne's candle in a hundred small round suns. Nobody there. The cook and the scullery maids slept in the attics. At the far end, a door; beyond it, a stair going down, stone, narrow, and at the top of the stair a stool, and on the stool a man in a grey coat with silver buttons, slumped against the wall with his chin on his chest and an empty cup in his lap, snoring.

Evadne didn't look at him. She went past him down the stairs as though he were furniture. I went after her. Behind me, I heard Corran stop at the top.

"Miss," he said.

I turned.

He was standing at the head of the cellar stair, beside the snoring Greycoat, with his scarred hand on the doorframe. In the candlelight his face was young, and frightened, and something else.

"I'll be at the top of the stairs," said Corran. "Here. All night." He swallowed. "I didn't see anything. I don't see much, at night. My eyes aren't good." A pause. "My mam's from the Sump."

He turned his back on us, and stood in the doorway, and looked at the kitchen.

---

The old laundry was at the bottom of the stair. Low, vaulted, brick, with stone sinks along one wall and drying racks hanging from the ceiling on ropes, and at the end of it, squatting in a nest of brick like an enormous copper toad, the boiler. The copper. It was cold now, and green with age, and taller than me, and it hadn't been lit in a hundred years by the look of it. Behind it, in the shadow between the copper and the wall, there was a gap you'd never see unless you knew to look.

Evadne stood in front of it with the candle shaking in her hand.

I could see it shaking. The flame jumped and leaned. Her face, in its light, had gone the color of the stone sinks.

"Aldric showed me," she said. Her voice was very small. "The night before. He held the candle. He went first." She didn't move. "I'm afraid of the dark. I've always been afraid of the dark. I've never—"

I took her hand. The one without the candle. It was cold as ice and it was trembling, and I held it the way I'd held Kit's on the nights his lungs were bad, in both of mine.

"I've been in the dark," I said. "Lots. It's mostly just the dark."

She looked at me. His eyes. Wet.

Then she turned sideways and stepped into the gap behind the copper, still holding my hand, and I went after her.

---

It was a passage. Brick, low, with an arched roof I had to duck under, and it went down, steeply, in steps, into cold that smelled of old wine and older stone. I counted. Twenty-two steps. Evadne went first, with the candle, and she didn't let go of my hand once, and she didn't stop, even when the candle guttered in a draft from somewhere below and went blue and nearly out.

At the bottom, the cellars.

Not the wine cellars. Below them. A long low corridor with a vaulted roof and a stone floor, and doors on either side, heavy, oak, banded with iron, with little barred windows in them at the height of a face. Strongrooms, Evadne whispered. From the old days. When the Houses murdered each other in their beds and needed somewhere to keep the ones they hadn't murdered yet.

At the end of the corridor, a lantern on a hook. Under it, on a stool, another man in a grey coat, sitting up, awake, with a truncheon across his knees. Durrow's.

He saw our candle. He stood up.

"Who's that?" he said. "Who's there? Nobody comes down here at—"

He didn't finish. Somebody came out of the dark behind him, out of the far end of the corridor where the lantern light didn't reach, very fast and very quiet, and hit him once on the back of the head with a taped fist, and he folded up over his own truncheon onto the floor.

Bry Kettle stood over him, breathing hard, shaking out her hand.

"Burn me," she said. "I've been wanting to hit one of these all *week*."

And behind her, stepping out of the dark with a shuttered lamp in his left hand and his right hand bound to a splint, in a plain dark coat with cellar dust on the shoulders, came Severin.

---

He saw me first. I saw him see me, the way you see a man find something he's been looking for in the dark. Then his eyes went past me, to the woman standing beside me with a shaking candle in one hand and my hand in the other.

He stopped.

I'd seen him go still a hundred times. In the Refectory. On the Dial. In the tower when the cuff came off. This was different. This was like watching a clock stop between one tick and the next, the pendulum hanging at the top of its swing, and not coming down.

Evadne let go of my hand.

She stood in the cellar corridor in her plain grey dress with the candle held in front of her, and looked at her son. At his face. At his height, which she hadn't seen up close in fourteen years. At his splinted hand.

"Sev," she said.

He didn't move. He didn't answer. His face didn't do anything at all, and I could feel his heart through the thread, and it was doing everything.

She came forward. Slowly. One step, then another, the way you'd walk toward a deer in a wood. She stopped in front of him. She had to tip her head back to look at him; he'd been a boy of seven the last time she'd looked at him like this, and now he was a head taller than her. She reached out, very slowly, with her free hand, and touched the splint on his right hand. Just her fingertips. Barely touching.

"You hit someone," said Evadne Thane.

His voice, when it came, was a voice I'd never heard him use.

"A Lisle," he said.

Her mouth moved. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one.

"Good," said his mother.

And that was all. There wasn't time for anything else. Bry was already at the door of the last strongroom with the Greycoat's keys, and Evadne took her hand away from the splint and stepped back, and Severin turned his head away, sharply, toward the door, and I saw his jaw working, and I didn't say anything. Some things you let a person have alone, even in a cellar.

---

Pell was in the last strongroom, on a heap of straw against the back wall, with his hands chained to a ring and a bowl of porridge untouched beside him.

He looked up when the door opened. When he saw Bry in the lantern light, his face crumpled. Not the swollen side. The good side. All of it, the way a child's face crumples.

"You came," he said.

"Course we came, you *idiot*." Bry was on her knees in the straw with the keys, fumbling at the iron cuffs on his wrists, and her hands were shaking so badly she dropped the ring of keys twice. "Holding *doors*. Singing *hymns*. With your *back*. Six of them, Pell, six, I *counted*, you great soft—" Her voice broke. "Who told you you could hold a door?"

"Somebody had to," said Pell.

The cuffs came off. Bry sat back on her heels in the straw and looked at him and didn't say anything. Then she put her arms around his neck and her face in his shoulder, all of her, the way she'd thrown herself at me on the Hours Bridge on Tithe Day, and held on.

Pell sat very still in the straw with his big freed hands hanging at his sides, astonished. Then, slowly, carefully, as though she might break, he put them on her back.

I looked at his arm in the lantern light. Forty-one years. And a bit.

*Stolen,* I thought. Not sold. Not spent. *Taken.* Eight years she'd taken off him in two breakfasts. They were in her now, in that blaze of gold under her black lace, along with two hundred and forty-seven years of everybody else's.

---

"Come with us," said Severin.

We were in the corridor outside the strongroom. Bry had Pell up on his feet, his arm across her shoulders, his weight half on her; he swayed, and she braced, and swore at him, and he smiled. Evadne stood a little apart with her candle. And Severin stood in front of me in the light of the shuttered lamp with his splinted hand held against his chest and his face set.

"Now," he said. "Tonight. Through the ice house, and the tunnel, and out. You and my—" He stopped. "You and Lady Evadne. Both of you. Gannet's got a bed. Orrin's got a boat. By dawn you could be on the river to Calder Bay."

I'd known he would ask. I'd known since he said *tonight* at the second bell, three hours of his life for three words. I'd lain awake thinking about how I'd answer.

"I can't," I said.

"You can. It's twenty steps to the ice house—"

"Severin." I put my hand flat on his chest, over his heart. "If I run, she goes downstairs."

He went still.

"She doesn't have to chase me," I said. "She told me so, the first morning. *Where would you go, dear? Your mother is mine.*" I could hear my voice shaking. I let it. "If I'm gone in the morning, she won't send Durrow to the Wick. She'll take her stair down to the round room and put her hand on my mother's heart. And she'll take it out of her. Whatever she can't take out of me." I swallowed. "And the thread's still on my wrist. Hers. She'd find me anyway. In a week. In a month. Wherever we went."

"Cut it," said Bry, from behind him. "Thane. Cut it now. You've been learning."

"Not with this." He held up the splint without looking at it. "Not a Ledger thread. Not left-handed, not yet. If I cut it wrong—"

"It kills the weaker heart," I said. "And nobody knows which one that is. Lotte said. You said." I pressed my hand harder against his chest. "Not yet. At the Turning. When we're all down there. All of us, in that room. When we can take her too." I looked up at him. "When we can take my *mother*."

He looked down at me for a long time in the light of the lamp.

"Five days," he said at last. Very low.

"Five days."

"She means to tie you under the clock."

"I know what she means to do. I was there. I had the chocolate." I tried to smile. It didn't work very well. "You're going to stop her. You've got a plan. You've always got a plan. You've got a Charter and a laundress and a fisherman with a rope."

He didn't laugh. He bent his head and put his forehead against mine, in the cellar, in front of Bry and Pell and his mother, and stayed there, and I felt his heart under my hand and through the thread at the same time, two drums, and I closed my eyes.

"Go, Sev," said Evadne Thane, quietly, behind me.

---

He went.

He went down the dark end of the corridor with Bry and Pell, toward the ice house and the old tunnels and the Wick, with the shuttered lamp throwing a thread of light on the floor ahead of them. He didn't look back. I knew why. I felt him not look back, through the thread, every step, until the dark took the light and there was nothing at the end of the corridor but cold.

Evadne put her hand in mine again.

"The dark," she said. Her voice was shaking. "It's mostly just the dark."

"Mostly," I said.

We went back up the twenty-two steps behind the copper, with her candle guttering, holding hands. Through the gap. Through the old laundry with its stone sinks and its drying racks. To the foot of the kitchen stair. I counted. Nessa had said the night man would sleep like a stone. Corran had said he'd be at the top of the stairs. All night.

He was. I could see the edge of his grey coat in the doorway at the top, against the faint red glow of the banked kitchen ovens. Standing very straight.

Too straight.

And beside him, in the doorway, small and slight, in a long grey silk wrapper with her honey-gold hair in a plait over her shoulder and her bare feet on the stone, holding up a lamp, stood the Keeper of the Ledger.

She looked down the stair at us. At Evadne, in her grey dress, with the candle shaking in her hand. At me. At our hands, joined.

"Corran tells me you were walking," said Lady Aurelia Thane pleasantly. "In the laundry. With Evadne. At midnight." She smiled. "I couldn't sleep either, dear. I felt your heart go so fast. I thought you might be ill."

Corran, beside her, stared straight ahead at nothing, white as paper.

"Well," said the Keeper. She lifted the lamp a little higher, so its light fell all the way down the stair onto my face.

"If you won't stay where I put you, dear," she said, "I'll put you somewhere you will."
