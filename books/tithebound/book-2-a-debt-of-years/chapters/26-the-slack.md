# Chapter Twenty-Six

## The Slack

::: {.pov}
Tamsin
:::

He came out of the dark like something falling out of the sky.

I saw him because I was looking up. I was the only one in that room who was looking up. Everyone else was looking at the Keeper, at her bowed head and her bleeding palm on my mother's heart and her other hand rising with the silver knife in it. I was looking past her, past the pendulum, into the black of the shaft, where I'd felt him all the way down.

A shape. Dark. Long. Dropping past the rim of the great iron disc into the gold light pouring up round its edge.

He hit the stone between Corisande Marchbank and the Crane steward, in the ring of the kneeling Long-Lived, so hard I heard his knees crack. He went down. He rolled, sideways, the way you'd roll off a cart, and came up on one knee with his splinted hand clamped against his chest and his face white and his eyes on me.

For a heartbeat, nobody moved.

The whole round room, the whole ring of the Gilt on its knees with their threads on, the binders, the Lisle men, Durrow with my brother against his chest, every one of them stared at the Thane heir, who had just fallen out of the dark above the pendulum into the middle of the Renewal, in a Wick coat, with cellar dust on his shoulders.

And the Keeper's eyes opened.

She didn't move. She *couldn't*. Ismay Lisle had told me, on a barge, behind a fold of black silk: *in that blink, the Keeper's heart and the well's are the same heart; she can't draw, she can't cast, she can't do anything at all except tie*. Her palm was on my mother's breast. The slack was going out between two beats of my mother's heart like a long held breath, and the Keeper was in it, joined to it, frozen in it with the silver knife half raised. All she could move was her eyes.

They found him.

I'd never seen her face do what it did then. I'll never forget it. Not fear. Not surprise. *Recognition*. The way you'd look at a clock you'd wound every morning for twenty-one years, if it suddenly opened its face and spoke.

He raised his arm.

---

It wasn't like the ballroom. It wasn't like anything. In the ballroom he'd held up the whole of Thane House with one arm over his head and his teeth bared, and his life had poured out of him like blood out of a vein. This was worse. This was faster. He didn't stand up. He stayed on one knee on the black stone, with his splinted hand against his chest, and lifted his left arm, and *stilled the ring*.

The air rang.

Like every glass in Lanthorn struck at once. Gold light rolled out from him across the black stone in a wave, round the ring, over the kneeling Long-Lived, over the binders, over the Lisle men in white who had been on their feet already, I saw, I saw them, halfway into a lunge toward the Keeper's knife hand. Over Corwin Lisle behind them. Over Durrow by the wall.

And everything stopped.

The Long-Lived froze on their knees with their mouths open and their arms held out. The binders froze with their velvet cushions. The two men in Lisle white froze in the middle of their lunge, one foot off the floor, hands reaching. Durrow froze with my brother crushed against his chest and the black needle against Kit's wrist, and Kit froze too, his mouth open, his eyes on our mother.

Frozen, nothing could happen to him. The needle couldn't move. I understood that, and something in my chest let go that I hadn't known was holding on.

The working didn't touch my mother. It couldn't; I'd learn why later, from her, in a quieter place. *You can't still a well. Time comes out of us. It doesn't stop in us.* It didn't touch me, on his thread, beside her. It didn't touch the Keeper, joined to the well through her own blood in the slack. And it didn't touch the woman standing alone by the silver door in her plain grey dress, too far back, outside the ring.

I felt it go out of him.

All of it. A river. Down the silver thread, out of his wrist, into the ringing gold air. A day. A week. A month. A year. The stilling over a ring of twenty people with their threads all running out through the walls to their houses, holding the whole Inner Ledger of Lanthorn still on the floor of the well-chamber at once.

He had eight.

*Ours,* I thought.

I didn't wait for him to ask. I reached down into the silver thread with everything I had, the way I'd done in the ballroom, and I *pushed*.

---

It went out of me like a flood.

Years. Down the silver thread into him, as fast as he was spending them, faster. And behind them, under them, from the deepest thread of all, from the woman kneeling beside me with her shoulder against mine, the well came up. Through her. Into me. Out of me. Into him. Like water through a pipe. Like a river through a mill.

I felt my mother feel it. She turned her head, as far as the threads would let her, and looked at me, and her dark eyes were wide.

"Tamsin," she said. Out loud. "*Tamsin.*"

"I know," I said. "I know, Mama. Let it."

Above us, the threads began to sing.

All of them. The ones on my wrists and ankles, the ones on hers, the Stay rising from her throat-ring into the dark, all of them at once, humming, a long low enormous chord like the strings of a harp the height of the tower. The pendulum shivered. Dust fell out of the dark above it in a long grey curtain.

And across the ring, still on one knee, with his arm raised and the gold pouring out of him and back into him at once, Severin Thane looked at me. His face was white. His teeth were bared. But the color was coming back into it. I could see it. Line by line. He was spending it as fast as I gave it, and I was giving it faster.

He got to his feet.

---

He didn't come to me. That was the plan; we'd laid it out, he and I, down the thread in the dark, in pieces, over three nights. *The door first.* He walked across the round room with his arm still raised, holding the whole ring still, between the frozen kneeling Long-Lived, past the frozen Lisle men, to the silver door.

Evadne Thane was standing beside it.

She'd seen him fall. She'd seen him raise his arm. She'd seen the gold roll out over the ring and stop at the edge of where she stood, and stop everything inside it. She was standing with her back against the black stone beside the door and her hands clasped in front of her so tightly that I could see the bones, and her face was the color of the stone.

He stopped in front of her. For one breath. With his arm raised.

She looked up at him.

Then he put his splinted right hand flat on the silver door.

It parted. Like water. Down the middle. The two halves drawing back from each other without a sound, the way they did for the Keeper's palm. *From inside, it opens for any hand. It was made to keep people out, not in.* And on the other side, in the carved passage, in the dark, at the bend where the clock faces began, the Unwound.

They came in like a flood.

Vadra first, tall, in dark green, her sleeves pushed back to the elbow for the first time I'd ever seen, her scars bare. Then Hob, with his burnt arm in its sling and a cudgel in the other. Wick faces I didn't know, men and women with thread-scars on their wrists. Magpie, small and quick, already moving. Pell, enormous, with his swollen face and his thumbs held outside his fists, exactly as he'd been taught.

And Bry.

She came through the silver door with her taped fists up and her black eye gone yellow and her teeth bared, and she saw me, kneeling in silver in the light, and she saw Durrow by the wall with Kit, and she said something I won't write down.

And behind them all, last, in her apron, with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her raw red arms bare and a short plain knife in her hand, the kind you'd cut thread with at a mangle, Lotte Varga.

Severin let the stilling go.

---

It all came back at once.

The whole frozen ring crashed back into motion. Twenty Long-Lived on their knees suddenly screaming, scrambling, tangled in their own threads. The binders dropping their cushions. The two Lisle men finishing their lunge, toward the Keeper's knife hand, and finding it already moving.

Because the slack had ended.

My mother's heart beat. And the Keeper came out of it, unfrozen, free, with her palm still flat on my mother's breast and the silver knife in her other hand. She didn't look at the Lisle men. She didn't look at the Unwound pouring through her silver door. She looked at me.

*Willing, dear?*

*Yes,* I'd said.

She grabbed my wrist.

My right one, held up and out in silver. She seized it with the hand that held the knife, and dragged it toward the Stay, toward the braided cord rising from my mother's throat-ring, and turned the blade to open my palm and bleed me into the knot. Willing blood. Given. I'd said yes in front of the whole Inner Ledger. It would hold. I knew it would hold. I felt the Stay *want* it.

The knife touched my palm.

And stopped.

---

It stopped in the air, a hair's breadth from my skin, in her small white fist. Frozen. Not her hand; her hand was straining, I could see the tendons in her wrist go white with it, but the knife wouldn't move. Like a sword stilled in flight, three feet from the caster. *Six hours*, the Table of Hours said. Less, if you only held it for a heartbeat.

The Keeper turned her head.

At the edge of the ring, on his feet, in plum velvet, with his tin cuff pushed back off his arm and his hand held out toward the knife and his long freckled face white as milk, stood Hollis Penhallow.

A thin line of gold was running out of his count into his fingers. Out of six years.

"*Penhallow,*" said the Keeper.

"I'm terribly sorry about all this," said Hollis.

---

It was a heartbeat. That was all it could ever have been; it was all he could afford. But a heartbeat was enough.

Behind me, by the wall, Durrow roared.

I didn't see what happened. I couldn't turn my head that far. I heard it. Durrow's roar, and then a different sound, high and raw, a man in sudden pain, and the clatter of something long and thin falling on the stone. Later Bry told me. That while the ring was frozen, Evadne Thane had walked across the round room from the silver door to the wall where Durrow stood, step by step, the way you'd walk toward a deer in a wood, and stood beside him. And when the stilling broke and he moved his needle to my brother's wrist, she'd pulled the long silver pin out of her scraped-back hair and driven it, point first, with both hands and all her weight, through the back of his.

Then Bry got there.

"I've been *saving* this," said Bry Kettle.

I heard the punch. Everybody in that room heard the punch. It was the sound you'd hear if somebody dropped a side of beef off a bridge onto cobbles. I heard Durrow's nose go. I heard him go down, all of him, the whole red heavy length of him, onto the black stone, and not get up.

And then Kit's voice, high and terrified and *free*, shouting my name.

---

Severin reached me.

He came across the round room through the chaos, through the Long-Lived scrambling and screaming on their knees, through the Unwound and the Lisle men colliding at the edge of the light, Hob's cudgel, a Lisle man going down, Corwin Lisle in his white gloves coming at me from the other side, reaching for the silver threads on my wrists, and Magpie hitting him from behind at the knees like a dog. Severin came straight through it to the pool of light, to me, and went down on his knees in it, beside me.

The Keeper's hand was still on my wrist. The knife was moving again; Hollis's heartbeat was over. It was moving toward my palm.

Severin took my other wrist.

My left. The one with all the threads. His silver. Bry's black. My mother's, invisible. And hers. The Ledger thread, silver, cold, tied in this room with a small neat flat knot, a double loop with the tail tucked under.

He had Lotte's little knife in his left hand.

He looked at me. Just once. His face was so close. Grey eyes, the grey of tallow ink. White to the lips. Steady.

*A Ledger thread's got a slack the length of a blink.*

I felt him feel for it. Through his fingers on my wrist. The Keeper's heart, at the other end of the cold thread, slow, a corridor in a shut house. *Beat.* Mine, fast, frightened. *Beat. Beat.* And between them—

He cut.

It parted like wet paper.

The cold went out of me.

All at once. The heart like a clock in a cellar, the hunger at the other end of the line like a mouth, the thing that had been drinking from me every morning at the eighth bell for five weeks: gone. Like a hand let go of my wrist. Like a door shut on a cold room. I gasped. I couldn't help it. I gasped the way you gasp when you come up out of cold water.

And the Keeper of the Ledger screamed.

---

I'd never heard her raise her voice. Not once. Not in the Refectory, not on the Dial, not in the Glass Room with Pell on the tiles. It came out of her now high and raw and terrible, a sound like the clock tower itself tearing. She let go of my wrist. She ripped her bleeding palm off my mother's breast. She turned on her knees, in white, in the pool of light, with the silver knife in her fist and her honey-gold hair coming down out of its diadem.

And she grabbed Severin.

His left wrist. The bare one, with his count on it. With Lotte's knife still in that hand. She seized it in her small cold fingers the way she'd seized Lysander's on the Dial, and her brown eyes, gold-flecked, warm, met his.

"I told her, darling," said Lady Aurelia Thane. "If she leaked again, I'd find where it went."

She smiled. Both dimples.

"And I'd drink him."

---

She drew.

I felt it through his thread like a hook sunk into my own chest. Not a pull. A *tearing*. His life going out of him, out of his wrist, up into her fingers, years, all at once, the way I'd seen Joss's go, the way I'd seen Lysander's go on the Dial in a single breath.

I pushed.

I pushed everything I had down the silver thread into him as fast as she drank it out. And my mother came up through me, the well, from beside me, through my heart, through my wrist, into him. And out of him, into her. Through all of us. A river. Maren to me to Severin to the Keeper, pouring.

Above us, the pendulum swung. A long low arc through the dark over our heads with a sound like a ship's keel on stone. The threads screamed.

And still it wasn't enough.

Because she wasn't drinking only him.

Her other hand had gone out sideways, behind her, to the ring. To the threads. The Lines, running from the walls to the wrists of the kneeling Long-Lived, from the well to the Gilt. She seized a fistful of them. Five, six, silver, humming. And she pulled. Not on the well. On *them*.

Corisande Marchbank screamed. I saw her count go, all down her bare arm, two hundred years of gold, guttering, brown, grey. Lord Alder Ostrander, on his back on the stone where Fitch had laid him, made no sound at all. His arm went dark from his shoulder to his wrist in one breath, every line, all at once.

Like a row of candles blown out by a single breath.

Fitch Ostrander knelt beside his great-great-uncle on the stone and stared at him.

She was drinking her own. She was drinking the oldest families of Lanthorn, the Inner Ledger, the ones who'd knelt in her ring for two hundred years and drunk from her well and clapped when she said *justice*. She was emptying them into herself, to fill herself, to pour through her and down Severin's wrist and out of him faster than I could pour in.

Severin's knees went.

I felt his count. Through the thread. Going down. Seven. Six.

*Ours,* I thought, and pushed, and it wasn't enough.

---

And on the floor, under all of it, under the fight and the screaming and the swinging pendulum and the Keeper's white silk and Severin's falling body, someone was crawling.

Across the black stone on her elbows and knees, in her apron, with her raw red arms bare and a short plain knife in her teeth. Under a Lisle man's flailing arm. Past Durrow, flat on his back with his nose broken. Through the scattered velvet cushions of the binders. Into the pool of light.

To my mother.

Lotte Varga came up on her knees in front of my mother, where the Keeper had knelt. She took the knife out of her teeth. She looked at the Stay, rising braided and silver from the ring at my mother's throat into the dark, shivering, humming.

My mother looked down at her.

"Who are you?" said my mother.

"A laundress," said Lotte Varga.

She laid her big raw palm flat on my mother's breast, over her heart, where the Keeper's bleeding palm had been. I saw her close her eyes. Feeling for it. The beat. The silence after. The slack.

Then she reached up with the other hand, with the little knife, and laid it on the Stay.
