# Chapter Twelve

## Never Again the Well

::: {.pov}
Severin
:::

He'd been nine the last time he was lost under the Gilt.

He'd followed his grandmother down a stair in the Ledger House that nobody else used, on a Council day, because he'd wanted to see where she went. He'd lost her at the bottom in the dark, and turned the wrong way, and the wrong way again, and spent four hours in the Founders' tunnels with a candle that went out after one. He remembered sitting down in the black with his back against wet brick and his knees pulled up, not crying, because Thanes didn't, counting his own heartbeats to know how long he'd been there. He'd got to eleven thousand and something when he saw a light coming, and it was his father, with a lamp, and his father hadn't said anything at all. He'd just knelt down and put his arms around him in the dark.

*There you are, Sev.*

He thought about that now, going the other way, up the same tunnel with a lamp of his own and Kit's map in his pocket, twelve years later, eight years and some months on his arm, and the Founders' Charter somewhere above his head.

The tunnels under the Gilt were older than the ones under the river. The brick was smaller and darker, and in places it gave way to rock, rough-hewn, with the marks of picks still in it. They branched and joined and branched again, the way the alleys of the Sump did, with no plan anyone could see, as though the Founders had dug them in a hurry, each family on its own, each one wanting a way into the others' cellars. Here and there, iron gates. Here and there, a ladder going up into dark. Here and there, carved into the brick at the junctions in letters worn almost smooth, a name: *Lisle. Ostrander. Thane.*

He followed Kit's map. Kit had drawn it from his description, every turn he could remember, and then made him describe it all again from the other direction, and then drawn it a second time, and where the two maps disagreed he'd written *(?)* in very small letters. There were a lot of very small question marks.

He followed them anyway. A guess is better than nothing. A counter always guesses and then checks.

---

At the fourth bell, the thread at his wrist warmed.

*Hollis is in,* said Tamsin.

He stopped in the dark tunnel with the lamp in his hand. Her voice, small and clear, in his blood. She was in the Violet Rooms, at the top of the Gilt, behind a wall of new brick, and she was sitting at the window, he knew, with her hand around her wrist, listening down two threads at once.

That had been her idea. *Bry can talk to me down the black thread,* she'd said, in the dark, two nights ago, with her head on his chest. *She can pay the hours. She says she's got eighteen years and nothing better to spend them on. And I can talk to you, and it doesn't cost me anything.* A pause. *I'll be the middle. I'll be the string between two tins.*

*You'll be in Thane House.*

*I'm always in Thane House. I might as well be useful in it.*

*Hollis is in,* she said now. *Bry says he came in the old Penhallow carriage with a lame horse, and the footman at the door didn't know who he was, and he said "Penhallow," very loudly, and the footman said "Who?" and Hollis said "Exactly." Bry nearly dropped her tray.*

He stood in the black tunnel under the Gilt and laughed, once, silently.

*Bry and Pell are in the kitchens,* said Tamsin. *Wat's in the archive. Orrin's on the roof. He says the light-well's a long way down and he likes it.* A pause. *Your grandmother arrived at the third bell. She's wearing grey. Bry says she looks tired.*

He reached down into the silver thread and paid an hour.

*Good.*

---

It took him another hour to find the drain.

Kit's map had it right, mostly. The tunnel ran north under the third terrace, under the Ledger House, and came out at the bottom in a round brick sump where water had once collected and been pumped up to the house. The pump was long gone. There was an iron grate in the roof of the sump, rusted, set into the stone, and above it, he knew, if his memory and Kit's question marks were right, the lowest level of the Ledger House archive.

He set the lamp on the floor. He reached up and put his hands on the bars of the grate, and pushed.

It didn't move.

He'd known it might not. Rust, and three hundred years, and maybe somebody had mortared it shut. He looked at it for a long moment in the lamplight. Then he laid his palm flat against the iron where the hinge must be, under the rust, and closed his eyes.

An unwinding. Small. Not the iron; the rust. Run it backward a few hundred years, just here, just the hinge, till it was bright metal again and moved the way it had been made to move. He did the sum without wanting to. A month, perhaps. Six weeks.

*You've got a year more than you had when you came in. I counted. Spend some.*

He spent it.

The rust went. Not fast. It crept back off the hinge under his palm like frost retreating off a window, orange to brown to dull grey to the dark shine of new iron, and when it was done he pushed again and the grate swung up and over with a groan, and a fall of dust, and a cold breath of air that smelled of paper.

He climbed up into the archive of the Ledger House.

---

It went on forever.

That was what he remembered from being nine, and it was still true. The lowest level of the archive was a forest of shelves, iron stacks rising from a stone floor into the dark, row after row after row, going away in every direction further than his lamp could reach. And on the shelves, books. Ledgers. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Every one bound in the same grey cloth, every spine marked with a year and a month and a district in faded gold, every one full, he knew, from the first page to the last, of names and numbers. Every year bought and sold in Lanthorn for three hundred years, written down.

*Never sell twice in a season,* Tamsin had told him her mother used to say. *Somebody's always writing it down.*

Here was where they kept what they wrote.

He moved through the stacks with the lamp shuttered almost closed, a thin line of light on the floor in front of his boots. Somewhere above him, through three floors of stone, he could feel the Ledger House going about its Council day. A faint vibration in the floor. Feet. Voices, too far away to make out. And once, very faintly, the sound of a bell: the Council chamber's bell, calling the session to order.

At the end of a long aisle, a light.

Not his. A candle, on a high clerk's desk at the end of the row, and a skinny figure perched on a stool behind it with a pen in its hand and its nose almost on the page. The figure looked up as his lamp came round the end of the stack. A long nose. Inky fingers. Nervous eyes that went everywhere at once.

"You took your *time*," said Wat Sorrel, in an agonized whisper.

---

"The Keeper opened it at the half-hour," Wat said. He was leading the way through the stacks at a half-run, bent double, his candle cupped in his hand. "Like always. Council day. They all come down, all seven delegates, in a line, and she opens the big doors with the key round her neck, and they go in and look at the seals and say the words, and they come back up." He was breathing fast. "And she leaves it open. That's what I told Bry. She always leaves it open, the whole session, because they might want to go back down. Nobody ever does."

"Who's on the door?"

"Two of her men. In black. At the top of the vault stairs, by the great doors. Not at the bottom." Wat stopped at the end of a row and pointed. "Down there. Past the old stacks. Where the floor goes down."

Severin looked.

The archive ended. The last row of iron shelves stood against a wall of stone, older stone, darker, and in the wall there was an arch, and through the arch a short flight of steps going down, wide and shallow and worn into dips by three hundred years of Keepers' feet. At the bottom of the steps, a pair of doors. Bronze. Green with age. Taller than a man. Standing open.

And above them, carved into the stone of the arch in letters three feet high, filled in with gold that caught even the faint light of Wat's candle and threw it back:

*NEVER AGAIN THE WELL.*

He'd stood under those letters a hundred times, at the top of the stairs, in the great hall, holding his grandmother's hand. He'd never been on this side of them. He'd never known they were carved twice. Once above the Council chamber, for the Gilt to see. Once down here, above the vault, where nobody saw but the Keeper.

"I'll be at my desk," whispered Wat. "If anyone asks, I never saw you, and I've been copying the Saltmarket ledgers for Frostmonth all morning, which is true, they're very dull." He swallowed. "Be quick, Thane. Please."

He went.

---

The thread warmed.

*Bry says the session's started,* said Tamsin. *Your grandmother's talking about the Renewal. Hollis is sitting very straight and looking at the ceiling. Bry says Lord Crane didn't come; he sent his steward, and when your grandmother asked where he was, the steward said he was "indisposed by grief," and nobody said anything for a long time.*

Lysander's father. He'd wondered.

*Bry says Pell's about to drop a tray,* said Tamsin. *Whenever you need him to. He's very nervous. He says he's never dropped one on purpose before.*

He reached down into the thread and paid two hours.

*Not yet.*

*Not yet,* said Tamsin. A pause. *Severin. Be careful.*

He didn't answer that. He didn't have to. He started down the steps under the gold letters toward the bronze doors, with his lamp shuttered to a thread of light, and the thread on his wrist warm with her listening.

---

The steps were wider than they looked from the top. Seven of them. He counted, because she would have. At the bottom, the bronze doors stood open on darkness, and there was a smell coming out of the darkness that he didn't expect: not dust, not paper. Something older. Stone and cold and, under it, faintly, like a memory of something burnt, the smell of a working cast a very long time ago.

He'd imagined the vault for three days. A room of treasures, he'd imagined. Gold. Relics. The Founders' swords, maybe; the Seven had been fighting families before they were anything else.

He stopped on the last step, just outside the doors, with his hand on the cold green bronze.

There was light inside.

Not his lamp. His was shuttered to a thread. Not Wat's candle, which was far behind him in the stacks. This was another lamp. Inside the vault. Moving. A yellow glow sliding across the stone floor beyond the doors, and back, the way a lamp moves when somebody's carrying it and searching for something.

And a sound. Soft. Paper. The dry whisper of old vellum being turned.

He stood very still on the last step under *never again the well*, with the thread at his wrist warm and listening and the bronze cold under his hand.

The vault was already open.

Someone else was inside.
