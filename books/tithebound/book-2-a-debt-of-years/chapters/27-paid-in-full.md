# Chapter Twenty-Seven

## Paid in Full

::: {.pov}
Severin
:::

He was a pipe.

That was the only way he'd ever be able to describe it afterward, and he never did, to anyone but her. A pipe, with a river going through it. His grandmother's small cold fingers clamped on his left wrist, drinking, a hook sunk into the middle of him and pulling, and his life going out of him into her, years, faster than he could count them. And down the silver thread on the same wrist, from the girl kneeling in silver beside him with her face white and her teeth bared, years coming *in*. Hers. Her mother's. The well's. Pouring into him as fast as they poured out, faster, slower, faster, so that he couldn't tell anymore which way anything was going, only that it was all going *through* him, scalding, like boiling water through a cold pipe in winter.

He was on his knees. He didn't remember going down. The black stone was warm under them, wet with the light that ran off the well.

*Six,* said the counter in his head. Her voice, not his. *Five.*

*Seven,* said the river coming in.

*Five.*

Behind his grandmother, the ring of the Inner Ledger was screaming. She had her other hand thrown back behind her, fist closed round a bundle of the Lines, the silver threads that ran out through the walls to the great houses of the Gilt, and she was pulling on them. Not on the well. On them. He could see it past her shoulder, past her white silk and her honey-gold hair coming down. Corisande Marchbank on her knees with her arm going dark from the shoulder down. A Vey he'd danced the Turning with at three Long Nights, slumped forward over his own thread. Lord Alder Ostrander on his back on the stone with his arm grey from end to end and his eyes open and Fitch kneeling beside him, not touching him.

She was drinking her own house to drink him.

*Five,* said the counter. *Four.*

He could feel Tamsin feel it. Through the thread. Her heart going like a hammer, and the well coming up through her in a flood, and her pushing it all into him, everything, and still the count going down, because his grandmother had two hundred years of practice at this and twenty Long-Lived at her back to fuel her and he had a splinted hand and a girl.

*Ours,* he thought. It was the last clear thing he thought for a while.

And then, past his grandmother's shoulder, in the middle of the light, he saw Lotte.

---

She was kneeling in front of the well.

Where his grandmother had knelt. In the pool of gold, in her apron, with her sleeves rolled and her raw red arms bare. Her left palm was flat on the well's breast, over her heart. Her eyes were closed. Her right hand was raised, and in it was the short plain knife she'd pressed into his own left hand at the chandlery and closed his fingers round, or one just like it, and the blade was resting on the Stay.

The well was looking down at her. The well, Tamsin's mother, with her grey-dark hair and her arms of light, looking down at a laundress she'd never seen before with an expression on her thin face that he couldn't read. Not fear. Something gentler. As though she knew exactly what she was looking at, and was sorry.

Lotte's lips were moving. Counting, he thought. Under her breath. The way she counted in the steam-house, with her hand on a nail and Abel's on the other, waiting for the slack.

His grandmother didn't see her. His grandmother had her back to the well and her eyes on his face, drinking, smiling, with her dimples.

He felt the well's heart beat. Through the Stay. Through the stone. Through Tamsin's thread. Slow, deep, a long beat going up the braided cord past Lotte's knife into the dark of the shaft.

And then the silence after it.

Going out.

And out.

He watched Lotte open her eyes.

---

She cut.

It didn't part like wet paper. Not the Stay. It parted like the world ending.

There was a sound. He'd hear it for the rest of his life, at night, in the small hours, in the moment before sleep. Not a snap. Bigger than a snap. A crack, deep and enormous and *final*, as though the biggest string in the world had been cut through under more strain than any string was ever meant to bear, and it went up. Up the cut cord into the shaft, up through six hundred and forty feet of dark, up past the oak plank still swinging on Orrin's rope, up through the grate in the floor of a room full of clocks, up into the works.

And the whole tower answered.

He felt it in the stone under his knees. A shudder, starting at the top, three hundred feet of rock above them and three hundred more above that, coming down. Like something enormous waking up and stretching. The black stone walls of the round room groaned. Dust came down out of the dark in long grey sheets. Somewhere far above, very faint, he heard something give way that had been held for three hundred years.

The escapement. The fork and the wheel. Caught. Released.

The pendulum swung.

Not a shiver. Not a twitch. A *swing*. The great black disc of iron above the well went out over their heads in a long, slow, enormous arc, through the dark, so close above him that he felt the wind of it in his hair. Out. To the very edge of the room. And paused there, at the top of its swing, for one held breath.

And came back.

*TICK.*

It came down the shaft like the sky falling. Like a door the size of the tower slamming shut on the world. He felt it in his teeth and his ribs and the backs of his eyes. And then, very faint, very far above, filtering down the shaft from the open grate and the open air beyond it, another sound.

Bells.

Not one. All of them. Every bell in Lanthorn, from the bronze alarm in the Horologe's own belfry to the cracked tin bell of the chapel in the Sump, ringing and ringing, all at once, all out of time with each other, like every clock in his room at once.

He didn't need to see the clock face to know where the hand was.

---

The threads fell.

All of them. The silver threads that had bound the well for ten years, from her wrists and her ankles and her throat up into the dark, to the works that weren't holding anything anymore. They went slack. All at once. And dropped, out of the dark, falling past the swinging pendulum in long silver coils like hair cut off and thrown down, and lay on the black stone in the light.

The Lines burned.

He saw it. All round the round room, where the threads ran out of the walls to the wrists of the kneeling Long-Lived, the threads flared. White. A line of white fire running along each one from the wrist to the wall, fast as a spark running down a fuse, and into the stone, and through it, gone. Out through the walls, toward the Gilt, toward the great houses on their white terraces, burning. He'd learn later that in every house of the Inner Ledger, in a cellar or a closet or a locked room, a silver thread had come out of the wall that morning in a line of white fire and vanished into smoke. He saw it now. He saw the Lines go out like candles in a gust, and the ring of the Long-Lived jerk on their knees as they went.

And the well fell forward.

All the threads that had held her arms up for ten years were gone, and she had nothing to hold her, and she fell forward out of the light, the way you'd fall if someone cut the strings of a puppet. And Tamsin caught her.

He saw her do it. Her own threads had gone too; they'd been tied to the same works. Her arms came down, free, and she caught her mother against her chest as she fell, both arms round her, and held on, and the two of them went down together on the black stone in the light, and Tamsin was making a sound he'd never heard anyone make.

---

His grandmother's fingers opened on his wrist.

Not because she let go. Because she *couldn't hold*. He felt it happen through the thread: the hook in the middle of him, the pull, the drinking, all at once going slack, like a hand that has been holding a rope suddenly finding the rope has been cut. She'd been drinking him *through* something. Through the Stay. Through the well. Through her own blood on the knot. And the knot was gone.

She knelt in front of him in her white silk, in the pool of light, with her small hand open in the air where his wrist had been, and her honey-gold hair down round her shoulders, and the silver diadem of clock hands crooked on her head.

She looked down at her own arm.

It was smoking.

He didn't have a better word for it. Gold, coming off her arm. Through the white silk sleeve. Like steam off a kettle. Like breath on a cold morning. A thin, steady, shining smoke rising from her count, from the long blaze of it from her wrist to her shoulder, drifting up off her into the air of the round room, toward the shaft, toward the swinging pendulum and the dark above it.

Going home.

She stared at it.

All round the ring, it was the same. Corisande, on her knees with her dark arm held out in front of her, and from what was left of her count, gold smoke rising. The Crane steward. The Vey he'd danced with. Ismay Lisle, on her feet now at the edge of the light with her white fur torn and her fox's face perfectly still, staring down at her own arm with gold smoke coming off it like incense. All of them. Every one of the Long-Lived in that room, and, he'd learn later, every one of them in the city. A thin shining smoke rising from their arms, all at once, into the air, and drifting away.

*Every hour that was ever bought,* his grandmother had said, over chocolate, in a grey wrapper, *goes back to the one who sold it.*

Not all at once. Not yet. A sixtieth part, for each minute the hand walked. The rest at midnight.

He looked at his own arm.

No smoke. Gold. Lines of it. Twelve, and a little.

---

"*In the name of the Ledger!*"

A voice from the silver door. Boots on the stone. Grey coats. Captain Hesketh Marr came through the parted door with his twelve men behind him, at a run, with his hat gone and his long jaw set and his washed-out eyes taking in the round room in one sweep: the swinging pendulum, the fallen threads, the Long-Lived smoking on their knees, Durrow on his back with his nose broken and a silver hairpin through his hand, and the Keeper of the Ledger kneeling in white in the middle of it all with gold coming off her arm.

"Sergeant Kell Durrow," said Marr, loudly, to the whole room, "you are under arrest for the murders of eleven tithes of the Meridian and the abduction of a child."

He looked at the Keeper.

"My lady," he said.

He didn't say anything else. Severin watched him not say it. Watched him stand there in the silver doorway with twelve men behind him and look at the woman in white, and try, and not know how.

His grandmother stood up.

She did it slowly, gracefully, the way she did everything. She brushed off her white silk with one small hand. She straightened the diadem of clock hands on her head. She looked down at Severin on his knees in front of her, and at the girl on the stone beyond him holding the well, and at the laundress kneeling in the light with a short plain knife in her hand. Then she looked at Marr.

She lifted her hand.

The gold she'd drunk from the Inner Ledger went out of her in a wave. He felt it, the way you feel a door blow open in a gale. Not at him. At the doorway. At Marr and his twelve. And they stopped. Every one of them. Frozen in the silver door in their grey coats, Marr with his mouth open and his hand half lifted, a stilling cast by a woman with two hundred and forty-seven years on her arm and twenty Long-Lived emptied into her, and it held them like flies in amber.

She walked between them.

She didn't run. She walked, in white, with her hair down, with gold smoke rising off her arm in a thin shining stream behind her, out through her silver door, past the frozen Greycoats, into the carved passage. Toward her stair.

At the door, she stopped, and looked back.

Not at him. At the girl on the floor with the well in her arms.

Then she was gone.

---

He didn't follow her.

He couldn't have stood up if the tower had been falling, and it felt as though it might be. He went on his hands and knees across the black stone, through the light, through the fallen threads lying in silver coils, past the swinging pendulum's shadow going over him and back, to where Lotte Varga lay.

She'd fallen sideways. When the Stay parted. She lay on her back on the stone beside the well, in the light, with her eyes open, looking up into the dark at the pendulum swinging out and back over her. The little knife was still in her right hand. Her left arm lay on the stone beside her, palm up.

Dark.

From the elbow to the wrist. Every line. All eleven. Not brown at the ends, not guttering, not one going and then the next. All at once. All together. The way he'd seen it on Joss's arm on the flagstones under the clock, on the second morning of the term.

*Paid in full.*

But she was breathing.

Barely. Short and shallow and far apart, the way the last of a count goes, the last few minutes at the wrist that you can't see anymore. He got his arm under her shoulders and lifted her, and her head came against his chest, and she was so heavy, a laundress's weight, thirty years of hauling wet sheets on poles.

Her eyes moved. They found his face.

"Did it go," said Lotte Varga.

"It went." His voice wasn't working properly. "It went. The hand moved. Every bell in the city. The threads fell. She's free. Mrs. Varga, she's *free*—"

"Good," said Lotte.

Her right hand let go of the knife. It moved, slowly, across her apron, to the pocket. The buttoned one. She couldn't undo the button. He did it for her, with his good hand, and she reached in, and her fingers closed on something, and she brought it out and pressed it into his palm, and closed his fingers round it.

Small. Gold. Cheap gold plate. Warm from her apron. The crystal smashed into a star. An *L* worn almost smooth on the case.

Ticking.

"Yours," said Lotte. "Always was. Really." Her eyes were on his face. "She'd have wanted you to have it. Wet heron."

He couldn't speak.

She looked up at him for a long moment, in the gold light, with the pendulum going over them and back, and her face, her face with Liesl's eyes in it, changed. Not much. A little. The way a hard thing softens when the wind finally drops.

"Sev," said Lotte Varga.

The name only one person had ever called him by in that black house after his father died. The name a girl of twelve had given him on the back stairs, with a stolen pack of cards.

Then her eyes stayed open, and stopped seeing him, and in his closed hand the little watch went on ticking.
