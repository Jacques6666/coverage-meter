# Chapter Twenty-Eight

## Mama

::: {.pov}
Tamsin
:::

She weighed nothing.

That was the first thing. I'd caught her when the threads fell, both arms round her, and we'd gone down together on the black stone, and I'd braced for her weight the way you brace for a sack of coal, and there wasn't any. She was all bones and light. Like holding a bird. Like holding a lamp.

I knelt in the pool of gold on the floor of the round room with my mother in my arms and her head on my shoulder and her grey-dark hair over my face, and above us the pendulum swung. Out, slow, enormous, into the dark. And back. And out. Every time it reached the top of its swing, far above us in the works, something caught and released, and the whole tower went *tick*, and I felt it in my teeth.

She was breathing. Short, and shallow, but breathing.

"Mama," I said. "Mama. You're out. The threads are gone. Look. Look at your hands."

She lifted one.

Slowly. As if she'd forgotten how. Her left hand, the one with the crooked little finger, came up off the stone, free, with no silver thread on the wrist, nothing, only a raw red ring in the skin where the thread had been for ten years. She held it up in front of her face in her own light and looked at it.

Then she put it on my cheek.

"Tamsin," she said.

I put my face in her neck and cried the way I hadn't cried since I was nine.

---

Her arms were still light. That surprised me, later, when I could think about it. I'd thought, somehow, that when the threads fell, the well would go out of her too, the way a lamp goes out when you cut the wick. It didn't. Her arms still glowed from her fingertips to her shoulders, gold, continuous, no lines, no ladder. But dimmer. Steadier. Not pouring off her onto the stone anymore like something overflowing. Just *there*. Like a lamp turned down low, the way you'd turn one down for a sleeping child.

*It's still in me,* she said, much later, in a quieter place. *It'll always be in me. Until it isn't. That's the way of it.*

But there in the round room, I didn't know that. I only knew she was warm, and light as a bird, and her hand was on my face.

And then she lifted her head off my shoulder, and looked past me, and her whole body went still.

---

Kit was standing at the edge of the light.

Bry had got him. And Pell. I'd heard the punch, and Durrow going down, and Kit's voice shouting my name, but I hadn't seen. Now he was standing at the edge of the pool of gold where it lapped against the black stone, in his too-big coat with the sleeves rolled up four times, with Bry's taped hand on his shoulder and Pell's huge one on the other, and he wasn't moving.

His face was white under the soot. His eyes were enormous.

He was looking at our mother.

She looked back at him.

Nobody said anything. All round us, the round room was full of noise, the Long-Lived crying out, the Unwound shouting to each other, Marr's men in the doorway coming back to life one by one as the Keeper's stilling wore thin behind her and fell away. None of it reached the pool of light. Inside it, there was only the pendulum, swinging, and the tick, and the two of them looking at each other.

Bry took her hand off his shoulder. So did Pell.

Kit stepped into the light.

He came slowly. Step by step. The way Evadne had walked toward her son in the cellar. The way you'd walk toward a deer in a wood. Across the gold, through the fallen silver threads lying on the stone in long coils, past the place where the Keeper had knelt, until he was standing over us. Then he knelt down, too, on the stone, in front of her, in his too-big coat, and looked at her.

"Mama," said Kit.

She lifted her hand off my cheek. The left one. With the crooked finger. She reached out with it, slowly, and touched his face. His soot-streaked cheek. His singed hair. His eyes.

"You drew this room," said my mother.

Kit didn't move.

"Tamsin told me," she said. "Down the thread. In the dark. Every night. She said you drew the Meridian, from my stories. And under the tower, down in the rock, you drew a heart." Her thumb moved on his cheek. "*Where the clock keeps its heart.*"

Kit's face crumpled.

I reached inside my shirt.

It was still there. It had been there every day since Tithe Day. Against my ribs, under my scratchy grey wool, under the white silk of the Turning, folded small and soft as cloth from being opened and closed and carried. The first map. The one he'd pulled out from under his pillow on the morning they sold me, in the dark, on Tallow Lane. *So you don't get lost.*

I held it out to him.

"After," I said. My voice didn't work properly. "You said I had to give it back. After."

He took it. His hands were shaking. He unfolded it, there, on his knees in the gold light, and there it was. The black island. The long bridge with its twenty-four tiny careful lamps. The tower, with its clock face, with the single hand at eleven. The little gulls. And under the tower, down in the rock where nobody could see, a small circle, with a heart in it.

He held it up. Not to me. To her.

"I drew you," said Kit. "I didn't know. I didn't *know* it was you."

My mother looked at the map in his shaking hands for a long time. At the heart. Then at his face.

"I knew it was you," said my mother. "Every time Tamsin said your name. Every single time. Down the thread." She smiled. Her thin, grey, ten-years-under-the-clock face, and the smile went all the way through it like light through a window. "Thirty-one years, she said. And maps. And a watch. And a cough." Her voice broke. "I left you with ten."

"I know," said Kit. "Tam told me. She said you were coming back."

"I was."

"You *did*," said Kit fiercely, and threw himself at her, all of him, the way he'd thrown himself at me in the Glass Room, and she caught him, she caught him with both her freed arms, and held him against her, against both of us, and the three of us knelt there on the floor of the round room in the gold light under the swinging pendulum with the map crushed between us, and nobody said anything at all for a very long time.

---

When I lifted my head, the round room had changed.

Marr's men were moving. The stilling had worn off them; they'd come out of it in the silver doorway one by one, blinking, staggering, and now they were in the room, in their grey coats, among the Long-Lived. Two of them had Durrow up off the floor between them, with his hands bound behind him and his nose spread across his red face and Evadne's silver hairpin still through the back of one hand, and he was moaning. Marr stood over him, with his hat gone and his long jaw set, saying something low and steady that I couldn't hear.

The Lisle men were gone. Ismay Lisle was gone. Corwin. I hadn't seen them go. Out through the silver door after the Keeper, someone said later, in the confusion, before Marr's men woke. Or down the old Lisle tunnel that ran under the Gilt and nobody had known came out here.

The Long-Lived were still on their knees.

Most of them. Some had got to their feet and stood swaying at the edge of the light with their cuffs off and their arms held out in front of them, staring. Some were sitting. Corisande Marchbank was sitting on the stone with her back against the wall, in her sapphires, with her arm dark from the shoulder halfway to the wrist and only a little gold left at the bottom, crying in a thin high voice like a child. And Fitch Ostrander was kneeling beside his great-great-uncle on the floor, holding his dead hand, not crying at all.

And from every one of them, from every arm in the ring, gold was rising.

A thin shining smoke. Steady. Like steam off a kettle. Drifting up off their counts into the air of the round room and up toward the shaft, toward the pendulum, toward the dark. Not fast. Not much. A little. You'd have had to look to see it. But it didn't stop.

"Is it going to stop?" said Corisande Marchbank. To nobody. To the room. In that thin child's voice. "Somebody tell me. Is it going to stop?"

Nobody answered her.

---

I found Severin by the wall.

He was sitting on the black stone just outside the pool of light, with his back against the carved clock faces and Lotte Varga across his lap. He'd closed her eyes. He had one arm round her shoulders and her head against his chest, and his splinted hand lay on her apron, and his left hand was closed tight round something small.

He wasn't crying. His face wasn't doing anything at all. He was just sitting there, holding her, looking at the swinging pendulum.

Evadne was kneeling beside him. She wasn't touching him. She had one hand held out, an inch from his shoulder, not quite resting on it, the way you'd hold your hand out to a fire to feel if it's still warm.

I knelt on his other side.

He didn't look at me. But through the silver thread, I felt him feel me there. His heart went from nothing to something. Slow. Very slow.

"Her eyes," he said. Very quietly. To the pendulum. "She had Liesl's eyes. I never told her that."

"She knew," I said.

He opened his left hand.

The watch. Small, gold, cheap gold plate. The crystal smashed into a star. Ticking.

"She gave it to me," he said. "She said it was always mine. Really." His voice did something strange. "She said *wet heron*."

I didn't say anything. I put my hand over his, over the watch, and held it there.

And on his other side, after a long moment, Evadne Thane let her hand come down, at last, the last inch, onto her son's shoulder.

He didn't flinch. He didn't move at all. But I felt it through the thread. Something going out of him, very slowly, that had been held for fourteen years.

---

We went out through the cisterns.

Not the Keeper's stair. The Keeper had gone up it, in white, with gold smoke trailing off her arm, and nobody wanted to follow her. Out through the silver door, still standing parted, and down the carved passage with its clock faces, past the little square room with the iron rings where Bry had sat chained, down the steps into the black water.

Pell carried my mother.

He'd come to the pool of light and knelt in it beside us without a word and held out his huge arms, and my mother had looked at him, at his swollen gentle face, purple and green, and said, "Who are you?" And Pell had said, "I bake," and my mother had laughed, a real laugh, cracked and surprised, and let him lift her. He carried her against his chest the way he'd carried Bry off the floor of the Lantern Theatre, as though she weighed nothing, which she nearly didn't.

And as he waded into the cistern with her, into the black water up to his waist, he began to sing.

Anni's hymn. The one about the lamb. Every verse. His big gentle voice going out across the flooded dark between the pillars, and coming back off them, smaller, and smaller, and smaller, the way my gasp had come back the first time I'd waded in there alone.

My mother's arms lit the cistern.

I'd lit it with my count, the night I came for Bry. It had been bright. It had been nothing like this. She lay against Pell's chest with her arms across her body, glowing, gold from fingertip to shoulder, and the light went out across the black water in every direction at once and lit the pillars, all of them, row after row going away into the dark, every one of them standing up out of the water like trees in a flooded forest at sunrise.

Kit waded beside them with one hand holding hers.

I walked behind them. Bry beside me, with Magpie. Then the Unwound, Hob, Vadra, all of them. Hollis, leaning on the arm of the tall young man in Vey green, who had his book held up out of the water over his head and was looking round at the lit forest of pillars with his mouth slightly open, as though he'd never seen anything so beautiful and would certainly be writing a poem about it later. Evadne, holding her grey skirts up out of the water, not looking at the dark at all.

And last, alone, with Lotte Varga in his arms, Severin.

---

Steward Rook was at the top of the stairs.

At the small iron door at the end of the Tithe Hall, with the cut chain hanging and the bar leaning against the wall, with her lamp held up and her great ring of keys in her other hand and her milky eye catching the light. She'd been standing there since dawn. I knew she had. She'd said she would.

She counted us.

Out loud. One by one. As we came up the seventy-three steps out of the dark into the Hall, wet to the waist, she touched each of us on the shoulder as we passed her, and said a number.

"One," she said, to Pell, with my mother in his arms. And then she looked at my mother. At her arms. At the light. And her good eye went wide, and the milky one roved over my mother's face, and she said, very softly, "Burn me. So *that's* what was below the Hall." And then, pulling herself together: "Two." Kit. "Three." Me.

On and on. Bry. Magpie. Hollis. Ambrose Vey. Hob. Vadra. The Unwound, one by one.

"Nineteen. Twenty."

Severin came up the steps last, with Lotte in his arms.

Rook looked at him. At the woman he was carrying. At her arm, dark from the elbow to the wrist, hanging down.

She didn't say anything for a long moment.

Then she reached out and laid her hand, very gently, on Lotte Varga's shoulder.

"And one," said Agathe Rook. "Every one. I said I'd count every one back." Her voice was like gravel. "And I have."

---

They laid my mother on Rook's own bed, the one at the end of the Hall by the brazier, the only one in that long row of stripped iron frames that still had a blanket on it. Rook built up the fire herself. Kit sat on the edge of the bed with our mother's hand in both of his and wouldn't let go, and she didn't want him to, and after a while she fell asleep, with her arms glowing faintly on the grey blanket and her crooked finger curled round his, and he sat there and watched her breathe.

They laid Lotte on the next bed. Anni's. With a grey blanket over her, up to the chin, and her hands folded on top of it. Severin did it. He wouldn't let anyone help.

Then he went and sat down on the stone floor by the iron door, with his back against the wall and his knees up, and didn't move.

I went and sat beside him.

I didn't say anything for a while. Then I reached under the neck of the white silk, which was soaked to the waist now and grey with cistern water, and found the plain grey thread, and lifted it over my head. The ring swung on it. Heavy. Old. Silver gone dark, with a clock hand worn almost smooth on its face.

I broke the thread. I took his left hand, the one that wasn't splinted, the one that had cut my Ledger thread in the slack of a blink an hour ago, and I put his father's ring on it.

He looked at it for a long time.

"From your mother," I said. "And your father. She said she's sorry it took so long."

He turned his hand over, so the light from the brazier caught the inside of the band. I watched him read it. *A.T. — Every Thane needs a way out.*

"I found one," he said, very quietly.

---

Rook was standing by the brazier.

She'd been building up the fire. She'd stopped. She was standing with the poker in one hand and her other arm held out in front of her in the firelight, with the sleeve of her grey wrapper pushed back to the elbow, looking at it.

At her count. The few short lines of it, up her forearm. Thirty-one years of other people's.

And from them, faint, so faint you'd have to know to look, a thin shining smoke. Rising. Drifting up off her arm in the firelight and away toward the vaulted ceiling of the Tithe Hall, toward the green slots of the windows, toward the river and the island and the clock.

Going home.

She stood there and watched it for a long time.

Then she pulled her sleeve back down over it, and went on building up the fire, and didn't say anything at all.
