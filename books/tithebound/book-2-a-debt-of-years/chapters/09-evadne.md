# Chapter Nine

## Evadne

::: {.pov}
Tamsin
:::

*Learn the house*, Pell's note had said.

So I learned it.

I'd been learning it for three weeks, really, without knowing that was what I was doing. Counting doors. Counting stairs. Counting the steps from the Violet Rooms to the Glass Room (two hundred and six) and from the Glass Room to the long gallery (ninety-one) and from the long gallery to the top of the great stair (forty-four). Counting the guards: two at the door of my rooms, day and night, polite, the one I'd bitten and a younger one with a stammer; two more at the head of every staircase that went down; four at the great front doors, and more at the gates, and more on the walls of the gardens, in the snow, with their breath smoking. Counting the windows that had bars on the outside you couldn't see from the inside.

All of them did.

But Pell's note had come from Severin, and Severin had grown up in this house, and he hadn't said *count the guards*. He'd said *learn the house*. As if there were something in it to learn.

So after the eleventh bell, when the house went quiet, I got up out of the enormous bed and put on the plain grey wrapper Nessa had found me, the only thing in the Violet Rooms that wasn't silk, and went walking.

Nobody stopped me. The guard with the bandaged hand opened my door and bowed and said, "Good evening, miss," and fell in behind me at a distance, the way he always did. I was allowed anywhere in the house. She'd said so. *Where would you go?*

I went everywhere.

---

Thane House at night was a different house.

By day it was black stone and gilt and mirrors and violets, servants moving silently along the corridors with trays, the Keeper's voice somewhere, light and warm. At night it was a cave. The lamps in the corridors were turned down to blue beads, and the black stone drank what little light they gave, so that you walked in pools of dark between them, and the portraits on the walls watched you go by with eyes you couldn't see. It was cold. It was always cold, away from the fires; the stone held the winter in it like a cellar. And it was never quite silent. Water ticked in pipes somewhere inside the walls. The wind found its way in through some gap and moved along the corridors like breathing.

And there were drafts.

That was the first thing I noticed, on the first night of walking. I'd stop in a corridor under a portrait and feel it on my ankles, a thin cold stream of air, coming from nowhere. From the wall. From the dark panelling between two pictures, where there wasn't a door, or a window, or anything at all.

I'd stand there with my hand flat on the wood, feeling the cold come through the seam, and the guard would stop a little way off and wait, politely, the way you'd wait for a madwoman.

On the third night, I heard music.

---

It came from the far end of the west wing, past the long gallery, down a corridor I hadn't walked yet. Three notes. The same three, over and over, slow, on an instrument that was out of tune, a high plinking jangle like a music box that had been dropped. Three notes, and a pause, and the same three notes again. Not a tune. A piece of one. The beginning of something that never got any further.

I followed it.

The corridor ended in a pair of doors, one of them half open. Light came through the gap, a single candle, yellow. I pushed the door wider and looked in.

A music room. Small, for Thane House, with a painted ceiling of clouds and cherubs gone grey with dust, and dust sheets over most of the furniture, and in the middle of the floor, under the only candle, a harpsichord. Old. Its lid was propped open and half its strings were broken; I could see them curling up out of the case like dead vines.

And at the keyboard sat the grey woman.

The one from the end of the long gallery. The one the Keeper had called *furniture*. She was sitting very straight on the bench in her plain grey dress, with her hands on the keys and her eyes closed, playing the same three notes. Over and over. Her face in the candlelight was pale and fine-boned and very tired, and older than I'd thought in the gallery, or not older exactly. Worn. Like stone that water has run over for a long time.

She stopped playing. She opened her eyes and looked at me in the doorway.

I knew where I'd seen the eyes before. I'd known in the gallery, almost. I knew now. Grey. Clear. The exact grey of tallow ink.

"You're the girl he bought," she said.

---

Her name was Evadne Thane, and she was his mother, and she was alive.

I stood in the doorway of the music room and couldn't move. I'd known him a whole winter. I'd sat beside him at a velvet table every night for weeks. I'd held his bare arm with its nine gold lines and heard about Liesl and the long gallery and the hand over his on the thread. I'd stood in his boy's room at the top of the east wing with my count lighting up the shelves of clock parts. I'd been in his *house*.

He'd never once said his mother was alive.

"He doesn't speak of me," said Evadne Thane, watching my face. "I know. I'd be surprised if he did." She looked down at the keys. "Come in, if you like. Shut the door. The guard can wait outside; he's used to waiting outside doors for me. Aren't you, Corran?"

"Yes, my lady," said the guard behind me, very quietly, and shut the door himself.

---

I sat on a dust sheet over something that turned out to be a sofa. She stayed on the harpsichord bench, turned sideways now, with her thin hands folded in her grey lap. The candle burned between us on the lid of the instrument.

"I was a Crane," she said. "Before. My sister married a Crane cousin and had a golden boy called Lysander, and I married Aldric Thane, who was the Keeper's heir, and had a boy called Severin." She said both names the same way, flatly, carefully, like someone stepping around broken glass. "You knew my nephew, I think."

"Yes."

"I'm told he's dead."

"Yes," I said. "She drained him. On the Dial. In front of everyone."

Evadne Thane closed her eyes for a moment. Then she opened them.

"Yes," she said. "That's how she does it."

I waited.

"Aldric was soft," said Evadne. "That's what she called it. He didn't like to draw. He'd do it, when he had to, when she was watching, but he didn't *like* it, and she could always tell. She could always tell what people liked." She looked at her hands. "He began to hide things from her. Small things. A tithe he'd send home early, with a few years left. A Wick boy he'd paid a broker to release. She found out. She always finds out." A pause. "Severin was seven. She had Aldric brought to the long gallery, and the boy too, and she told the boy to watch. And she took Aldric's wrist."

The candle flame bent in some draft I couldn't feel.

"She said it was a lesson," said Evadne Thane. "For the boy. So he'd understand what happens to Thanes who are soft."

I had my hand over my mouth. I didn't remember putting it there.

"The long gallery," I said, through my fingers.

"Oh, yes. She's very fond of the long gallery. The light in the afternoon is quite something." Evadne's mouth twisted. "And afterward, she came to me, and she said, *Evadne, dear, the boy needs a firmer hand than yours. I'll take his upbringing from now on.* And I let her."

She looked at me across the candle.

"I let her," she said again. "I gave him to her. I thought, if he's *hers*, if she raises him, if he's her heir and her project and her pride, she won't do to him what she did to his father. I thought it would keep him alive." She laughed, a small terrible sound. "And it did. It kept him alive. And nine years later she stood him in the same gallery with a Wick girl on his thread and put her hand over his, and made him—" She stopped.

"I know," I said. "He told me."

"Did he." She looked at me for a long moment. "He told you that. And not that I was alive."

"He said—" I tried to remember. *She was the only person in that house who ever called me Sev.* Liesl. Not his mother. He'd talked about Liesl as though she'd been the only person in the whole black house who'd ever been kind to him, and I'd believed it, and I'd never once asked who else lived there.

"He thinks I gave him up," said Evadne. "And I did. I've never told him why. What would I say? *I gave you to her to save your life, and she made you kill a girl with your own hands anyway?*" She shook her head. "I've been furniture in this house for fourteen years. It's what I'm good at. I sit in the music room and play the first three notes of a song Aldric used to play, because I can't remember the rest of it, and she walks past me in the corridors and doesn't see me. It's safer. For everyone." She looked at her thin hands. "I don't drink, you know. The years. She offers. Every Long Night, a glass from the fountain, every Turning, a vial. I've never taken one. Not one. I'm forty-six years old and I look sixty, in a house full of people who are two hundred and look thirty." Something that wasn't quite a smile. "It's the only thing I've ever refused her."

---

"Is he well?" she said.

It came out of her suddenly, at the end of a long silence, as though she'd been holding it in her mouth the whole time and it had finally fallen out.

"Severin. Is he—" She stopped. Her hands had come unfolded in her lap and were gripping each other. "I heard about the Dial. I was there, at the back of the gallery. I saw her take him. I saw him go down." She swallowed. "How many?"

I looked at her. At his eyes in her worn face.

"Eight," I said. "Now."

"Eight." She said it as if she didn't understand the word. "Eight *years*?"

"He had nine on Tithe Day. He spent five in the ballroom on the Long Night and I gave him three. And she took three on the Dial. And he spent one last week saving a shed full of laundresses from Durrow's men, and I gave him five." I heard my voice go strange. "Eight. And two months."

Evadne Thane looked at me across the candle with her son's eyes, and they filled.

"He was born with seventy-three," she said.

"I know."

"I held him. The morning he was born. I held his arm up to the window and counted them. Seventy-three. The midwife said it was the longest she'd ever seen on a Thane baby. She said he'd see the next century." Her voice cracked down the middle. "Eight."

"He'll have more," I said. "I'll make sure."

She looked at me for a long time. At my face, and then down at my wrist, at the threads, at my count glowing faintly under the sleeve of the grey wrapper. I saw her understand. I saw her put it together, the way he'd put it together on the Hours Bridge in the fog, the way his grandmother had put it together on the Dial.

"*You* gave him five," she said slowly. "Last week. That was you. The tick, in the night. The glass." She stared at me. "You moved the clock. For my son."

"Yes."

Evadne Thane put her face in her thin hands and didn't make a sound.

---

When she lifted her head, her face was wet, and something in it had changed. I don't know what. Something that had been folded up small for fourteen years was being unfolded, carefully, one crease at a time.

"Aldric showed him the passages," she said.

I went still.

"When he was six. Before the gallery. Before any of it." She wiped her face with the back of her hand like a child. "There's a way out of every room in the west wing. Aldric said. The first Thanes built them, when the house was new, when the Houses still murdered each other in their beds. They go between the walls. Down to the cellars, and out, under the gardens, to the old tunnels." She shook her head. "I never learned them. I'm afraid of the dark. But Aldric took the boy through every one, when she was in the Gilt on business, and made him promise never to tell her." She looked at me. "*Every Thane needs a way out of his own house, Sev.* That's what Aldric used to say to him. I heard him say it."

*Sev.*

Not just Liesl, then. His father too. Before the long gallery.

"How do you find them?" I said. My voice was very quiet. "The ways in?"

"By the draft," said Evadne Thane. "Aldric said you could always find them by the draft. Cold air, coming out of a wall where there's no door."

I thought about three nights of standing in dark corridors with my hand flat on the panelling, and a thin cold stream of air on my ankles from nowhere.

---

I walked back to the Violet Rooms slowly, with Corran the guard a polite distance behind me.

I didn't count anything. I didn't need to. I walked through the pools of blue light and the dark between them, past the portraits, past the place in the long gallery where the light in the afternoon was quite something, and I thought about a boy of six following his father between the walls of this black house in the dark with a candle. *Every Thane needs a way out of his own house, Sev.* And a boy of seven in the long gallery, made to watch. And a woman in a music room playing the first three notes of a song she couldn't remember the rest of, for fourteen years, so that nobody would see her.

I'd thought he'd been alone in this house. All of it. The whole time.

He hadn't been. He'd only thought he was.

Corran opened the door of the Violet Rooms for me and bowed and said, "Good night, miss," and shut it behind me.

The fire had burned down to embers. The room was dark, except for the faint red glow from the grate and the gold of my own count under my sleeve. I stood just inside the door for a moment, letting my eyes adjust. The enormous bed. The violet silk on the walls. The tall curtained windows. The painted ceiling.

And a draft.

On my ankles. Cold. Thin. Coming from the wall to the left of the fireplace, from the dark violet silk between the mantel and the corner, where there wasn't a door.

I'd felt it every night for three weeks. I'd thought it was the chimney.

As I stood there with my heart going, very quietly, with a sound no louder than a breath, the wall moved.

A seam opened in the silk. A straight dark line, floor to ceiling, where no seam had been. It widened. A panel of the wall, a tall narrow section of violet silk and the dark wood behind it, swung slowly inward into blackness.

And somebody stepped out of the dark between the walls into the dim red light of the room.

Tall. In a plain dark coat. With a candle stub held up in one hand and the other hand held out in front of him, palm up, the way you'd hold out your hand for a coin.

I didn't need the light to know who it was. I'd known for three months. I'd have known at the bottom of the sea.

The silver thread at my wrist was on fire.
