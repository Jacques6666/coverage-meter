# Chapter Four

## One Word

::: {.pov}
Severin
:::

The steam-house was cold at night.

That was the first thing that surprised him. By day it was an oven, a red roaring cloud; by night, with the furnaces banked and the vats gone still and the women gone home to their courts and their children, it was a long dark brick shed that dripped. Water ran down the walls and pooled on the floor. The great iron mangles stood in a row like sleeping animals. And at the far end, by the last vat, under a single lamp hung from a rafter, Lotte Varga sat on an upturned basket with her big raw hands on her knees, waiting for him.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm early."

"You're late by my clock." She didn't take out the watch. She didn't need to; he'd heard it as soon as he came in, the tiny dry tick from her apron pocket, the smallest voice in the room. "Sit. Not there. On the floor. You don't get a basket till you've earned one."

He sat on the wet brick.

There was a man beside her, on another basket. A big man, a dock porter by the shoulders, maybe forty, with a short Wick count and a face like a fist that had given up. His left sleeve was rolled to the elbow, and around his wrist, among a dozen pale scars of old threads, there was a new one. Black. Fibrous. A broker's twist under the skin like a grain of rice.

"This is Abel," said Lotte. "Abel owes Pruett on the Row nine years he never meant to sell, on account of a fever and a wife and a broker who reads his contracts faster than Abel does. Pruett's been drawing them out of him a month at a time down that thread for a year now. Abel's got three left." She looked at Severin. "Tonight you're going to cut it."

"I don't know how."

"No. That's why it's tonight and not next week." She took Abel's wrist, gently, and turned it to the lamp. "Give me your hand."

He gave it to her. She put his fingertips on the black thread where it ran under Abel's skin. It was warm. And alive. He could feel it the way he'd learned to feel his own thread to Tamsin, a line with a little give in it, a rope with something on the other end.

"Two hearts," said Lotte. "Abel's, here. And somewhere up the Row, asleep in his bed over his shop, Pruett's. Feel them both."

He felt for them. Abel's was easy, slow and heavy and resigned, right under his fingers. The other took longer. Fainter. Further. A quick, shallow, fussy beat, a long way off.

"Got it," he said.

"Now listen to them together. They're not in time. Two hearts never are. Abel's beats. Then Pruett's. Then Abel's again. Every thread there ever was pulls on the beat. Every time one of the hearts beats, the thread goes tight. Between the beats—" She held up one finger. "There's a moment. Neither heart pulling. Nothing moving down the line at all. The thread just *lies there*, slack, like a rope on a deck when the wind drops."

"The slack."

"The slack. Cut it there, and it parts like wet paper, and both ends go on breathing, and nobody's any the wiser till they look at their arm. Cut it on the beat," said Lotte Varga, "and the thread snaps back like a whip, and it takes the weaker heart with it."

Abel didn't look at either of them. He was looking at the dark end of the shed.

"Black thread's easy," said Lotte. "Black thread's got a slack the length of a breath. You could drive a cart through it. A Ledger thread's got a slack the length of a blink. Less. That's why everyone who tries to cut one kills somebody." She let go of his hand. "So you'll practice on black. Every night. A hundred threads. Two hundred. Till you can feel the slack coming before it comes, the way you feel the step at the bottom of a stair in the dark."

She took a knife from her apron. Small, plain, the kind you'd cut thread with at a mangle. She held it out to him, handle first.

"And it costs," she said. "A cut costs. Not much, on black. A few minutes of yours, to make the blade go through time as well as cord. On a Ledger thread, more." She looked at his arm. "You can't afford many mistakes, Thane."

"I know."

"Then don't make any."

---

He made eleven.

Not on Abel. On a length of black thread Lotte had strung between two nails on the side of the vat, with a heartbeat in it she made herself by laying her own palm on one nail and Abel's on the other. Eleven times he felt for the slack and cut, and eleven times he cut on the beat, and the thread snapped back with a sound like a whip and stung both their hands, and Lotte said nothing at all, only restrung it.

On the twelfth, he felt it.

He couldn't have said how. It was like the moment in the Lantern Theatre, a whole winter ago, when Tamsin had sat beside him with her eyes closed and suddenly said *it didn't come from me*. A thing you can't see, and then can. Two hearts, out of time, Lotte's slow and hard, Abel's slow and heavy, and between them, for the length of a breath, a stillness. Not silence. A held breath. A pause in the middle of a step.

He cut.

The thread parted. Like wet paper. No whip. No sting. The two ends just fell away from each other and hung down from their nails.

Lotte looked at them for a long time.

"Again," she said.

---

Near the second bell, she cut Abel's thread herself, to show him. She did it so fast he almost missed it: a hand on the porter's wrist, a pause, the little knife flicking once, and then the black thread lying in two pieces on the wet brick and Abel staring at his own arm as if he'd never seen it before.

"Three years," said Abel slowly. His voice was rusty, as though he didn't use it much. "Three. And they're *mine*."

"They're yours," said Lotte. "Go home to your wife."

He went. He stopped at the door of the shed and turned around, and looked at Lotte, and then at Severin sitting on the wet floor, and didn't say anything, and went out into the fog.

Lotte sat back down on her basket. She took the watch out of her apron pocket and looked at it, and put it to her ear, and put it back.

"My price," she said.

He'd been waiting for it. All night. He'd been waiting for it since the steam-house, since she'd stood over him in the red steam and said *you'll hear about my girl, every single day*. He'd braced himself for it like a blow. He didn't know what she'd choose. The worst thing, he supposed. The last thing. What it had been like, the day the Thane steward came down the back stair to tell her.

"She came home after her first week at the big house," said Lotte Varga, "on her half-day. Twelve years old. Sat at my table and ate everything in the house and talked the whole time. About the kitchens, and the cook, and the other scullery girls, and the dog that slept by the ovens." She was looking at the dark end of the shed, where Abel had gone. "And a boy."

He went very still on the wet floor.

"There's a boy up there," said Lotte, in a voice that wasn't hers, a lighter voice, quick and scornful and twelve years old. "*There's a boy up there, Mam, the Thane boy, and he's got a face like a wet heron and he sulks. He sulks all the time. He sits on the back stairs with a broken clock and sulks at it.*" She stopped. Her own voice came back, flat. "She said she was going to teach you cards. To cheer you up. She said nobody'd ever taught you anything useful in your life."

He couldn't breathe.

"That's tonight's," said Lotte. "You'll get another tomorrow." She stood up. "Go home, Thane."

---

He didn't go home.

He sat on the wet floor of the steam-house after she'd gone, with the lamp burning down and the water dripping off the rafters and the length of black thread hanging in two pieces from its nails. He had the little knife in his hand. He didn't remember deciding to keep it.

*A face like a wet heron.* He'd told Tamsin that. At the velvet table, in the tower, the night his cuff came off. *She told me I looked like a wet heron when I was sulking.* He'd thought it was a thing Liesl said to him. He hadn't known it was a thing she said about him, at a table in the Sump, to her mother, on her half-day, with her mouth full.

He hadn't known he'd been talked about at all.

He got up, eventually. His legs were stiff with cold. He restrung the black thread between the nails, because it seemed wrong to leave it hanging, and laid his own palm on one nail and his own other palm on the other, which made no sense, one heart can't make a slack with itself, and stood there with his hands spread against the cold iron and his eyes closed, feeling for it anyway.

And the thread on his wrist moved.

---

It wasn't a beat. It wasn't the pull of something going out of her, or the slow seep of it coming back, the things he'd learned to feel every morning at the eighth bell. It was something he'd never felt before in his life. A warmth, going *into* him. Not years. Less than a minute. A breath. A breath of time, coming down the silver thread from the far side of the city, through the fog, across the river, and arriving in his wrist.

And inside it, wrapped up in it like a stone in a handkerchief, there was a word.

*Severin.*

He heard it. Not in his ears. In his blood. In the place in his chest where her heartbeat had lived all winter, under his own.

Her voice. Her *voice*. Thin and strange and very far away, as if it had come up out of a well. But hers. The flat Wick vowels. The way she said his name, as if it were a word she'd had to learn and was still slightly suspicious of.

He staggered.

He actually staggered, a step backward, away from the nails, and his heel caught the lip of the vat and he went over it, backward, into four feet of cold grey water that had been boiling linen twelve hours ago.

---

"What," said Lotte Varga, "in the *name* of every saint in the Sump."

She'd come back. He never found out why; for her shawl, she said later. She hauled him up out of the vat by the collar with one raw arm as if he were a sheet on a pole, and dropped him streaming on the wet floor, and stood over him with her fists on her hips.

"Her," he said. He was shaking. He was soaked to the skin and shaking, and it wasn't the cold. "In my wrist. Her voice. She said my name."

Lotte stared at him.

"Her *voice*."

"Down the thread. She—it came down the thread. Like time does. Like a breath of it, with a word inside." He held up his wrist, as if she'd be able to see something there. "She said my *name*."

Lotte Varga stood over him for a long moment. Then she sat down, slowly, on her basket.

"Well-speech," she said.

He looked up.

"My grandmother used to tell it," said Lotte. "Over the vats. A laundress's story, like the one about the clock. That the Marrow women could talk down a thread. Not out loud. Inside. A breath of time with a word in it." She shook her head slowly. "I thought it was a story. Like the wells were a story." She looked at him, dripping on her floor. "I suppose I'll have to stop thinking that about a great many things."

"Can I answer?"

"Can you—" She stared at him. "Thane. It's *well*-speech. Your girl's mother's a well, and your girl's got her mother's thread on her, and the time comes back to her as fast as she spends it. You're not a well. You're a dying boy with four years on his arm." She leaned forward. "My grandmother said the one at the other end could answer, if a well opened the line first. And she said it cost them. Every word. Going back uphill, against the grain."

"How much?"

"An hour," said Lotte Varga. "A word."

He sat on the wet brick in his soaked coat with the water running off his hair into his eyes, and looked at his wrist.

An hour a word. He had four years and two months. Thirty-six thousand hours, give or take. He'd done the sum before she finished the sentence. Thirty-six thousand words. A long letter. A short book. A lifetime of saying nothing, the way he'd always said nothing, and then all of it at once.

He reached down into the silver thread, the way he'd felt her do, and pushed one word into it.

*Here.*

He felt it go. Not a breath. An hour. He felt it leave him, the long lonely drop under the breastbone, an hour of his life going down the thread across the dark city wrapped around one word, uphill, against the grain. And he didn't care. He'd have paid a year.

A pause. Long. Long enough that he thought it hadn't reached her, that he'd done it wrong, that he'd thrown an hour into the dark for nothing.

Then, warm, down the thread:

*Alive?*

His throat closed.

*Yes,* he sent back.

Another hour. He felt it go.

---

"Two hours," said Lotte, watching his face. "In a minute. You'll be dead by Midsummer at that rate."

"Worth it."

She looked at him for a long moment, sitting soaked on her floor with his hand clamped over his wrist and his eyes shut.

"Go home, Thane," she said again, more quietly. "Before you drown in my vat and I have to explain it to the Keeper."

---

He walked back up through the courts of the Sump in the fog with his coat dripping and his teeth chattering, and he couldn't stop smiling. He didn't know he was doing it until a woman coming the other way with a basket of fish looked at his face and crossed to the other side of the alley.

Kit was awake when he came down the cellar ladder at Gannet's. Kit was always awake. He was sitting on the bottom rung in his too-big coat with the tin watch in his hand, winding it, not too tight, the way he did every morning before it was light.

"You're wet," said Kit.

"I fell in a vat."

Kit considered this. "On purpose?"

"No."

"Oh." He went back to winding. Then he stopped, and looked up, and his eyes went very big in the light from the lamp at the top of the ladder. "Something happened."

Severin sat down on the cold floor at the bottom of the ladder, in his wet coat, beside the boy.

"She spoke to me," he said.

Kit stared at him.

"Tam?"

"Down the thread. In here." He held up his wrist. "I don't know how. Her mother must have taught her. She spoke to me."

"What did she *say*?"

"My name."

Kit waited. When nothing else came, his face fell, just slightly.

"That's all?"

Severin looked at the tin watch in the boy's hand, ticking. At the small thin wrist above it, with thirty-one gold lines on it, and the binder's scar where the Grace had gone in.

"That's everything," he said.

Kit thought about that for a long time, with the watch ticking between them. Then he held it out. Not to give it to him. Just to show him.

"Will you tell her something? From me?"

"It costs," said Severin. "Every word. An hour."

"How many hours have you got?"

"Thirty-six thousand. About."

Kit did the sum. Severin watched him do it, his lips moving, his eyes going far away the way Tamsin's did when she was counting. Then he looked back up, and his face was very serious.

"Then you can afford four," he said. "Tell her I'm winding the watch."

Severin closed his eyes, and reached down into the silver thread, and paid four hours without a second thought.

*Kit winding the watch.*

The answer came back almost at once, warm, a breath of time with a word inside, and he didn't need to tell the boy what it was, because Kit was watching his face and saw it.

---

Hollis Penhallow came down the cellar ladder at the fifth bell, in plum velvet, out of breath, with coal dust on one cheek and an expression of deep personal suffering.

"I came on a coal barge," he said, before anyone could ask. "From the Gilt landing. Under a tarpaulin. With *coal*." He looked down at his coat. "This was my grandfather's. He was buried in its twin."

"Penhallow," said Severin.

"Yes, yes. I'm here. I bring news." Hollis took a breath, and the joke went out of his face, and what was underneath it was very pale. "Grandmother Thane's holding a salon. At the house. Tomorrow evening. For the Inner Ledger, the oldest families, the ones she sells to. Mother got an invitation and fainted. Actually fainted, in the hall. We've never been asked before; we've never been *old* enough." He swallowed. "I'm going in her place. I told her I'd represent the family and she was so pleased she fainted again."

"And?" said Severin.

Hollis looked at him. Then at Kit, sitting on the bottom rung of the ladder with the tin watch. Then back.

"And the invitation says there'll be *a demonstration*," said Hollis quietly. "She's showing her, Thane. She's going to sit Tam up in front of the oldest people in the Gilt and *show* her."
