# Chapter Eleven

## Chocolate at Midnight

::: {.pov}
Tamsin
:::

"Lamp oil," I said.

My voice came out thick and slow, a voice dragged up out of sleep. I didn't have to pretend much. My heart was going so hard I could feel it in my teeth.

"Nessa filled the lamps this afternoon. From a new jar. It smells like something off a cart." I pushed my hair out of my face with one hand and kept the blanket up to my chin with the other. "I told her it would give me a headache."

Lady Aurelia stood in the doorway with her candle and her little silver tray and looked at me. Then, for a long moment, at the room. The dead fire. The tall curtained windows. The violet silk on the walls, dark in the candlelight. The wall to the left of the fireplace, where a black line ran from floor to ceiling in the silk, a finger's width wide.

I didn't look at it. I looked at her.

"Nessa," she said, at last, with a small sigh, as though the name explained a great deal about the world. "I'll speak to the housekeeper."

She came in and shut the door behind her with her heel.

---

She sat on the edge of the bed.

She'd never done that before, not even on the morning after the clock moved, when she'd come in barefoot in the same grey wrapper with no violets. She set the tray on the coverlet between us and handed me a cup. It was hot and thick and dark, and it smelled of cinnamon, and I took it because I didn't know how not to.

"Drink it, dear. It's good. My cook makes it with chilies from the south; you'll feel it in your toes."

I drank it. She was right. I felt it in my toes.

On the floor beside the bed, half under it, lay the plain grey wrapper Nessa had found me, where it had fallen hours ago and nobody had picked it up. I saw Lady Aurelia's eyes go to it. She bent, quite casually, and picked it up, and shook it out, and laid it across the foot of the bed.

"You'll catch cold," she said.

I said nothing. I drank my chocolate. Ten steps away, behind a finger's width of dark in the violet silk, I could feel him. Through the silver thread, so close it hurt. His heart going like mine. Holding his breath.

Lady Aurelia wrapped both small hands around her own cup and didn't drink from it. She sat there on the edge of my bed in her grey silk with her plait over her shoulder, looking at the dark window, and for a long time she didn't say anything at all.

"I can't sleep," she said finally. "Since the clock moved. Did you know that?"

"No, my lady."

"Not a wink. Not properly. I lie down and close my eyes and I hear it. *Tick*." She said it softly, the way you'd say the name of something you were afraid would hear you. "It's absurd. Two hundred and forty-seven years old, and I'm lying awake like a child who's been told about the bogeyman."

She turned her cup slowly in her hands.

"Do you know what it's like," said the Keeper of the Ledger, "to be afraid of a clock?"

---

I didn't answer. I don't think she wanted me to. She was looking at the window, at the dark curtain, and beyond it, I knew, across the river, at a pale face hanging over an island with its single hand pointing at two minutes past eleven.

"I was born in the Year 53," she said. "After. My mother was Keeper. She'd been there, you know, on the bridge, the night they did it. She was sixty-one; she told me once she could still hear the water." A small smile. "She took me down to see the well when I was five. Down the stair, my stair, it was hers then. Through the silver door. I remember holding her hand." She looked down at the chocolate in her cup. "And there was a woman under the pendulum. Made of light. Kneeling, with her arms held out, all silver threads, and so bright I couldn't look straight at her. The most beautiful thing I had ever seen."

I held my cup very tightly.

"I asked my mother if she was an angel," said Lady Aurelia. "And my mother laughed and said, *No, darling. She's ours.*"

The candle on the tray guttered.

"I learned the Renewal at my mother's side," she said. "Every Turning. I'd stand beside her in the chamber and watch her open her palm with the Keeper's knife and lay her hand over the well's heart and tie the knot. Year after year. And when she died, I tied it. And I have tied it every Turning of the Year since." She closed her eyes. "A hundred and forty times. My blood on the cord and my hand over the heart of whichever poor creature was under the clock. I've never missed a year. Not once. Not when I was ill. Not when my children died. Not the year Rufus shot himself on the terrace; I tied the knot that morning and buried him that afternoon." She opened her eyes. "Because I know what happens if it isn't tied."

"What happens?" I said.

She turned her head and looked at me.

"Do you know," she said softly, "what happens at midnight, dear? On that clock?"

I shook my head. I'd heard Bry say *Lotte told him a story, a laundress's story, about the clock being made to give*. I'd heard the madman on the Row shouting about it, through Bry, the morning after the second tick. *Every hour goes home*. I didn't know what it meant. Not really.

"Everyone goes home," said Lady Aurelia Thane.

She said it the way you'd say a prayer you no longer believed in, and were afraid of anyway.

"Every hour that was ever bought," she said, "goes back to the one who sold it. Every hour that was taken goes back to the one it was taken from. Everything settles. Everything is paid. The way it was before the Founders, when the clock went round every night, and at midnight every account in Lanthorn came out even." She looked down at her own arm, at the blaze of gold under the grey silk of her sleeve, from her wrist to her shoulder and over it. "Do you know where my hours came from, dear? Two hundred and forty-seven years. I was born with sixty."

I didn't say anything.

"Sixty," said the Keeper. "A good count. A Gilt count. I spent it by the time I was forty." She smiled, without any dimples at all. "Everything since then has been somebody else's. Bought. Taken. Drawn. From the Row, from the Tithe Hall, from the fountain, from the well." She turned the cup in her hands. "If that clock strikes twelve, every one of those hours goes home. In a breath. And I go with them." A pause. "*Everyone goes home.* It used to be a lullaby, did you know that? Before the Drowning. The Wick sang it to their children. *Everyone goes home at midnight.*"

I thought of a song with no words, rising and falling like water. A laundress's song. A mother's song, over a cot.

"And it's moved," said Lady Aurelia quietly. "Twice. For the first time in three hundred years. Because of you."

She looked at me for a long time, in the candlelight, with her chocolate going cold.

---

"So I've come to make you an offer," she said.

She set the cup down on the tray. She folded her small hands in her lap. And she was the Keeper again, all at once, the dimples back, the warmth back, the voice like honey.

"At the Turning, I'll take you down to your mother. You know that. I told the Inner Ledger; I'll tie the Stay with you." She tilted her head. "There are two ways I can do it, dear. I can bind you. Under the clock, beside her, in silver, the way she's bound. And you'd stay there, the two of you, as long as you lasted, and the Stay would be stronger than it's ever been, and the clock would never move again." She said it quite gently. "Or."

I waited. I could feel him behind the wall. I could feel him not breathing.

"Or you could come down the stair beside me," said Lady Aurelia. "Of your own will. And kneel by your mother for one night, and give your blood and your heart to the knot, willing. That's all the Stay needs, you see. A well's blood, given, not taken. The Founders' Charter is very particular about that. And then, in the morning, you'd stand up, and walk back up the stair. With me." She smiled. "As my heir."

The room was very quiet.

"Keeper," she said. "After me. When I'm done. Which will be a long time from now, dear, but not forever; nothing's forever, not even me. You'd sit at the top of the Ledger. Every year in Lanthorn would pass through your hands. You'd never get old. You'd never die." She leaned forward. "And your brother. Christopher. Thirty-one years is nothing. You could give him a hundred. Two hundred. You could watch him grow up and grow old and keep him from growing any older, for as long as you liked."

I didn't move. I couldn't.

"And the boy," said Lady Aurelia.

My heart stopped.

"My great-great-great-grandson. My foolish, soft, beautiful boy, with his eight years and his clocks." She looked at me with something in her face that, on anybody else, I'd have called kindness. "I know, dear. I'm not blind. I watched him hold you on the Dial with his arm stretched out like a bar across a door. I watched you pour three years into him in my ballroom." She shook her head fondly. "I'd let him live. As a favor to you. You could keep him. Give him all the years you liked. Like a pet."

Behind the wall, I felt his heart go like a fist hitting a door.

"And my mother?" I said.

Lady Aurelia's smile didn't change at all.

"The well must stay, dear," she said. "Someone has to hold the clock."

---

I want to tell you I didn't think about it.

I did.

I sat in the enormous bed in the Violet Rooms with the cold chocolate in my hands and the Keeper of the Ledger smiling at me, and for the length of three heartbeats I thought about it. Kit at forty. Kit at sixty, at a hundred, with his maps, with his lungs that filled every winter and never killed him because I wouldn't let them. Severin with seventy years on his arm again, seventy-three, the count he'd been born with, all that grey filling back in with gold, line by line, because I'd put it there. A velvet table and a room full of clocks and a cup of tea at my elbow, forever. Never Tallow Lane again. Never the Row. Never selling a single year to Pruett and throwing up in the yard.

And a woman under a pendulum in a round black room, with her arms held out in silver. Kneeling. For another hundred years. Two hundred. Until she wore out, and they went and found another one.

I thought about it for three heartbeats. I'll always know that. That it was three and not none.

Then I put the cup down on the tray.

"I'll think about it," I said.

She looked at me for a long moment. And smiled. Both dimples.

"Of course you will, dear," said Lady Aurelia Thane. "That's all I ask."

She stood up, and picked up her tray, and her candle. At the door she stopped, and turned, and looked back at the room, at the dead fire and the violet silk on the walls and the wall to the left of the fireplace.

"This was my great-aunt Hortense's room," she said. "She died here. In that bed, as it happens." She smiled. "People say it's haunted. Do you ever hear things, dear? In the walls?"

"Only the pipes," I said.

"Mm," said the Keeper, and went out, and shut the door.

---

I didn't move until I heard her footsteps reach the end of the corridor. Then the panel by the fireplace opened, slowly, and he came out of the wall, in his shirt, with his boots and his coat in his hands, and stood in the dark in the middle of the room.

He didn't come to the bed. He stood there, and I sat there, and we looked at each other in the light of my count.

"You heard," I said.

"I heard."

"*Like a pet,*" I said, and my voice did something strange in the middle of it.

He crossed the room and sat down on the edge of the bed where she'd been sitting, and put his hand over mine on the coverlet. His hand was cold. He'd been standing in the wall in his shirt for half an hour.

"She's afraid," he said quietly. "I've never—" He stopped. "In twenty-one years, I've never once heard her say she was afraid of anything."

"Was any of it true?"

He was quiet for a long time.

"She's lying about one of them," he said at last. "The two ways. Bound, or kneel for one night and walk back up the stair as her heir." He looked down at our hands. "I don't know which one's the lie. Maybe the first. Maybe she'd let you go. Maybe she really does want an heir who can't die, with a well inside her." He looked up. "Or maybe she'd let you kneel, willing, and give your blood to the knot, and then she'd simply never untie you. And you'd have walked down the stair yourself."

"Either way," I said, "my mother stays."

"Either way, your mother stays."

I turned my hand over under his and held on.

"Three heartbeats," I said. "I thought about it for three heartbeats. Kit. You. All of it." I swallowed. "I want you to know that. That it wasn't none."

He looked at me for a long moment in the gold light. Then he lifted my hand and kissed the inside of my wrist, over the threads, over all of them, his and Bry's and my mother's and hers.

"I'd have thought about it for four," he said.

---

He went before dawn. Back through the panel, into the dark between the walls, with Kit's map in his pocket and a candle stub I'd found him from the drawer by the bed.

"The day after tomorrow," he said, in the gap. "Council day. The Charter."

"Be careful."

"I'm always careful."

"You spent a year on *laundresses*."

The corner of his mouth moved in the dark. "I'm careful with everything else." He put his hand flat on the edge of the panel. "I'll come back. Through here. After."

"You'll *tell* me. Down the thread. The second it's done."

"An hour a word."

"I don't care. You've got a year more than you had when you came in. I counted." I held his eyes. "Spend some."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he leaned through the gap and kissed me, once, hard, and stepped back into the dark, and pulled the panel closed. This time it shut all the way, with a small click, and there was nothing on the wall to the left of the fireplace but violet silk.

And a draft.

---

I woke to hammering.

It was late. Gone the eighth bell. Nobody had woken me for breakfast. I sat up in the enormous bed, confused, with the morning light coming grey through the curtains, and listened.

Hammering. Close. In the corridor. And further off, the long gallery maybe, the music room, other hammers. And voices, men's voices, calling to each other, and a sound I knew from the Wick, from every building site on the Row: the scrape of a trowel on brick.

Nessa came in. Her face was white.

"Masons, miss," she whispered. "Since the seventh bell. All through the west wing. Lady Aurelia's orders." She looked at me, and then, very carefully, not at the wall by the fireplace. "She told the housekeeper the old rooms have drafts. She said it's high time somebody saw to the walls."

Two men came in behind her without knocking, in canvas aprons, with a hod of bricks and a bucket of mortar, and bowed to me, and said, "Beg pardon, miss," and went to the wall to the left of the fireplace.

They didn't need to look for it. They knew exactly where it was. One of them took a knife and slit the violet silk from floor to ceiling, in a straight line, where the seam had been. Behind it was the panel, dark wood, old, with a brass latch on the other side that I'd never seen.

I sat in the bed and watched them brick it up.

It took them all morning. Course after course, red brick and grey mortar, scrape and tap, while I sat there and didn't move and Nessa stood by the door with her hands knotted together. When they'd finished they pasted the violet silk back over it, neatly, so you'd never have known. They bowed again, and took their hod and their bucket, and went next door to do the same to someone else's walls.

I got out of bed and went and stood in front of the place where the panel had been, and put my hand flat on the silk.

By noon, the draft was gone.
