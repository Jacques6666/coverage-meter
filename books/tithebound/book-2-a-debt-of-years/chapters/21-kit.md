# Chapter Twenty-One

## Kit

::: {.pov}
Tamsin
:::

*Kit.*

That was all he sent. One word, at the third bell, down the silver thread, an hour of his life. And then nothing.

I didn't need anything else. I'd felt the fire. I'd been asleep in the enormous bed with my hand over my wrist, and I'd come up out of it with the silver thread burning and his heart going like a fist on a door, and under it, faint, down the black thread, Bry's, pounding. I'd felt him pull. A working, cast blind, a lot of time going out of him at once. I'd felt it go into something and stop, and his heart go on hammering, and then a long, terrible nothing.

And then that one word.

I sat up in the dark. I sent down both threads at once, the silver and the black: *What? What happened? Where is he?*

Bry answered. She always answered. A rush of words in the dark, at an hour a word, all of them broken.

*Durrow. Fire. Gannet's is gone. He went back for his maps, Tam. He went back. Durrow took him out through the front. Thane tried. He stilled the smoke. It was the smoke. He couldn't see.* A pause. *We dragged him out. He was fighting us. He was screaming for him.*

I didn't sleep again. I sat in the window seat with the curtain pulled back and watched the clock across the river, three minutes past eleven, until the sky behind it went grey, and I didn't cry. I couldn't. There wasn't room. Everything in me was taken up with knowing where Durrow would bring him.

---

"I have a surprise for you, dear," said Lady Aurelia at breakfast.

She was dressed already, in black lace, with her silver box at her elbow and her hair up, and she looked rested, which she never did anymore. She looked like a woman who'd slept well for the first time in a month.

She lifted one small hand.

The door at the end of the Glass Room opened, and Durrow came in.

He had Kit by the arm. Not dragging him. Just holding him, the way you'd hold a dog on a lead, his big red hand closed round Kit's thin arm above the elbow, and Kit walking beside him in his too-big coat with the sleeves rolled up four times, soot all over his face and hands, his hair singed at one side, his eyes enormous.

He was coughing. That deep wet terrible cough I'd heard every winter of his life, the one that meant his lungs were filling, the one I'd sold three years in Saltmarket to drain. He was walking with his shoulders hunched and his mouth open, taking those quick little sips of air he took when it was bad.

He saw me.

He stopped dead in the middle of the Glass Room, between the orange trees and the violets, and Durrow's hand jerked on his arm, and he didn't notice.

"*Tam*—"

"Let him go, Sergeant," said Lady Aurelia, buttering toast.

Durrow let him go.

He crossed the Glass Room so fast I didn't see him move, and hit me in the stomach with his whole body the way he had on Tallow Lane the night I came home with the laundry, and I went over backward in my chair and we both went down on the tiles in a heap, and I held him. I held him so hard he squeaked. He smelled of smoke and soot and Gannet's back room and under it, faintly, of the soap Mrs. Achterberg made, and he was all ribs and elbows and he was *alive*, and he was coughing into my neck, and I held on.

"You're *here*," he kept saying, into my shoulder, between coughs. "You're here. You're here."

"I'm here."

"They burned it. Gannet's. Everything. My maps. Tam, my *maps*—"

"I know. I know. Shh. Breathe. Breathe slow. Like I taught you."

He tried. I felt him try. Slow, in, out, the way we used to do it on the bad nights, sitting up in the cot together with the blanket round both of us, counting. It didn't work very well. His chest was rattling against mine like a cart on cobbles.

Above us, at the table, Lady Aurelia took a bite of toast.

"He has a cough," she observed. "My physic will see to it. I can't have him dying on me before the Turning, dear, can I? That would be a waste of a perfectly good lesson."

---

She let us stay on the floor.

She ate her breakfast and drank her tea and looked out of the glass at the clock, and Durrow stood by the door with his thumbs in his belt, and I sat on the tiles of the Glass Room with my back against the leg of the table and Kit in my lap, both arms round him, rocking him a little, the way I hadn't done since he was six and said he was too old for it. He didn't say he was too old for it now. He curled into me with his face in my neck and coughed and breathed and held on to the front of my wrapper with both sooty fists.

At the eighth bell, she held out her hand.

Not across the table. Down. To me, on the floor.

I gave her my wrist. Over Kit's shoulder. She drank. He felt it; I felt him feel it, his whole body going stiff in my arms as my count went out under her fingers, a whole year, brown and grey and gone. He lifted his head off my neck and stared at my arm.

Then he watched it come back.

Slowly. Up from below. The top line warming, brightening, lit. Seventy-one years and four months.

He'd never seen it. I realized that, sitting there. In ten years, I'd never once let him see it happen. I'd sold fifteen years in Saltmarket and come home with my sleeve pulled down and told him I'd had a good day at Gannet's.

He looked at my arm. Then at my face. His eyes were very big.

"Tam," he whispered.

"Later," I said. "I'll tell you everything. Later."

Lady Aurelia let go of my wrist and patted it twice.

"Now," she said. "Christopher."

Kit went rigid in my lap.

She'd turned in her chair to look down at him. Not unkindly. With interest, the way she'd looked at Pell when he started to sing. She took a violet from her silver box and held it out to him between two fingers.

"Would you like one, dear? They're from the south. They taste of summer."

Kit looked at the violet. Then at her face. Then he turned his head and put his face back in my neck.

Lady Aurelia laughed, delighted, and ate it herself.

"He's like you," she said to me. "Isn't he? Stubborn. Your mother was stubborn too, the first year. She wouldn't eat. I had to have her fed with a spoon." She brushed sugar off her lip. "Thirty-one years, Christopher's got. Did you know that, dear? I'm sure you did. Thirty of them Grace. Given by my own House, out of my own reserves, on Tithe Day, by my own foolish heir." She tilted her head. "That's a great deal of my time to have walking around Lanthorn in a little boy."

I didn't say anything. I held Kit.

"So here is how the Turning will go," said Lady Aurelia Thane.

She said it pleasantly. The way she'd told the Inner Ledger, in the Orrery Room, under the painted clock. The way she'd told me, over chocolate, the two ways it could be done.

"The day after tomorrow, at dawn, you'll come down my stair beside me. In white. And you'll kneel by your mother under the clock, and hold out your arms, and let my binders put you in silver. And when I open my palm and lay it on her heart, you'll give your blood to the knot." She smiled. "Willing. With your heart in it. The way the Charter says. The way I told you."

Kit's fists tightened in my wrapper.

"Or," said Lady Aurelia, "I'll drink your brother's Grace in front of you."

The Glass Room was very quiet. The orange trees. The violets. The clock in the window.

"All thirty years," she said. "The way I drank the baker. Right there on the floor of the chamber, in front of your mother, with you in silver beside her watching. It won't take me a minute." She looked at Kit. "And then he'll have one year left, and a cough. And I'll let him go home to Tallow Lane with it." She tilted her head. "I'm not a monster."

I sat on the floor with my brother in my arms and looked up at her.

"You'll have tonight," said Lady Aurelia. "Together. In the Violet Rooms. You can talk. You can hold him. You can tell him whatever you like." She smiled with both dimples. "And you can think. That's all I ask, dear. Think about it."

---

Her physic came to the Violet Rooms at noon, an old man with cold hands and a leather bag, and listened to Kit's chest with a brass trumpet, and gave him a draught that smelled of tar and honey, and he slept.

He slept all afternoon in the enormous bed with the violet silk on the walls and the violets painted on the ceiling, curled on his side under the coverlet the way he slept on Tallow Lane, with his knees up and his fists by his face. I sat beside him and watched him breathe. In. Out. Less rattle now. The draught was good. Of course it was. She wanted him alive.

I sent down the silver thread. Free, every word.

*He's here. In my bed. Asleep. Her physic's seen to his chest; he's breathing better. She says if I'm not willing at the Turning she'll drink his Grace in front of me.*

A long silence. I felt Severin's heart go still, and then very hard and slow, the way it went when he was holding a door shut on something.

Then three words. Three hours.

*Watch. Safe. Wound.*

I put my hand over my mouth.

---

Kit woke at dusk.

He woke all at once, the way he did, sitting up with his eyes wide and his hair sticking up on the singed side, not knowing where he was. Then he saw me, and the violet silk, and the enormous bed, and his face crumpled, and then uncrumpled, very carefully, the way you'd fold a map.

"It wasn't a dream," he said.

"No."

He looked around the room. At the tall curtained windows, the fire, the painted ceiling. The bricked-up place on the wall to the left of the fireplace, where the violet silk had been pasted back over new mortar. He didn't know what it was. He looked at it anyway, the way he looked at everything, as though it were a map he'd need later.

"My watch," he said. "I dropped it. In the fire. When the big man picked me up."

"He found it."

Kit looked at me.

"Severin," I said. "He went back in at dawn. When the fire was out. It was in the ash where the table was." I held up my wrist. "He told me. Just now. *Watch. Safe. Wound.*"

Kit stared at my wrist for a long moment. At the silver thread.

Then he put his face in his hands and cried. Not the way he'd cried on the tiles of the Glass Room with his face in my neck. The way he cried on Tallow Lane when he thought I was asleep. Without any noise at all.

I held him until he stopped.

---

Later, when it was dark and the fire was low and we were lying side by side in the enormous bed with the blanket pulled up to our chins, he said, very quietly, to the painted ceiling:

"Is it true?"

"Is what true?"

"What Thane said." His voice was very small. "At Gannet's. Before. When we were drawing the shaft. He said there was a lady under the clock. Chained. In the round room. And Tam was going to get her out." He swallowed. "He didn't say who."

I lay on my back beside him and looked at the painted violets going dark on the ceiling.

I'd thought about how I'd tell him. Every night for a month, down the deepest thread, with her voice in my head asking about him. I'd thought about it on Tallow Lane, long before, when I was nine and sitting up all night with him squalling in my arms waiting for a door, and later, every year, every birthday, every time he asked about her and I told him one of her stories instead. I'd thought there'd be a right way. A right day. Some way to say it that wouldn't knock him flat.

There wasn't.

"It's Mama," I said.

He didn't move.

I turned my head on the pillow and looked at him. He was lying on his back with his eyes open, looking up at the ceiling, very still. In the light of my count his face was white under the soot I hadn't been able to wash off.

"She didn't go into the river," I said. "Not really. They took her. The Keeper. The night after you were born. Three weeks after. She tied something on me first, a thread, so I'd always have years. She was going to come back the next night and tie one on you. She told me. She was coming back." My voice did something strange. "They put her under the clock. She's been there ever since. Ten years. Kneeling. With her arms held out. Feeding it. Feeding the Keeper. Feeding *me*. Every year I ever sold came back from her." I swallowed. "I've seen her. I've talked to her. Every night. She asks about you first. Every single night, Kit. Before anything else. *How's Kit.*"

He didn't say anything for a long time.

Then he said, in a strange flat voice, still looking at the ceiling:

"The heart."

I didn't understand.

"Under the clock. On my map. The first one. The one I gave you." He turned his head on the pillow and looked at me, and his eyes were enormous. "I drew a heart. Under the tower. Down in the rock. Because you said Mama said. *Where the clock keeps its heart.*" His voice cracked. "I thought it was a story."

I stared at him.

"It was her," said Kit. "Wasn't it? The heart. Down in the dark. It was *her*. She told you where she was going to be." His face crumpled. "Before she even went. She *told* you. And I drew it."

I put my arms around him. I couldn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say. I held my brother in the enormous bed in the Violet Rooms in the dark, and he held me, and the map he'd drawn when he was nine, folded small and soft as cloth, was still against my ribs inside my shirt, where it had been every day since Tithe Day. *You have to give it back. After.*

After.

---

He fell asleep again near midnight, with his head on my shoulder.

Before he did, with his eyes already closing, he said one more thing. Very quietly. Not to me. To the dark.

"Then I'll see her."

I lay very still.

*At the Turning,* said Lady Aurelia's voice in my head, pleasant and warm. *You'll give your blood to the knot. Willing. Or I'll drink your brother's Grace in front of you.*

*At the Turning,* said Severin's, three hours for three words. *Watch. Safe. Wound.*

*At the Turning,* said my mother's, from under the clock, *she means to bring you down to me. And keep you.*

I looked at Kit's face on my shoulder in the gold light of my own arm. Ten years old. Thirty-one years. A cough. And a map of a heart.

I'd thought about it for three heartbeats, on the night of the chocolate. Kit at forty. Kit at a hundred. I'd thought about it for three, and I'd said no.

I'd thought about it all day today. I'd stopped counting.

If they didn't come. If something went wrong. If Lotte's hand shook or Severin's rope broke or Lisle's people moved first or Durrow's men were waiting. If I was kneeling in silver beside my mother on the black stone with the Keeper's palm open over her heart and my brother on the floor in front of me with Durrow's hand on his arm—

I knew what I'd do.

I'd give my blood. Willing. With my heart in it.

And I'd never tell him why.

"Yes," I whispered to my sleeping brother, in the dark. "You'll see her."
