# Chapter Twenty-Four

## The Keeper's Stair

::: {.pov}
Tamsin
:::

There were three carriages, all black.

The first was hers. She rode in it alone, in white, with the silver box in her lap. The second was ours: me, in the white silk nightgown with a white cloak over it, because she'd said there was no point dressing for the Renewal when I'd only be undressed again under the clock; Kit beside me in his too-big coat, with his face washed and his hair combed flat over the singed side; and across from us, filling the whole seat, Sergeant Kell Durrow, with his red face and his thumbs in his belt and a long black broker's needle in a loop at his hip.

The third carriage was Evadne.

"Family should witness," Lady Aurelia had said, on the steps of Thane House, in the grey dawn, when Evadne came out between two guards in her plain grey dress and stood blinking at the light like someone who hadn't been outside in fourteen years. "It's tradition. Every Thane goes down for the Renewal. Even the furniture."

Evadne hadn't said anything. She'd looked at me across the drive. Just once. Then she'd got into the third carriage.

---

The Gilt Road was empty. Every curtain on every window down every white terrace was drawn, and behind them, I knew, the Long-Lived were watching. Then the Wick, and the Wick was not empty. They were in the street again. Thousands of them. Standing on the cobbles and on the roofs and on the river wall, in shawls and coats and nightshirts, in the cold grey light, not shouting, not doing anything. Watching the three black carriages go by.

Watching the clock.

Three minutes past eleven.

Kit had his face pressed to the glass of the carriage window. I watched him look for Tallow Lane as we passed it, and find it, and see somebody at the corner, a big woman in a shawl with her arms folded, Mrs. Achterberg, and press his hand flat against the glass. Mrs. Achterberg saw him. Her mouth fell open. She lifted her hand.

Then we were past.

Across from us, Durrow laughed.

"Wave to the old cow," he said. "She won't be seeing you again."

Kit took his hand off the glass. He didn't look at Durrow. He looked at me, and I looked back, and under the white cloak I put my hand over my wrist and felt for the far thread.

He was there. Above. Close. Closer than he'd ever been, except in the Violet Rooms. And not in the Wick, not on the river. Up. High up. On the island. In the tower. Sitting, I thought, the way you sit when you're waiting, with your feet hanging over the edge of something.

*Crossing the bridge,* I sent.

One word came back. One hour.

*Ready.*

---

The court of the Meridian was full of the Gilt.

I'd stood in that court on Tithe Day in a line of Wick candidates with my sleeve rolled to the elbow and my arm held out, and the Long-Lived had sat in the gallery above and looked down at us like apples on a stall. Now they were down in the court itself, on the black flagstones under the clock, in their furs, in the cold, standing in a loose crowd at the foot of the tower. Twenty of them, the Inner Ledger, with their faces smooth and their eyes going to their arms. The delegates of the Seven Houses, with their stewards behind them. Binders in grey with their velvet cushions.

I knew some of them now. Corisande Marchbank in sapphires, with her cuff pushed up and her eyes on her own count, which was trembling in the grey light like a candle in a draft. Lord Alder Ostrander in a carrying chair, wrapped to the chin in furs, his ear trumpet in his lap, with Fitch standing behind the chair, white-faced. The Crane steward, heavy, red, with a black band round his arm. Lady Ismay Lisle in white fur, with her silver hair cut short as a boy's, and beside her, tall, fair, in white gloves, with a bruise along his jaw gone yellow at the edges, Corwin. And two men behind them in Lisle white who didn't look like stewards at all.

And at the edge of the crowd, in plum velvet, with a tin cuff on his arm and his mother's smelling salts in his pocket, Hollis.

He wasn't alone. Beside him stood a very tall young man in Vey green, with a lot of dark hair falling over his forehead and a book under his arm, actually a book, at a Renewal, who was looking at the clock over the court with an expression of absorbed and gentle interest, as though he might write a poem about it later. As I watched, the tall young man said something to Hollis without looking down, and Hollis went pink to the ears.

Ambrose Vey.

Hollis saw me. His face did something complicated. Then he looked away, at the tower door, quickly, the way you'd look away from a friend in a crowd so nobody would know you were friends.

At the tower door, the narrow door in the base of the Horologe that I'd walked past for a whole term and never noticed, stood Greycoats. Twelve of them, in a line, with their silver buttons and their hands behind their backs.

And at the end of the line, in a coat worn shiny at the elbows, Captain Hesketh Marr.

He didn't look at me. He looked at the Keeper, getting down from her carriage in white. But as I got down from mine behind her, with Kit's hand in mine and Durrow behind us, his washed-out eyes moved. Just once. To my face, and away.

Durrow had stopped. He was looking at the line of Greycoats at the tower door. At their faces. His red face was creasing into a frown.

"Where are my men?" he said. "Captain. Where are my men? I had twelve on this door. Picked them myself."

"Needed on the Row," said Marr, without turning his head. "Queues outside every broker's from here to the Sump. Riots in Saltmarket last night. I've moved every man I can spare." He looked at Durrow at last, mildly. "Captain's orders, Sergeant. The Keeper's stair is well guarded. You needn't fret."

Durrow's jaw worked. He looked at the Keeper. She was already at the tower door, in white, with her silver box, not listening, not looking back.

He couldn't argue. Not here. Not in front of her.

"Captain," he said, through his teeth.

"Sergeant," said Marr pleasantly, and stood aside to let us through.

---

Three hundred and four steps.

Red carpet. Silver sconces shaped like clock hands. Carved clock faces all the way down the walls, every hand on eleven.

I'd come down this stair three times now. On the Dial day, behind her, with my count bare. On the night she bound me beside my mother for a taste of the Turning. And now. With the whole Gilt behind me, coming down in a long slow line, their furs whispering on the walls, their breath loud in the narrow space. Somebody behind me was crying, softly. Corisande, I thought.

Kit held my hand. He counted under his breath. I heard him. Our mother's way, and mine. *Eighty-one. Eighty-two.*

Durrow was right behind him. I could hear the needle at his hip clicking against his belt buckle at every step.

And above. Through the thread. Close. Directly above us, now. I could feel it, the way you feel someone standing behind you on a dark street. Not in the stair. In the tower itself. In the stone. Somewhere up there, high above, at the top of the long straight dark that ran down through the middle of the Horologe like a spine.

*Going down,* I sent.

Nothing came back. He didn't need to answer.

But I felt his heart go hard and slow, the way it went when he was holding a door shut on something. And then I felt him move.

Down.

---

The passage. The warmth. The singing.

The silver door.

She laid her palm on it, and it parted like water, and the singing stopped, and the Gilt came down out of the passage behind her in their long whispering line, into the round black room under the clock.

I'd seen them do it already, in an engraving two hundred and twenty years old. I'd seen it on Vadra's desk under a lamp with the lemon tree in the corner. *The Founders' Well.* The robed figures in a ring, kneeling, each with a silver thread running from their own wrist to hers, every face turned up, mouths open.

They did it exactly the same.

They didn't have to be told. They'd never been down here, most of them; they'd only ever drunk from the Lines in their own houses, through the walls, from a well they'd never seen. But they knew. It was in them, somehow, the way the Wick knows the old song without anyone teaching it. They came into the round room and saw her, and they stopped, every one of them, at the edge of the pool of light, the way I'd stopped the first time.

My mother.

Kneeling under the pendulum. Arms held out and up in silver. Head bowed, grey-dark hair falling forward over her face. The light pouring off her onto the black stone.

And rising from the ring around her throat, braided, thick as my wrist, silver, alive, humming, up into the dark of the shaft above the pendulum: the Stay.

I'd seen it before, among all the other threads. I'd never *seen* it. Now I couldn't look at anything else. It was the only thing in that room that didn't shine. It was silver like the threads but dull, like old metal, and it moved. Very slightly. With every beat of her heart. A long slow shiver going up it from her throat into the dark, the way a rope shivers when something heavy hangs on the other end.

The Long-Lived stared at her.

Then, slowly, one by one, they went down on their knees.

Around the edge of the light. In a ring. In their furs, on the black stone. And they held out their left arms, all of them, with the cuffs pushed back, toward her. And a binder went round the ring with his velvet cushion, from one to the next, and at each one, he touched a silver needle to the thread that ran out of the wall behind them, the Lines, the threads from the houses, and drew it down, and fastened it to their wrists.

Corisande Marchbank put her other hand over her mouth when the thread touched her. Her count stopped trembling.

Lord Alder Ostrander, lifted out of his carrying chair by Fitch and laid on the stone, lay there on his back with his arm held up and the thread on his wrist, and wept.

Ismay Lisle knelt in white fur with her thread on, and her fox's face perfectly calm, and her eyes not on my mother at all. On the Keeper. On the Keeper's hands.

Behind her, the two men in Lisle white didn't kneel.

---

Kit saw her.

He'd been behind me in the passage, with Durrow's hand on his shoulder. Now he was beside me, at the edge of the pool of light, and he saw.

He didn't say anything at first. He stood there with his mouth open and his eyes enormous, looking at the woman kneeling under the pendulum in her light, with her arms held out, with her head bowed and her hair falling down.

Then, so quietly I almost didn't hear it:

"Mama."

The woman under the pendulum lifted her head.

Slowly. The way she'd lifted it the first time I came into this room. Her hair fell back from her face. Her eyes opened, dark and too big, and went past me, past the Keeper, past the whole kneeling ring of the Long-Lived.

To him.

Ten years. Three weeks old, in a cot by a window that sticks, with eleven years on his arm and lungs like wet paper. *Tomorrow night. I'll come back tomorrow night.*

She looked at him. He looked at her. Neither of them moved.

"Kit," said my mother.

Just that. One word. Out loud. Cracked and dry and so full of everything that it broke in the middle.

He lunged.

He'd have gone to her. Across the black stone, into the light, under the pendulum. He was already moving. And Durrow's big red hand came down on his shoulder and closed, and pulled him back, off his feet, against Durrow's chest, and held him there, with the other hand going to the broker's needle at his hip.

"Now, now," said Durrow, grinning. "Not yet, lad."

Kit fought him. Silently, the way a cat fights, all elbows and heels. Durrow didn't even seem to notice.

My mother's head had come up all the way. She was looking at Durrow now. Her face, in her own light, had changed completely. I had never seen it look like that. Not in ten years of memory, not at the end of my bed, not anywhere.

I knew that look. I'd seen it on my own face in Gannet's mirror, the night I came home from Pruett's.

---

"Bind her," said Lady Aurelia.

The binders came for me.

They were quick and quiet, the way binders always were. They took the white cloak off my shoulders and folded it and laid it on the stone. They led me into the light, beside my mother, so close our shoulders touched, and pressed me down, gently, onto my knees. And they threaded me. Silver, through the skin of my left wrist, and my right. Around my ankles. Up into the dark, into the works, the way she'd been threaded for ten years.

It hurt. Like being sewn shut. I didn't make a sound.

The younger binder lifted his needle toward my throat.

"Not the throat," said Lady Aurelia. "Not yet." She smiled at me. "The throat's for the Stay, dear. That comes after."

They stepped back.

I knelt on the black stone in the white silk nightgown, with my arms held out and up at my sides by the silver threads, beside my mother, in her light. Like the woman in the engraving. Like someone about to catch a child.

Across the pool of light, Durrow held my brother against his chest with one hand. In the other, now, was the needle.

---

Lady Aurelia knelt in front of us.

In white, in the pool of light, on the black stone. She was so close I could smell the violets on her breath. She took something from a sheath at her waist, under the white silk, and held it up so the light ran along it.

A knife. Slender, silver, very old. The blade no longer than my hand, and the hilt shaped like the hand of a clock.

"*At each Turning of the Year,*" she said, softly, in a voice I'd never heard her use, a voice like someone saying a prayer they'd said a hundred and forty times, "*the Keeper shall go down unto the Well, and open her palm with the Keeper's Knife.*"

She drew the blade across her own left palm.

The blood came up, dark, in a line.

"*And lay her hand upon the Well's heart,*" she said.

She laid her bleeding palm flat on my mother's breast, over her heart, under the throat-ring where the Stay rose. My mother didn't flinch. She was still looking at Kit.

The Stay shivered, all the way up into the dark.

"*And in the slack of that heart,*" said the Keeper of the Ledger, "*shall tie again the Knot of the Keeper.*"

She turned her head and looked at me.

"Willing, dear?"

---

I looked past her.

At Kit, against Durrow's chest, with his face white and his eyes on our mother. At the broker's needle in Durrow's hand. At its point, resting against the inside of Kit's thin wrist, just below his count, just where a binder had put thirty years in on a Tithe Day with a silver needle, the safe way.

At the ring of the Long-Lived on their knees with their threads on, watching. At Ismay Lisle, watching the knife. At Hollis, at the edge of the ring, white as paper, with his hand on Ambrose Vey's arm. At Evadne, standing alone by the silver door in her plain grey dress with her hands clasped in front of her so tightly the knuckles were white.

At the dark of the shaft above the pendulum, where nothing moved.

He wasn't there.

I felt for him. Down the silver thread. He was close, so close, above me, somewhere in that long straight dark, coming down. But not here. Not yet. And the Keeper's bleeding palm was on my mother's heart, and her eyes were on mine, and Durrow's needle was against my brother's wrist.

*If it goes wrong,* I'd said, in the dark, in the Violet Rooms. *I'll give it to her. I already decided.*

"Yes," I said.

The Keeper smiled. Both dimples.

She turned back to my mother. She bowed her head, and closed her eyes, and I felt her, through the cold Ledger thread on my wrist, go still. Listening. Waiting for the slack. My mother's heart under her hand, slow and deep. *Beat.* And the long silence after it, going out, and out. And the Keeper's other hand rising, with the silver knife in it, toward the Stay, toward my wrist, to bleed me into the knot.

And in my other wrist, the silver thread went hot.

He was right above me. Right there. In the dark over the pendulum. I could feel his heart, hard and slow, close enough to touch.

I reached down into the thread with everything I had.

*Now.*
