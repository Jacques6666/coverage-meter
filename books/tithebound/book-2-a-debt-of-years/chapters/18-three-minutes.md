# Chapter Eighteen

## Three Minutes

::: {.pov}
Severin
:::

He spent the night on Gannet's roof.

He hadn't meant to. He'd come back through the tunnel from Thane House with Bry and Pell at the second bell, and got Pell down the cellar ladder and onto the cot by the stove, and stood there while Gannet looked at the boy's face and swore in a language he didn't know. And then the thread at his wrist had gone cold.

Not her. Her heart was still there, fast, frightened, alive. But it was moving. Fast. Away from Thane House. Down. Down the Gilt Road, through the Wick, across the river. He'd stood in Gannet's back room with his hand clamped over his wrist and felt her cross the Hours Bridge, twenty-four lamps, in the dark, at one in the morning.

Then down.

He knew where. He'd felt it once before, on the Dial day, with his grandmother leading her away down a red-carpeted stair. He felt the stair. He felt the silver door. He felt the warmth.

Then he felt something go into her wrists. Both of them. A needle, and thread, and a binding, going up. Up into the dark.

He'd climbed out of Gannet's attic window onto the roof because it was the only place in the Wick where you could see the clock, and he'd sat there in the cold on the wet slates with his back against the chimney stack and his splinted hand against his chest, and watched it, all night. The great pale face over the island, lit from beneath. Two minutes past eleven.

And all night, through the thread, he felt the two of them.

He didn't hear anything. She didn't speak to him, not once, not a word. But he felt it. Something going back and forth down there in the dark, between her heart and another heart, slower, deeper. Warmth. Like breathing. Like two people breathing the same air. Hour after hour.

At dawn, the slates shook under him.

*Tick.*

He watched the iron hand move. Slowly. One notch, out of the place where it had sat for a week, a long grinding inch across the pale face of the clock, the way the minute hand on Liesl's watch had moved for the first time in five years, by itself, in his palm.

Three minutes past eleven.

He sat on the roof in the grey light and laughed, and then put his face in his good hand.

---

*She brought me back up,* said Tamsin. At the eighth bell. Down the silver thread, in a rush of words, every one free. *At dawn. In the carriage. I'm in the Violet Rooms. She's locked the door. Durrow's men on it now, not Corran. I don't know where Corran is.* A pause. *I spent the night with Mama.*

He was standing in Gannet's back room with a cup of tar in his hand. He put it down.

*We talked all night. About Kit. About my father. He lit the lamps on the Hours Bridge, Severin. All twenty-four. I never knew.* A longer pause. *And then we sort of breathed. Through the thread. Back and forth. We didn't mean to. And the clock ticked.* Another pause. *She came down the stair and the binder told her and she said "interesting." That's all she said. Interesting.*

He closed his eyes.

*She drank from me at breakfast like nothing happened. And then she said I'm not to leave the Violet Rooms until the Turning. Not even to the long gallery. And Evadne's locked in her rooms too.* A pause. *Your mother's all right. Nessa says she's playing the three notes. Over and over. All morning. Louder than usual.*

He reached down into the thread and paid three hours without counting them.

*Are you hurt?*

*No,* she said. *My wrists. From the binding. That's all.* A pause. *Severin. It moved because of us. Because of me and her, together. Not you, not five years, not a flood. Just us. Breathing.* Another pause, longer. *I think it wants to move. I think it's been wanting to for three hundred years.*

---

The Wick was in the street again.

Not standing, this time. Not staring. Moving. He went up the Row at the ninth bell with his collar up, because Gannet needed flour and Hob was asleep and he couldn't stand the back room for one more minute, and the Row was a river of people going one way and another, all of them talking at once.

And the brokers' shops were full.

He'd never seen anything like it. Every broker on the Row had a queue out of the door and down the cobbles. Not Wick people. Gilt servants. Footmen in livery, maids in good wool cloaks, a steward with a purse the size of a cat, standing in the cold with their masters' silver, waiting to buy. At Pruett's, the queue went past three shops. Pruett himself was standing in his doorway with his spectacles pushed up on his forehead and his purple fingers counting coins, and his face was the face of a man who'd died and gone to heaven.

*Ten,* said a sign in his bow window, in big new letters. *TEN CROWNS THE YEAR.*

Ten. Pruett paid three to buy. Last month he'd have sold for nine. *Ten crowns the year* meant the Gilt was buying at thirty.

"They're frightened," said a voice at his elbow.

He turned. Lotte Varga, with a basket of washing on her hip, standing at the edge of the crowd looking at Pruett's queue with no expression at all.

"Their counts are going," said Lotte. "Since last night. Since the tick. Not just at night now, in the small hours, flickering. In the *daytime*." She shifted the basket. "My sister does for a family on the fourth terrace. Ostrander cousins. She says the old lady came down to breakfast this morning and her arm was going like a candle in a gale, and she screamed, and the footman dropped the coffee." Lotte looked at the queue. "They're buying all they can before it all goes home. Like people filling buckets before a flood."

At the corner of the bridge, the man with no buttons was on his crate again, shouting at the clock. A crowd round him now. Not laughing.

"And the Wick?" said Severin.

Lotte jerked her chin.

A little way down the Row, outside a smaller broker's shop, a knot of people in Wick coats had gathered round the door. Not a queue. A crowd. Arguing. A woman's voice, high and furious: "*Then I'm not selling! You hear me? Not one year! If it's coming home, I'm keeping mine!*" And other voices, agreeing. And the broker in his doorway, shouting back. And two men in grey coats with silver buttons pushing through the crowd toward the woman, with truncheons.

Lotte watched it with her basket on her hip.

"Half of them want to sell at ten crowns while they can," she said. "And half of them have heard the madman on the crate and think it's all coming back to them anyway, so why sell?" She shifted the basket again. "And the Greycoats don't know which side they're supposed to be beating."

As they watched, one of the grey coats raised his truncheon over the woman's head.

And another grey coat caught his wrist.

A tall one. Older. In a coat worn shiny at the elbows. He didn't shout. He just held the younger man's wrist up in the air, and said something, too quiet to hear across the Row, and the younger man lowered the truncheon and stepped back, red in the face, and the crowd went quiet.

Captain Hesketh Marr let go of the man's wrist. He looked at the woman in the Wick coat for a moment. Then he turned and walked away up the Row, alone, with his hands behind his back, through the crowd, which parted for him.

As he passed Severin, his washed-out eyes flicked sideways, once, and found his face under the turned-up collar.

He didn't stop. He didn't say anything. He walked on up the Row and was gone.

"Huh," said Lotte Varga.

---

Pell was awake when he came back down the ladder.

He was propped up on the cot by the stove with three of Gannet's shawls round him and a bowl of broth in his huge hands, and his face was still swollen on one side, purple and green, the eye closed, but he was smiling. Bry was sitting on the floor beside the cot with her back against it and her knees pulled up. She wasn't doing anything. She was just sitting there, close, the way a dog sits close to someone it's decided is its own.

"Thane," said Pell, in his slow gentle voice, when he saw him. "Gannet says you got me out."

"Bry got you out. I held a lamp."

"Bry says you came through the walls." Pell looked down at his broth. "And Tam was there. And your mam." He was quiet a moment. "I didn't say anything. At the breakfasts. She kept asking, and Tam kept saying she didn't know, and I didn't say anything."

"I know."

"I just sang," said Pell. "Anni's song. It's the only one I know all the words to." He looked at his arm, lying on top of Gannet's shawls. Forty-one. "Is it true what they're saying on the Row? Bry told me. That it's coming home. All of it. At midnight."

"I don't know," said Severin. "The Charter says so."

"Then mine'll come back," said Pell slowly. "The eight she took. Won't they?"

Severin looked at the boy on the cot. At Bry beside him, with her head down on her knees. At the bowl of broth in Pell's big careful hands.

"Yes," he said. "If it strikes. They'll come back."

Pell thought about that for a long time. Then he smiled, the good side of his face, and went back to his broth.

On the floor by the cot, Bry didn't lift her head. But her hand, hanging down by her side, found the edge of Pell's blanket, and held on to it.

---

They planned it that night at the long table, all of them, for the first time with the shape of the thing in front of them.

The Turning of the Year. Five days. His grandmother would go down her stair at dawn with the Council of Houses and the Inner Ledger behind her to witness it, and Tamsin with her, in white, and she would bind Tamsin under the clock beside her mother and open her palm and tie the Knot of the Keeper in the slack of a well's heart. With Tamsin's blood in it. Given.

*She'll make her willing. One way or another.*

They knew where it would happen. They knew when. They knew who'd be there. What they didn't know was how to get in.

"The silver door opens for the Keeper's hand," said Vadra. "Nobody else's. There's no lock. There's nothing to pick." She looked at Magpie, who was sitting on the floor by the ladder as usual, with her arms round her knees. "Not even for you, girl."

"Wasn't going to offer," said Magpie.

"Then we're outside it," said Bry. "In the passage. With all their guards. While she does it."

Nobody said anything. The stove ticked.

"There's another way in."

Kit. He'd been at the end of the table all evening with his pencil and his maps, not drawing, listening. Now he pulled one of the maps out from under the others and pushed it across the table, into the lamplight, in front of Severin.

It was the Meridian. Not from above, this time. From the side. As if somebody had cut the island in half with a knife and drawn what was inside. The court. The Tithe Hall under the river, and the cisterns under that. The tower, going up and up. And through the middle of the tower, from the top to the bottom, from the clockworks all the way down through the rock to the round room at the very bottom, a long straight double line.

*SHAFT,* Kit had written beside it. *PENDULUM.* And at the top, where the double line came down past a small square room just under the clockworks: *HIS ROOM.*

"You said," said Kit. He looked up at Severin. "In the tower. You told Tam, and she told me. The pendulum comes down through your room. Through the grate. You can see it from your table." He put his finger on the long straight lines. "And it goes all the way down to her. To Mama." He looked up again. "So there's a way in from the top."

The back room was silent.

Severin looked at the map. At the long double line going down through the rock in pencil that pressed too hard. At *HIS ROOM*.

"Six hundred feet," he said slowly. "Maybe more. In the dark. Straight down."

"I've got rope," said Orrin Fisk, from his crate by the stove.

They all looked at him.

He was sitting with his coil of tarred rope across his knees, as he always sat, running a length of it through his brick-red hands. He didn't look up.

"Six hundred feet of rope," said Orrin, "is nothing. I've got eight hundred in a cellar in Calder Bay. I can have it on a barge by tomorrow night." He tied a knot in the length in his hands, without looking, and pulled it tight. "Done the herring masts in a gale at sixteen. Shaft's got no wind in it." He looked up at last, and his sunburnt face split in a grin. "Only question is who goes down it."

---

Lotte had his left hand on the black thread that night, in the cold steam-house, with Abel the porter on one nail and herself on the other.

"Again," she said.

He cut. Left-handed. It was like writing with his foot; everything backward, everything slow. But he felt for the slack and found it, and the little knife went through, and the thread parted like wet paper.

"Again."

He cut again.

"That's the twentieth," said Lotte. "Left-handed. Clean." She didn't sound pleased. She never sounded pleased. "You'll do."

"For black."

"For black." She took the knife back and turned it over in her raw hands. "Ledger's another thing. You'll have one blink. Less. With the Keeper's own heart at the other end, and a well's on yours." She looked at him. "And if you get it wrong, you kill your girl or you kill the old woman, and nobody knows which, and either way you'll have done it with your own hand."

"I know."

"You don't. You won't till you've done it." She put the knife in her apron, next to the watch.

He waited. He always waited, at the end. For his price.

She sat on her basket for a long time without speaking.

"She taught you cards," said Lotte at last. "My girl. You know that. She told you she was going to."

"Yes."

"She came home on her half-day," said Lotte, "the week after she started teaching you. And she sat at my table, and she said, *I let him win once, Mam.*" Lotte's voice had gone very flat and careful, like someone carrying a full basin across a floor. "*Just once. On purpose. A whole hand. And he was so happy he didn't sulk for a week. A whole week, Mam. Not once.*"

He couldn't breathe.

He remembered. He'd been twelve. On the back stairs at Thane House with a pack of greasy cards she'd stolen from a footman, and she'd lost a whole hand to him for the first time, and he'd been so astonished and so delighted that he'd gone round for days afterward feeling as if he'd been given something. He'd never known. He'd never once in nine years thought she might have done it on purpose.

"Thank you," he said. It came out of him in pieces.

"Don't," said Lotte.

---

She stood up. She didn't go to the door. She stood in front of him, on the wet brick, with the lamp behind her and her big raw arms folded, and looked down at him sitting on the floor.

"When the time comes, Thane," said Lotte Varga, "it'll be my hand on the Stay. Not yours."

He went cold.

He'd known since Wat read it. Since *paid in full by the hand that makes it*, in that darker ink, and her face across the lamp in Gannet's back room not changing at all. He'd known, and he'd put it away, the way Tamsin said you put away the thoughts you can't afford.

"No," he said.

"Eleven years." She held up her left arm. "That's what I've got. I'm fifty-three. My girl's dead. My husband's dead. My brother's dead." She lowered her arm. "You've got eight, and a girl in a black house who'd tear the clock down with her teeth for you, and a mother playing three notes on a harpsichord who's been waiting fourteen years for you to come home." Her jaw set. "*Paid in full.* Whatever that cut costs, I've got less to lose paying it."

"*No.*"

"It's my trade," said Lotte. "Cutting. Not yours. You've been at it a fortnight. I've been at it twenty years. You'll have one blink, with the Stay. One. If it's anybody's hand, it ought to be the steadiest hand in Lanthorn, and that's not one with a splint on it."

"Mrs. Varga—"

"I wasn't asking, Thane," said Lotte Varga.

She took the watch out of her apron pocket, and held it up to her ear for a moment, the way she always did. Then she put it back, and buttoned the pocket, and went to the door.
