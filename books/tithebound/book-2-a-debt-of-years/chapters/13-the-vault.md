# Chapter Thirteen

## The Vault

::: {.pov}
Severin
:::

He stepped through the bronze doors.

The vault was round. He'd guessed that much; everything the Founders built underground was round, the way the Dial was round and the chamber under the clock was round, as if they'd only ever known how to build things shaped like clock faces. The walls were dark stone, dressed smooth, and set into them at even intervals were seven deep niches, arched, each one with a name carved over it. *Thane. Crane. Lisle. Vey. Ostrander. Marchbank. Penhallow.* In each niche, on a stone shelf, something lay under glass. He caught a glimpse of one as his lamp swept past: a silver comb. Another: a short sword with a hilt worn smooth. Another: a pair of old shears, the blades black.

In the middle of the floor stood a plinth, waist high, and on the plinth a case of old green glass with its lid propped open.

And bent over the case, with a lamp set on the plinth beside it, turning pages, were two people.

One was a tall man in a long grey cloak, with the hood pushed back, fair-haired, perhaps forty or a hundred and forty, it was impossible to tell. He wore white gloves. He had a sheaf of old vellum lifted half out of the glass case in both hands, and he was turning the top page with great care, the way you'd lift a wet leaf.

The other was a girl.

Small, wiry, sixteen at the most, in a Wick coat with the sleeves cut off at the elbow, with her hair cropped short and a thief's quick, flat, empty face. She was standing at the edge of the lamplight with her arms folded, watching the door. Watching him, now. She'd seen his lamp before the man had.

"Corwin," she said.

The tall man looked up.

---

Severin knew him. Everyone in the Gilt knew everyone in the Gilt; it was the smallest city in the world. Corwin Lisle. Ismay Lisle's great-grandson, or great-great; her heir, at any rate, or one of them, with a cuff on his arm worth more than the Tithe Hall and a reputation for buying Wick paintings and Wick years with equal enthusiasm. They'd been at the same christenings. They'd danced the Turning in the same ring at a dozen Long Nights, with partners whose names neither of them remembered.

Corwin Lisle looked at him across the glass case, with the Founders' Charter in his white-gloved hands, and smiled.

"Thane," he said. "The disowned heir. What a pleasure."

"Put it down."

"I don't think I will." Corwin turned another page, delicately, without looking at it. "My great-grandmother has been trying to see this for a hundred years, you know. Your great-great-great-grandmother has never once let her. Council day after Council day, standing at the top of those stairs, watching the Thane Keeper go down alone." He smiled. "Great-grandmother thinks it's wasted on yours. She thinks it would be very interesting to know exactly what's in it, before the Renewal. Exactly how the Stay is tied. Exactly what it needs." His pale eyes moved over Severin's face. "Knowledge is leverage, Thane. Your family taught us that."

"How did you get in?"

"Same way as you, I expect. The old tunnels. Lisle has its own door." He tilted his head toward the niche with *Lisle* carved over it, where, Severin saw now, the stone shelf was empty and the glass lay shattered on the floor. "We took the comb as well. Great-grandmother's always wanted it back."

The girl at the edge of the lamplight hadn't moved. She was still watching Severin. Not the way Corwin watched him. Her flat thief's face had gone very still, and she was looking at his left arm. At the sleeve of his Wick coat, pushed up a little where he'd worked the grate, and the bare skin of his wrist, and the silver thread.

"I'll take the Charter," said Severin. "You can keep the comb."

Corwin laughed.

And moved.

---

It was a hastening. A good one. Lisle trained its heirs as hard as Thane did; harder, some said. One moment Corwin was standing at the plinth with the vellum in his hands, and the next he was a white-gold blur, and the air tore, and something hit Severin in the chest like a door slammed into him and he went backward into the stone wall beside the *Ostrander* niche hard enough to knock the lamp out of his hand.

The lamp hit the floor and went out.

The dark came down. Only Corwin's lamp, on the plinth, throwing everything into long swinging shadows. And somewhere in the shadows, very fast, Corwin, moving again.

He didn't hasten back.

He'd thought about this. All the way up the tunnel under the Gilt, in the dark, with Kit's map and its question marks. He'd thought about what he would do if something went wrong, and he'd thought about what it would cost, and he'd made himself a rule. Not because he was proud. Because Tamsin had sat on the floor of the Violet Rooms with her wrist against his and said *ours*, and he'd felt every hour of that night's slow tide come into him from her, and he would be damned before he threw it away on a Lisle in a cellar.

So he didn't hasten. He listened.

You can hear a hastening, if you know how. The working tears the air. A sound like silk ripping, very high, very quick, and it comes from wherever the caster is going to be a heartbeat from now. He'd learned that on the Dial, a long time ago, against men who'd been trying to kill him for sport.

He heard it. Left. Low.

He stilled the lamp.

Not Corwin. The lamp on the plinth. A stilling a child could cast; a cup of water costs more. The flame stopped mid-flicker, frozen, a little bent tongue of fire hanging motionless on its wick, and the shadows stopped swinging. And Corwin Lisle, coming out of his hastening a heartbeat later exactly where Severin had heard him, low and left with his fist drawn back, found himself in light that didn't move, against a wall he hadn't expected, with nowhere to go.

Severin hit him.

Not a working. A fist. The way Bry had taught Tamsin and Tamsin had once, in a fit of temper, tried to teach him, on the floor of the tower, with the clocks ticking. *Thumb outside, Severin. You'll break it. Saints' teeth, have you never hit anybody?*

He hadn't. Not like this. He hit Corwin Lisle in the jaw with everything he had and felt something in his own hand give, and Corwin went sideways into the plinth and the green glass case went over and smashed on the floor.

The Charter went with it.

The vellum pages scattered across the dark stone like leaves. A dozen of them, two dozen, sliding, spinning, coming to rest in the frozen lamplight with their old brown ink and their seven red seals.

Corwin was on his knees. He was reaching for them.

---

The girl got there first.

She came out of the shadow at the edge of the light so fast that Severin thought, for an instant, she was hastening too. She wasn't. She was just a Wick thief, and Wick thieves are fast. She dropped to the floor among the scattered pages and swept them up in both arms, all of them, vellum crackling, seals clacking together, and came up holding them against her chest.

Corwin, on his knees, held out his white-gloved hand. "Good girl. Give them here."

She didn't.

She stood in the frozen light with the Founders' Charter crushed against her Wick coat, and looked at Severin. At his face. At his wrist.

"You," she said.

He didn't understand.

"The steam-house," said the girl. Her flat face wasn't empty anymore. "Last week. At the bottom of the Sump. The old woman's shed." She swallowed. "You cut my grandad's thread. Forty years he'd been paying a broker in Saltmarket. Paid it off three times. Still paying." Her voice cracked. "You cut it. He cried. He's never cried. Not when my mam went grey, not ever."

Severin remembered. An old man with a broker's twist on his wrist, from a debt older than his granddaughter, looking down at his bare arm in the lamplight of the steam-house and then up at him, and saying nothing, and his face doing something terrible and slow.

"Magpie," said Corwin Lisle. His voice had changed. "Give me those pages. Now. You've been paid."

The girl looked down at him on his knees on the floor.

"Paid," she said. "Yeah."

She turned her back on him, and walked across the vault, and put the Founders' Charter into Severin's arms.

---

"*Magpie,*" said Corwin, behind them, and the air began to tear.

Severin didn't wait to find out what he'd cast. He grabbed the girl by the arm and they ran, out through the bronze doors and up the seven worn steps under the gold letters, with the vellum crushed against his chest and the girl half a step behind, and behind them both a white-gold blur coming out of the vault.

And at the top of the steps, in the archive, lights.

Lanterns. A lot of them. Swinging. Coming through the stacks from the far end, from the main stair, fast. Men's voices, calling. Grey coats, between the iron shelves.

The thread at his wrist warmed. Tamsin's voice, fast and sharp.

*Severin. Bry says Durrow's in the building. He came with the Keeper's guard for Council day. Someone shouted in the archive. They're coming down. Get out.*

He reached down into the thread. He didn't have time for more than one word, and he didn't care what it cost.

*Pell.*

A pause. He knew she was sending it on, down the black thread to Bry. He kept running.

The light-well was in the middle of the archive. He'd seen it on Kit's map, and on Orrin's finger tapping Kit's map: a square shaft, open, running straight up through every floor of the Ledger House from the bottom of the archive to a skylight in the roof, so the clerks had daylight to copy by. He could see it ahead, a pale column of grey daylight falling down between the stacks onto the stone floor.

And hanging down it, swinging gently, from four floors up, a rope. Tarred. As thick as a man's arm.

*Pell's dropping the tray,* said Tamsin. *Now.*

Somewhere far above, through three floors of stone, faint, there was a crash. A long rolling crash of silver and crystal and china, as though somebody very large had dropped every tray in the Ledger House at once. And then voices. Shouting. A woman shrieking. Feet, running, upstairs. And the lanterns coming through the stacks toward them stopped, and wavered, and half of them turned and went back the way they'd come.

Half.

He reached the rope. He looked up the light-well. Four floors up, against the grey square of sky, a brick-red face was looking down at him over the edge of the skylight, salt-white eyebrows raised.

"Tie it on!" Orrin's voice came down the shaft, echoing. "The bundle! Tie it on and I'll haul!"

He tore off his Wick coat. He wrapped the Charter in it, the whole sheaf of vellum, seals and all, rolled tight, and knotted the sleeves round it, and tied the bundle to the end of the rope with a knot Orrin had made him practice forty times at Gannet's long table, a fisherman's knot, the kind that holds harder the more you pull on it. He yanked it once.

The rope went taut. The bundle went up the light-well, fast, swinging, out of his hands, up into the grey daylight, and was gone.

He turned.

The girl, Magpie, was standing beside him with her back to the stacks, breathing hard. The lanterns that hadn't turned back were coming. Twenty paces. Fifteen. Grey coats. And out of the dark behind them, white-gold, tearing the air, Corwin Lisle.

"Can you climb?" said Severin.

The girl looked at the rope. It was already coming back down for him, swinging, Orrin paying it out from the roof. She looked at it, and at him, and at the lanterns coming.

"I'm a *thief*," she said, with deep contempt, and went up it like a squirrel.

He followed.

---

He'd never climbed a rope in his life.

He found out halfway up the second floor that you don't climb a rope with your arms; you climb it with your legs, and your arms only hold on. He found that out by doing it wrong for a whole floor, until his shoulders were screaming and his hands were raw from the tar and the knuckles of his right hand, the one he'd hit Corwin with, had gone from aching to something white-hot and wrong. Below him in the light-well, lanterns. Men's faces turned up. Someone shouting his name, his name, *Thane, it's the Thane boy, get him*. And something tearing the air.

He felt it go past. A working. A stilling, cast up the shaft at him, fast and sloppy, Corwin's or a Greycoat's, and it missed him by a hand's width and hit the brick wall of the light-well beside his head, and the brick went grey and old and crumbled away in a cloud of red dust, a hundred years of weather in a breath.

He kept climbing.

At the top, hands. Orrin's, brick-red and salt-rough, closing on his wrists, and the girl's, small and hard, on his collar, and they hauled him up over the lip of the skylight onto the leads of the Ledger House roof, into wind and grey daylight and the whole of the Gilt spread out below them, white terraces falling away to the Wick and the river and the island and the clock.

Two minutes past eleven.

He lay on his back on the cold lead of the roof with his chest heaving and his hands bleeding and the sky going round over his head.

"Got it," said Orrin, somewhere above him. He had the bundle, Severin's coat with the Charter rolled up inside it, tucked under one arm. "Got it, Thane. All of it. Here." He was hauling up the rope, hand over hand, coiling it as fast as it came, so nobody below could use it. "Come on. There's a ladder down the back to the stables. We go now, we're in the Founders' tunnel under the Ostrander gardens before they've found the stairs."

He sat up.

The thread at his wrist was warm. It had been warm the whole climb, he realized. Warm and silent. He'd been too busy to notice.

*Tamsin,* he sent. One word. An hour. *Bry?*

The answer didn't come at once. When it came, it came slowly, the way she spoke when she was choosing every word, and her voice was very small.

*Bry's out. With Hollis. Through the kitchens, into the stable yard. Hollis is pretending to be drunk, and Bry's pretending to carry him.* A pause. *She's not pretending to cry.*

He went cold all over on the windy roof.

*Pell,* he sent.

A long pause. The wind blew across the leads. Orrin had stopped coiling.

*Pell held the kitchen door,* said Tamsin. *When Durrow's men came up from the archive. So Bry and Hollis could get out. He stood in the doorway and held it shut with his back, and they broke it in on him.* A pause. *Bry says six of them. Six coats. On top of him. She says she could hear him the whole time, all the way across the stable yard.*

*Hear him?*

*Singing,* said Tamsin. *Bry says he was singing.*

Severin knew what the song was before she told him. He'd heard it once, on the stone floor of the Tithe Hall, on the night they lit two rows of tallow stubs for Joss and Anni. A big gentle voice filling a vaulted room. Every verse.

*The hymn about the lamb,* said Tamsin. *Anni's hymn.*

A pause.

*They've got Pell.*
